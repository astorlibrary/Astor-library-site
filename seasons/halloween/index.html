Your Halloween reading list

Start with Sleepy Hollow and Other American Halloween Stories: twelve complete stories whose later seasonal associations are explained alongside their original publication histories. Add Victorian and American anthologies, M. R. James’s collected ghosts, Poe’s tales and Hodgson’s Carnacki investigations.

The longer-reading shelves include Dracula, Frankenstein and works in which the evidence of a haunting remains contested. The free guides below cover Halloween’s literary history, ghost stories, Gothic fiction and individual works. Close readings offer shorter starting points for a class, reading group or evening at home.

The Astor reading note

How a story becomes a Halloween story

The twelve works in Sleepy Hollow and Other American Halloween Stories were not originally written for Halloween. Their later association with the festival grew through the repeated use of ghosts, witches, black cats, haunted houses and other supernatural figures in American October culture.

Keeping those two histories separate makes the reading more useful. First ask when and where a story appeared, who narrates it and what evidence it gives. Then consider the customs, adaptations and later publications through which it became familiar seasonal reading.
