# Astor Library editorial standard

## The institution's purpose

The name Astor Library nods to the nineteenth-century reference library founded through John Jacob Astor's bequest and later absorbed into the New York Public Library. The original library's books were there to be consulted and studied. Astor Editions carry that reference-library idea into the book: notes, summaries, analysis and context sit around the original work.

Astor Editions are for readers who want help with a classic without losing sight of the book itself. The original work remains central. Supporting material exists to improve understanding, context and analysis.

In prose editions, chapters are supported by clear summaries and factual context. Shakespeare editions preserve scene divisions, line numbers and classroom support. The website also publishes study guides, booklets, presentations, close readings and teaching resources.

## Voice

Write like an informed human editor: direct, specific, warm and quietly authoritative. Prefer a precise observation about the book to a claim about how “timeless”, “transformative” or “immersive” it is.

Avoid:

- AI buzzwords and inflated promises;
- generic phrases such as “delve into”, “rich tapestry”, “journey through” or “unlock”;
- repetitive three-part lists used as a substitute for prose;
- describing the build process, filenames, uploads, cards or templates to readers;
- calling supporting material “comprehensive” unless the page demonstrates it;
- reducing a text to one theme or one exam answer.

Use lists only where the reader genuinely needs a list. Prefer connected paragraphs for interpretation and explanation.

## Individual book pages

Make each book page recognisably about that work. Include, as appropriate:

- a strong opening that names the dramatic or narrative situation;
- the correct Astor cover at the head of the page;
- a clear account of what the edition contains;
- the work's shape, movement or chapter/scene structure;
- close readings anchored in exact language;
- factual publication, historical, textual or performance context;
- characters and relationships treated through the text rather than labels;
- open questions for readers, students or teachers;
- authoritative further-reading sources;
- routes to its writer, subjects, passages, teaching material and related resources.

Do not pad pages to hit a word count. A major individual page should nevertheless be substantial enough to reward a search visit; roughly 1,000–2,500 carefully written words is often appropriate, with longer teaching rooms where needed.

## Writer and subject pages

Writer pages should not be short biographies followed by a shelf. Show how to read the writer: voice, form, recurring decisions, historical position and meaningful differences between works. Use institutional sources.

Subject pages should define a useful reading method, not flatten books into a category. Distinguish nearby forms and show how individual works complicate the subject.

## Free resources and teaching rooms

Every free resource needs its own Astor page even when the final resource is a PDF or external website. Explain its scope, likely reader, practical use and relationship to the relevant text.

Teaching rooms should be usable without additional explanation. Include timed options, exact passages, student-ready prompts, teacher guidance, an evidence route and links back to the complete text. Keep tasks rooted in language rather than generic theme spotting.

## Homepage and visual design

The homepage should feel like an independent literary institution with a distinct editorial idea. Do not lead with the Passage Room merely because it is a novel feature. Establish Astor's identity, then give clear routes into books, teaching and free resources.

Design for a phone first as well as desktop:

- preserve book-cover aspect ratios;
- avoid stretched grids and sideways overflow;
- keep the torch and wordmark fully visible;
- make tap targets and text comfortably readable;
- use motion sparingly and respect reduced-motion preferences;
- do not let moving media delay the page's essential content.

## Accuracy and sourcing

Check dates, textual history, publication facts, public-domain status and performance claims against primary or institutional sources. Prefer libraries, archives, museums, universities, scholarly editions and theatre institutions. Cite sources on the page where useful. Never invent a source or infer a historical claim from marketing copy.
