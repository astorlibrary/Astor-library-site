Notes from Underground

“I am a sick man.... I am a spiteful man.” A retired official, writing notes he says nobody will read, argues that people will always choose freedom over their own good, and then tells how at twenty-four he humiliated a young woman called Liza. Dostoevsky’s short novel of 1864, in Constance Garnett’s translation.

Two parts

The narrator is forty, a collegiate assessor who retired when a distant relation left him six thousand roubles, and he lives alone in a corner on the edge of St Petersburg. The first part, Underground, is his argument with an audience he calls gentlemen and says does not exist. Too much consciousness, he says, is a disease. The man of action accepts the stone wall of the laws of nature; the conscious man can neither accept it nor break it. And people will always prefer freedom, even the freedom to harm themselves, to any happiness worked out for them in advance.

À Propos of the Wet Snow, the second part, goes back sixteen years to the life behind the argument. At twenty-four the narrator stalks an officer who once moved him aside without noticing him, forces himself on a farewell dinner for a schoolfellow he hates, and ends the night in a brothel with Liza, a young woman from Riga. He moves her to tears with a speech about her future and gives her his address. When she comes to him a few days later he humiliates her, and she leaves without taking his money.

Chernyshevsky and the crystal palace

Dostoevsky wrote the book early in 1864, while his first wife was dying in Moscow, and it is partly an answer to Nikolai Chernyshevsky’s novel What Is to Be Done? (1863). Chernyshevsky argued that enlightened self-interest would make people good, and in his heroine’s fourth dream the rational future lives in a palace of iron and glass. Dostoevsky had seen the real Crystal Palace, the hall built for the Great Exhibition of 1851, in London in 1862, and had written about it in Winter Notes on Summer Impressions.

Nobody could put out his tongue at such a palace, the narrator says, and he will not call a hen-house a mansion just because it keeps him dry. Dostoevsky meant the argument to lead somewhere. When the first part appeared he complained to his brother that the censors had passed his mockery and blasphemy and struck out the places where he drew from it the need for faith and Christ, in the chapter he thought the most important.

Publication

Notes from Underground appeared in 1864 in Epokha, the journal Dostoevsky ran with his brother Mikhail: the first part in a double issue that came out at the end of March, the second in the April issue. Dostoevsky added a footnote to the title saying that the writer of the notes was imaginary, but that such people must exist in Russian society given the circumstances in which it had been formed.

At the time the book drew almost no response. The satirist Saltykov-Shchedrin mocked the journal and its contributors in a pamphlet, and serious critical attention came only after Crime and Punishment in 1866. Garnett’s translation keeps the book’s frame: Dostoevsky’s footnote, the lines from Nekrasov at the head of the second part, and the editor’s note at the end, which says that the paradoxalist could not stop writing but that we may stop here.

The narrator and his readers

The voice is the book. The narrator contradicts himself on purpose, confesses to lying and then to lying about the confession, and argues with objections he later admits he has invented. Dostoevsky told his brother that the first part might look like chatter, and that the chatter would be resolved in the last two chapters by an unexpected catastrophe, like a transition in music.

Readers have argued ever since about how far to trust him. He calls himself an anti-hero, and he rounds on those who judge him: he has only carried to an extreme, he says, what they have not dared to carry half-way. The one person in the book who acts freely and generously is Liza, and many readers take her refusal of his money as the book’s real answer to its first part.

Later readers

In Russia the critic Nikolai Mikhailovsky gave the book a central place in his essay A Cruel Talent (1882), an attack on what he saw as Dostoevsky’s appetite for tormenting his characters. Abroad its reputation grew through a French adaptation, L’Esprit souterrain (1886), which joined the second part to Dostoevsky’s earlier story The Landlady. Friedrich Nietzsche found it by chance in a bookshop in Nice in 1887 and wrote to a friend that he had felt an immediate kinship with its author. Twentieth-century readers made the narrator a founding figure of existentialism, and Walter Kaufmann began his anthology Existentialism from Dostoevsky to Sartre (1956) with him.

Constance Garnett’s translation and this edition

Garnett’s translation was published in 1918 in the tenth volume of her Novels of Fyodor Dostoevsky, where it follows White Nights. Her English is clear and a little stately, which suits the narrator’s mock formality; she calls the crystal palace the Palace of Crystal, and keeps his gentlemen, his dashes and his broken-off sentences.
