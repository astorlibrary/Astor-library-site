The Importance of Being Earnest

Jack Worthing is Ernest in town and Jack in the country; his friend Algernon has invented an invalid called Bunbury so that he can get out of dinners. Both are in love with women who say they could only marry a man called Ernest. Jack, Algernon and their invented identities

Jack Worthing uses the name Ernest when visiting London and has explained his absences from the country by inventing a troublesome brother. Algernon Moncrieff has invented an invalid friend, Bunbury, whose illnesses supply convenient reasons to avoid social obligations. Their arrangements become difficult to maintain when Algernon visits Jack’s country house as the supposed brother.

Gwendolen Fairfax and Cecily Cardew each believe that the name Ernest has a particular importance for the man they intend to marry. Lady Bracknell investigates Jack’s suitability as a husband, while Miss Prism and Dr Chasuble become involved in plans for christenings and the discovery of Jack’s origins. The action moves from a London flat to a country garden and drawing room.

The 1899 text used here

The edition follows the first book text published in 1899, the three-act version corrected by Wilde for publication. This textual basis is significant because the play survives in several forms. An earlier four-act version and the text used in the original 1895 production are distinct stages in its history.

The notes explain how the versions differ. The printed play retains Wilde’s subtitle, A Trivial Comedy for Serious People. Explanatory footnotes identify language and references in the chosen text, while act summaries provide a convenient record of the developing misunderstandings.

First performance and publication

The Importance of Being Earnest opened at the St James’s Theatre in London on 14 February 1895. George Alexander managed the theatre and played Jack Worthing. The comedy’s production belongs to the final months of Wilde’s public success before the trials and imprisonment that interrupted his theatrical career.

The first book edition followed in February 1899, issued by Leonard Smithers. Its title page named the author only as ‘the author of Lady Windermere’s Fan’. The recorded issue comprised 1,000 numbered ordinary copies, 100 signed large-paper copies and twelve signed Japanese-vellum presentation copies. These figures describe the first edition, not the Astor paperback.

What the footnotes explain

Wilde’s jokes frequently depend on contemporary social practices: visiting and dining, country houses, family connections, education, inheritance and the investigation of a prospective marriage. The notes explain the places, institutions, customs and terms that a first audience could have recognised more readily than a reader today.

The distinction between a practical obstacle and a verbal joke is often important. Jack’s uncertain parentage affects Lady Bracknell’s judgement of him; the proposed christenings concern the names preferred by Gwendolen and Cecily. Notes supply context for these actions while keeping the complete dialogue available for readers to judge the characters’ stated reasons.

Letters, diaries and family evidence

Cecily’s diary gives an already elaborate history to a relationship that has scarcely begun. Gwendolen’s record and Cecily’s account then help produce the confrontation in the garden. Written evidence appears authoritative to its keeper even when its contents reflect wishes or inventions.

The final act turns on a different set of documents and recollections. Miss Prism’s earlier employment, the lost manuscript and the hand-bag found at Victoria Station connect Jack’s history with the family before him. The comedy’s explanations depend on specific objects and testimony. Character studies in the Astor material follow these roles through the three acts.

Wilde and the supporting essays

Oscar Wilde was born in Dublin in 1854 and studied at Trinity College Dublin and Magdalen College, Oxford. His published work includes poetry, fiction, criticism and plays. The London society comedies brought his work to a substantial theatrical audience in the 1890s.

The Astor introduction places Earnest within that career and documents its composition, revision, first production and later reception. Critical perspectives accompany the text without requiring the reader to accept a single account of its humour or social argument. The combination of the identified 1899 text, footnotes and act summaries makes the edition suitable for sustained reading as well as reference to individual scenes.
