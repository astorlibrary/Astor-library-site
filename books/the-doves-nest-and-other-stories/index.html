About the work

Six finished stories and four fragments from 1923.

The account below names the completed stories and the unfinished pieces, and follows the collection from Mansfield’s death to Murry’s assembly of the posthumous book.

The work

What happens.

The Doves’ Nest and Other Stories appeared shortly after Katherine Mansfield’s death in 1923, bringing completed stories together with work she left unfinished.

This edition contains all six completed stories from the original collection: The Doll’s House, Honeymoon, A Cup of Tea, Taking the Veil, The Fly and The Canary.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

It also includes four significant unfinished pieces: A Married Man’s Story, The Doves’ Nest, Six Years After and Weak Heart. The edition says which pieces are unfinished.

The selection moves between New Zealand childhood, European domestic life, marriage, class, bereavement and illness, with context on Mansfield’s final years and John Middleton Murry’s assembly of the posthumous book.

Text and context

The stories and the fragments.

The edition presents ten of the 1923 collection’s twenty-one items and says so; notes and chronology connect the stories to Wellington, domestic service and Mansfield’s final years.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

Murry’s edition

John Middleton Murry’s introductory note makes an important distinction: Mansfield had chosen the title The Doves’ Nest and Other Stories, but he could not say that she would have selected exactly the contents he published. He printed a plan dated 27 October 1921 that marked proposed stories as London or New Zealand pieces. The item provisionally called At Karori became The Doll’s House.

Murry also describes the surviving documents. For the title story he found a clean typescript but no further manuscript; Weak Heart survived as an opening, notes from the middle and a rapidly written ending. These details explain why a posthumous collection cannot automatically be treated as a volume the author completed and approved. The Astor selection preserves that distinction by identifying its four unfinished works.

Social position in the completed stories

The Doll’s House follows the Burnell children as they display a new toy to selected schoolfellows. Lil and Else Kelvey are excluded because of their family’s social position; their mother works as a washerwoman, and their father is the subject of hostile rumours. Kezia’s invitation briefly crosses a boundary enforced by adults as well as children. The small lamp inside the house becomes a particular object shared by Kezia’s attention and Else’s memory.

A Cup of Tea transfers questions of access and generosity to an affluent woman’s home. Rosemary Fell brings a poor young woman indoors after being asked for money, but her response changes when her husband comments on the visitor’s appearance. Honeymoon and Taking the Veil are different in scale: each concentrates on a young woman’s imaginative response to an immediate experience, allowing thought to move beyond what another person in the scene understands.

Grief, memory and interrupted narration

In The Fly, an office visit introduces the subject of two sons killed in the war. After the visitor leaves, the boss attempts to recover his grief, then turns his attention to a fly struggling in ink. The story’s final failure of recollection is part of its action; Mansfield does not provide an explanation that resolves the relation between the remembered son and the treatment of the insect.

The Canary takes the form of a woman’s account of a dead bird that had kept her company during domestic work. Six Years After, an unfinished piece, brings bereavement into a husband and wife’s journey by steamer. Read together, these works offer different narrative circumstances for remembering a loss: conversation in an office, direct address to a listener and thought during travel. Their differences remain important, including the distinction between a deliberately completed ending and the point at which a surviving fragment stops.
