Alexander Pope

The Rape of the Lock and An Essay on Criticism

A young lord snips a curl from a beauty’s head at Hampton Court, and Alexander Pope tells the story as an epic, with sylphs for guardian spirits, a card game for a battle and a new star at the end. This edition pairs The Rape of the Lock with An Essay on Criticism, the verse argument about judging poetry that made Pope’s name in 1711, and prints the two poems in their later forms.

The stolen lock

In 1711 Robert, Lord Petre, then about twenty, cut off a lock of hair from Arabella Fermor, a celebrated beauty and a distant relation of his. They belonged to the small society of Catholic families that Pope moved in, and the quarrel that followed divided the two households. John Caryll, a friend of Pope who knew the families well, suggested that Pope should write a poem to make a jest of it. The first version, in two cantos, is said to have been written in a fortnight, and it appeared without Pope’s name in Lintot’s Miscellany in May 1712.

According to Pope, the poem had its effect, and nothing more is heard of the feud. Not everyone was pleased. Sir George Brown, taken to be the original of the blustering Sir Plume, was angry for a long time at being made to talk nonsense, and Arabella Fermor, who by Johnson’s account liked the poem well enough to show it to her friends, was reported by Pope himself to be offended. Lord Petre married a Lancashire heiress in 1712 and died the following year.

The second version

Addison admired the first version and advised Pope to leave it alone, but Pope saw what it lacked. Critics held that an epic needed machinery, the gods, angels or demons who take part in its action, and Pope found his in Le Comte de Gabalis, a French satire on the Rosicrucians and their spirits of the four elements: sylphs of the air, gnomes of the earth, nymphs of the water and salamanders of fire. The five-canto poem appeared in 1714 with a letter to Arabella Fermor that explains the machinery and assures her that Belinda resembles her in nothing but beauty.

The additions were the sylph’s dream, the dressing table, the game of ombre and the Cave of Spleen, joined to the old lines so smoothly that few readers notice the seams. Pope later counted the work among the greatest proofs of judgement he ever gave. In 1717, in his collected Works, he added Clarissa’s speech on good sense and good humour, to make the moral plainer. The final poem has 794 lines; the first had 334.

The mock-epic

Heroi-comical was Pope’s word for the poem, and it follows the plan of an epic: a statement of the subject and an appeal to the Muse, a hero arming (Belinda at her dressing table), a sacrifice (the Baron’s altar of French romances), a council of spirits, a contest fought as a battle, a descent to the underworld, heroic speeches, a general battle, Jove’s golden scales and a transformation. The game of ombre is modelled on a Latin poem about chess by the Italian poet Vida, and the chief precedent for the whole was Boileau’s Le Lutrin, a mock-epic about a quarrel over a church lectern.

Homer was on Pope’s mind in these years, since he was translating the Iliad, and many of the jokes lean on it. Clarissa’s speech parodies Sarpedon’s, the Baron swears by the lock as Achilles swears by his sceptre, and Belinda’s bodkin has a family history like Agamemnon’s sceptre. The comedy comes from scale. Pope’s couplets set the great and the small side by side, as when a nymph may stain her honour or her new brocade, and leave the reader to judge which the characters mind more.

Queen Anne’s London

The Rape is crowded with the fashions of about 1712: tea, still a costly luxury; coffee roasted and ground in the drawing room; snuff, newly in vogue; china jars, Indian screens, face patches, sedan chairs, hooped petticoats, and ombre, a Spanish card game played by three people with nine cards each. The game and the cutting of the lock take place at Hampton Court, where Queen Anne sometimes held councils and, in Pope’s line, sometimes took tea.

Pope makes these things glitter, and he also shows what lies behind them: the trade that brings the gems of India and the perfumes of Arabia to a lady’s table, the politicians who see through all things with half-shut eyes over their coffee, the judges who hurry through sentences of death so that the jury can dine. Readers still argue about how sharp the satire is, and about whether its target is Belinda, the society around her or the grand style itself.

An Essay on Criticism

Pope later dated the Essay to 1709, when he was about twenty-one, and it was published without his name on 15 May 1711. Its 744 lines fall into three parts. The first tells critics, like poets, to follow Nature, and describes the rules of the ancients as Nature methodised, discovered in the practice of Homer and the great poets. The second names the causes of bad judgement, beginning with pride and a little learning. The third sets out the manners of a good critic and ends with a short history of criticism from Aristotle to William Walsh, the friend who had advised the teenage Pope to aim at correctness.

Its ideas were the common stock of critics in Pope’s day, gathered from his early reading of Horace, Quintilian, Vida, Boileau and others. What was new was the expression. Several lines have passed into proverbs, among them “A little learning is a dang’rous thing”, “To err is human, to forgive, divine” and “Fools rush in where Angels fear to tread”, and in the passage on versification Pope makes the sound of each line imitate its sense, so that a needless Alexandrine drags its slow length along.

Admirers and enemies

The Essay made Pope’s name. Addison praised it in The Spectator on 20 December 1711. John Dennis, who recognised himself in the angry critic Appius, answered with a pamphlet that sneered at Pope’s youth and his body. An illness at about twelve had ruined Pope’s health and bent his figure, and he grew to only four feet six inches. The quarrel with Dennis was never made up.

Johnson thought The Rape of the Lock the most delightful of Pope’s poems and the finest example of comic poetry in English, and he believed that the Essay alone would have placed Pope among the first poets and critics. After Pope’s death William Warburton printed the poems with their author’s last corrections in 1751. The Romantics reacted against Pope’s school, and by 1906 Parrott, an admiring editor, could say that the Essay was worth taking seriously only as history, though he still admired its portrait of the true critic. The Rape has never lost its readers.

The texts and this edition

Pope revised the two poems for years. The Rape of the Lock exists in the two cantos of 1712, the five cantos of 1714 and the final text of 1717 with Clarissa’s speech. The Essay was corrected after Dennis’s attack, and its second edition, in 1713, carried Pope’s name. Most modern readers meet the poems in the forms Pope left them.

This Astor edition prints the two poems complete and unabridged in their later forms, with line numbering throughout and explanatory footnotes keyed to the text. It adds new material on Pope’s life, his sources and influences, eighteenth-century society, the game of ombre, the reading public, the poems’ reception and the critical debates surrounding them, with historical context, character studies, critical perspectives, a glossary and teaching pathways.
