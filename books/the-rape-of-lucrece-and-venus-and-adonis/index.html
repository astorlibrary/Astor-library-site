About the work

From Venus’s pursuit to the fall of the Roman kings.

The account below follows each poem’s story and what the pair reveal when read together, from erotic comedy to political change.

The work

Story, argument and structure.

In Venus and Adonis, the goddess of love pursues a young hunter who repeatedly refuses her. Shakespeare moves between erotic comedy, rhetorical display, physical coercion and the grief that follows Adonis’s fatal hunt.

The Rape of Lucrece follows Tarquin’s assault and its aftermath through power, shame, testimony and the language by which Lucrece tries to understand a crime committed against her. Her public naming of Tarquin and death become the catalyst for the overthrow of the Roman monarchy.

Reading the work

What the complete text brings into view.

Read together, the poems form a deliberate pair. Both concern desire that refuses reason and an attempt to possess another person through language and description. Both end with death and transformation, but their tonal and political consequences are sharply different.

Every difficult word, image and allusion is explained on the same page as the relevant line, allowing the poetry and commentary to remain in continuous relation.

Reading routes

Desire, testimony and a name made in print.

Print and reputation

The two dedications and early quartos belong to the period when Shakespeare first established his literary name in print.

Venus and Adonis

Venus pursues the young hunter Adonis, who resists her advances and wishes to return to hunting. Much of the poem consists of her attempts to persuade him and of his replies. The narrative also includes the episode of his horse and a mare, before following the consequences of Adonis’s fatal encounter with a boar.

The poem develops material from the story of Venus and Adonis in Ovid’s Metamorphoses. Shakespeare expands the exchange between the two figures into a sustained verbal and physical encounter. The explanatory material identifies classical references, unfamiliar language and rhetorical devices, allowing readers to follow the changing argument rather than encountering the poem only as a retold myth.

Lucrece and the Roman narrative

The Rape of Lucrece concerns Tarquin’s assault on Lucrece, the wife of Collatine, and its aftermath. Tarquin’s deliberations and Lucrece’s speech occupy a much larger part of the poem than a short account of the incident would suggest. After the assault she seeks to report what has happened to her husband and others.

The story belongs to Roman accounts of the expulsion of the kings and the establishment of the republic. Shakespeare’s poem includes Lucrece’s extended response to a picture of the fall of Troy before returning to her testimony and death. The supporting material explains the classical setting and the narrative’s movement from an individual crime to public action.

Verse, language and the edited text

The reading text is based on the quartos of 1593 and 1594. Spelling and punctuation are modernised, while Shakespeare’s wording, stanza forms and rhyme are preserved. This is a modernised reading edition: its presentation makes an explicit distinction between alterations to spelling and punctuation and changes to the substance of the poems.

Venus and Adonis uses six-line stanzas, while Lucrece uses seven-line rhyme-royal stanzas. The printed line numbers and same-page notes provide a way to follow references and return to particular passages. Explanations address difficult vocabulary, allusions and figures of speech in relation to the lines where they occur.

Sources, contexts and close reading

The introductions and essays cover classical sources, publication, language, style and the main figures in each narrative. Close readings follow particular exchanges and passages, including persuasion and refusal in Venus and Adonis and Lucrece’s attempts to describe the wrong done to her. They give readers evidence to examine rather than treating a broad thematic label as an explanation of the whole poem.

Reading the two works in one volume also permits comparisons between their different speakers and outcomes. Venus’s direct appeals, Adonis’s resistance, Tarquin’s self-argument and Lucrece’s addresses to personified forces are distinct uses of speech. The commentary helps identify those formal differences while keeping the complete passages available for judgement.

Reference material and study

A glossary supplies a further route into recurring language, alongside the notes attached to individual lines. Material on reception, critical approaches and teaching gives additional points of entry for readers studying the poems after an initial reading. The supporting essays remain separate from the texts, so that the poems can be read continuously or consulted passage by passage.

The combined edition is particularly useful for work on Shakespeare’s early publication, his handling of classical stories and the relationship between narrative and argument in verse. It contains both complete poems rather than an anthology of extracts. The original dates, the quarto basis, the modernisation policy and the location of the explanatory notes are stated so readers can identify the kind of text and assistance the volume provides.
