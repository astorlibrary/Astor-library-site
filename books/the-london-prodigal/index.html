About the work

A Prodigal Son in the London credit economy.

The account below follows Flowerdale’s frauds and Luce’s disguised loyalty, and shows how the 1605 play measures ruin in bonds, sureties and arrest.

The work

What happens.

A merchant returns from Venice disguised as Kester and lets his son Flowerdale believe him dead. He watches the young man lie about cargoes, gamble, borrow and exploit friends who stand surety for him.

Flowerdale invents wealth to marry Luce but is arrested for debt at the church door. Luce refuses to abandon him, yet he takes her money and rejects her. Disguised as the Dutch servant Tanikin, she follows his descent towards robbery and disgrace.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

The biblical Prodigal Son becomes a commercial London story in which ruin is measured through credit, forged documents and the arrival of officers.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

Authorship

The 1605 quarto was printed by Thomas Creede for Nathaniel Butter. It names William Shakespeare in full and states that the play was performed by the King’s Men. Butter later published the 1608 quarto of King Lear, making him a bookseller with a documented connection to Shakespeare’s printed work. The attribution is therefore a real feature of an early edition, even though it has not secured the play a generally accepted place in the canon.

The London Prodigal was omitted from the 1623 First Folio, then included among the additions to the Third Folio in 1664. The company attribution has attracted much less dispute than the author’s name. A play could belong to Shakespeare’s company without being written by him, and its absence from the Folio is evidence to weigh, not a final answer.

Credit

Flowerdale’s supposed prosperity is repeatedly established through reports of assets, trade and inheritance. Other people must decide whether to trust those reports and whether to risk their own money or reputation on his behalf. Marriage negotiations become part of this economy because a prospective husband’s financial standing is material evidence about the future he can offer a household.

The appearance of officers at the wedding brings those claims into direct conflict with enforceable debts. A ceremony intended to confirm a new social position instead exposes the liabilities behind it. Following who lends, guarantees, passes on or withholds money makes the action clear: in this play debts have consequences on stage.

The father’s disguise

Flowerdale’s father watches events under another identity, creating a difference between what the young man believes about his family and what the audience knows. His presence allows conduct to be observed when an expected source of authority and rescue appears to have been removed. The device also postpones recognition and makes the eventual restoration of a family relationship a staged event.

Luce’s disguise has another purpose. It enables her to remain near a husband who has rejected her and to intervene without being recognised as his wife. The two disguises should consequently be followed separately: one involves concealed paternal judgement, the other continued practical assistance. Together they make the ending depend on the disclosure of identities as well as on Flowerdale’s declaration that he will change.
