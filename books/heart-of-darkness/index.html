Marlow’s story

Five old friends sit on a yawl in the Thames estuary at dusk. One of them, the seaman Marlow, remarks that this too has been one of the dark places of the earth, and tells how he once got command of a river steamer for a Continental company trading in Africa. On the coast and at the Company’s stations he finds chained labourers, a grove where sick workers have been left to die, and agents who talk of nothing but ivory. Everyone speaks of Mr Kurtz, the chief of the Inner Station, who sends down more ivory than all the others together.

After months of repairs Marlow takes the steamer upriver. His helmsman is killed in an attack from the bank, and at the Inner Station he finds a young Russian disciple, heads on stakes round the house, and Kurtz himself, dying and worshipped by the people of the district. On the way downriver Kurtz cries out twice, The horror! The horror!, and dies. Back in Europe Marlow visits the woman Kurtz was to marry, and when she asks for his last words he tells her it was her name.

Conrad in the Congo

Conrad was born Józef Teodor Konrad Korzeniowski in 1857 at Berdychiv, then in the Russian Empire, to a Polish family; his father was imprisoned for nationalist activity, and he was an orphan at eleven. He went to sea from Marseilles in 1874, joined British ships in 1878 and became a British subject in 1886. As a boy, he later wrote, he had put his finger on the blank centre of the map of Africa and said that he would go there.

In 1890 he did. His diary begins at Matadi in June; he walked overland to Stanley Pool and went up the river as second in command of the company steamer Roi des Belges, as far as Stanley Falls, carrying the unfinished manuscript of his first novel, Almayer’s Folly. The voyage broke his health and helped end his career at sea. In his Author’s Note of 1917 he said the story was experience “pushed a little (and only very little) beyond the actual facts of the case”.

The Congo Free State

The territory Conrad saw belonged personally to King Leopold II of the Belgians, whose Congo Free State the European powers had recognised in 1885. Its business was ivory, and increasingly rubber, extracted by forced labour. The story never names the Congo, Belgium or the King: Marlow’s Company has its headquarters in a city like a whited sepulchre, and its stations are the Company’s station, the Central Station and the Inner Station.

The book appeared before the campaign that exposed the state. In 1903 the British consul Roger Casement travelled through the rubber regions, and his report of 1904 led to the founding of the Congo Reform Association. International pressure ended Leopold’s rule in 1908, when Belgium annexed the territory as the Belgian Congo.

How the story is told

Almost the whole book is Marlow’s tale, reported by an unnamed listener who opens and closes it with the Thames. Marlow had first appeared in Conrad’s story Youth. For him, the listener says, the meaning of an episode lay outside it, like a haze brought out by a glow. Marlow gives impressions before explanations: little sticks flying about before he realises they are arrows, round knobs on the fence posts before he sees that they are heads.

Kurtz reaches Marlow first as a name and a voice, and the most important things are left unsaid: the rites offered to him, the meaning of his last words, the truth Marlow will not tell the Intended because it would have been too dark altogether. The book ends where it began, with the Thames seeming to lead into the heart of an immense darkness.

Readers and critics

Modernist writers took the book up. T. S. Eliot gave his poem The Hollow Men (1925) an epigraph from it, the manager’s servant’s announcement Mistah Kurtz, he dead. For much of the century it was read as an attack on imperial greed and as a journey into the self, and Francis Ford Coppola moved it to the Vietnam War in Apocalypse Now (1979).

In 1975 the Nigerian novelist Chinua Achebe gave a lecture, published in 1977 as An Image of Africa, arguing that the book treats Africa as a backdrop for a European’s breakdown and denies its African characters speech and full humanity. Every serious discussion since has had to answer him, whether by defending the book’s irony and its exposure of empire, or by reading it as evidence of the racism it describes.

The 1902 text and this edition

The story first ran in three instalments in Blackwood’s Edinburgh Magazine, as The Heart of Darkness, in February, March and April 1899, beginning in the magazine’s thousandth number. It appeared in book form on 13 November 1902 in Youth: A Narrative, and Two Other Stories, between Youth and The End of the Tether.

This Astor edition prints the complete 1902 book text with explanatory footnotes throughout, historical context on the Congo Free State, critical perspectives and a documentary record. It adds original material on Conrad’s life and sources, the Congo Free State, narrative structure, language, major critical debates, reception history and the surviving documentary record.
