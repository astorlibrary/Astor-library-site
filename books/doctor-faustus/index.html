Tragedy · c. 1592–93

Doctor Faustus, Christopher Marlowe

Faustus begins by reviewing logic, medicine, law and theology. He dismisses every discipline because it cannot make him invulnerable or supreme. The first failure is therefore interpretive before it is magical: a gifted reader breaks texts away from context and hears only the limits he wants to escape.

This Astor Library edition presents the complete play with summaries and explanatory notes, then places the A-text and B-text, the Faust-book source, morality drama, Reformation argument and the play’s extraordinary stage life in view.

The longer study material follows knowledge, spectacle, repentance, despair and the changing relation between Faustus and Mephistopheles. It treats the play’s theological questions seriously without pretending that performance can settle them once and for all.

The shape of the work

Twenty-four years narrowing towards one hour

The play begins with global fantasies and ends by making every minute audible.

01 · Choice

Learning is declared too small

Faustus reviews the university disciplines and values each only by whether it can grant absolute power. Valdes and Cornelius encourage magic.

02 · Bargain

A warning becomes a contract

Mephistopheles explains hell, but Faustus negotiates with Lucifer, signs in blood and ignores the command “Homo, fuge”—fly, man.

03 · Diversion

Questions become spectacles

Good and Evil Angels compete for him. When his questions reach creation, Lucifer distracts him with the Seven Deadly Sins.

04 · Diminishment

Empire becomes entertainment

Faustus travels and performs tricks for the Pope, Emperor, duke and lesser customers. His imagined command of nations contracts into display.

05 · Last hour

Time becomes the antagonist

An Old Man makes a final appeal. Faustus asks for Helen, then for time, concealment and extinction as the clock moves towards midnight.

Read closely

Read the warning inside the wish

Each route begins with particular language and follows what changes around it.

Opening

Faustus begins by misreading

The opening soliloquy looks like an orderly survey of learning, but Faustus has decided its verdict in advance. Medicine is useless because it cannot grant immortality; law is petty; theology is reduced to selected statements about sin and death.

When he imagines the magician as “a mighty god”, scholarship becomes a fantasy of domination. His learning supplies magnificent language, but not the patience or humility needed to read accurately.

Hell

Mephistopheles knows more

“Why, this is hell, nor am I out of it,” Mephistopheles tells him. The statement overturns Faustus’s assumption that hell is merely a distant place or an old story intended to frighten people. For the fallen spirit, memory itself has become punishment.

Faustus speaks as though he has gained a servant, but Mephistopheles serves Lucifer and draws him towards the loss he already suffers. The supposed master repeatedly ignores the clearest witness available to him.

Midnight

The final hour changes time

The last soliloquy compresses twenty-four years into minutes and seconds. Faustus hears the clock and imagines escape through earth, cloud, water or non-existence. Each proposed refuge fails before the next can be completed.

Even here, he often seeks delay or annihilation rather than forgiveness. “I’ll burn my books” comes only at the end, when the knowledge he pursued can no longer protect him.

Book, history, form

Text, theology and theatre

Context matters here because it changes what we can notice in the writing.

Reformation

Grace and despair

Free will, repentance, predestination and despair were urgent theological questions. The play keeps repentance within Faustus’s hearing while showing how habit can make freedom feel impossible.

Text

Two early versions

The 1604 A-text and 1616 B-text differ in length, wording and incident. Theatre records also show payments for additions in 1602. Every modern performance must choose among versions.

Form

Morality meets tragedy

Good and Evil Angels, devils and personified sins come from morality drama. Marlowe places them beside a Renaissance scholar, classical reference and ambitious blank verse.

Lucifer’s agent: tempter, servant and deeply informed witness to damnation.

Lucifer

The Evil Angel

The Old Man

Questions that stay open

01

Can he still repent?

Is repentance genuinely possible for Faustus until the final moment, and what makes possibility feel unavailable?

02

Who controls whom?

Does Mephistopheles control Faustus, or does Faustus choose the interpretation he most wants to hear?

03

Why does ambition shrink?

Why do dreams of universal command end in tricks and entertainments for the powerful?

04

Which play do we see?

How does the tragedy change when a production follows the A-text, B-text or a combination?
