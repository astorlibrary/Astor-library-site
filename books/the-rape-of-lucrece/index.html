Narrative poem · 1594

The Rape of Lucrece, William Shakespeare

Shakespeare’s poem begins among Roman soldiers, when Collatine praises his wife’s chastity as an incomparable treasure. Tarquin’s desire forms before he has seen her. The language of praise, possession and military competition has already made a private person into something men can display to one another.

This Astor Library edition presents the complete poem with a clear account of each movement, explanatory notes and material on rhyme royal, complaint, Roman history, Shakespeare’s sources, the Troy painting, testimony and the poem’s political ending.

The additional material treats sexual violence with care and keeps responsibility with Tarquin. It also asks how inherited codes of chastity and family honour make Lucrece carry the public burden of a crime committed against her.

The shape of the work

From a boast at war to a revolution at home

The crime occupies one part of the poem; its aftermath, interpretation and public use occupy far more.

01 · Ardea

Praise becomes competition

Collatine praises Lucrece before Roman commanders. Tarquin hears her virtue described as treasure and leaves the camp to see her.

02 · Collatium

Hospitality is betrayed

Lucrece receives a kinsman and prince. Tarquin debates with himself, enters her chamber and uses force together with a threat to fabricate adultery.

03 · Morning

The world looks unchanged

Tarquin flees. Lucrece addresses Night, Opportunity and Time while trying to decide how the crime can be spoken.

04 · Troy

A painting becomes a witness

Lucrece studies the fall of Troy, identifies with Hecuba’s grief and directs anger towards the deceptively credible Sinon.

05 · Rome

Testimony becomes revolt

She names Tarquin, demands an oath and dies. Brutus takes command of the response and leads the movement that expels the Tarquins.

Read closely

Praise, consent and the control of a story

Each route begins with particular language and follows what changes around it.

Possession

When praise becomes dangerous

Collatine’s praise seems to honour Lucrece, but the narrator gives it the language of wealth and display. Tarquin wants what another man has described as incomparable. The first danger lies in a culture of comparison, ownership and competitive boasting.

The imagery of siege continues that culture. Tarquin approaches a body as another city to be entered. Rome’s soldiers claim to defend the state abroad, yet the habits of conquest return with him into a Roman household.

Responsibility

Tarquin knows what he is doing

Before entering the chamber, Tarquin supplies almost every argument against the crime. He knows the claims of kinship, hospitality, law and reputation. He is not unable to distinguish right from wrong; he keeps reasoning until reason serves the decision he wants.

His threat to stage evidence of adultery joins sexual violence to narrative violence. He intends not only to assault Lucrece but to decide what other people will believe the assault means.

Testimony

The Troy painting makes a witness

The painting gives Lucrece a field of faces when her own grief exceeds language. She finds Hecuba’s sorrow and studies Sinon, whose credible appearance concealed betrayal. She asks how an image can make suffering visible and how a face can hide intention.

Soon she must turn experience into an account for her family. Her naming of Tarquin is clear, but Brutus then converts testimony and body into a political emblem. Justice begins, yet control of the story moves quickly back to men.

Book, history, form

Stanza, history and reputation

Context matters here because it changes what we can notice in the writing.

Form

Rhyme royal and complaint

The seven-line stanza carries serious narrative and complaint. Its couplet can sound conclusive, but Shakespeare often makes one argument spill into the next, fitting a mind unable to settle imposed injury.

History

A Roman founding story

Livy and Ovid connect Lucretia’s death with the overthrow of the Tarquins. Shakespeare delays that political end for hundreds of lines in order to remain with Lucrece.

Code

Chastity and family honour

Lucrece insists that her mind did not consent, yet fears Tarquin’s act will attach to her name. The poem exposes a system in which innocence can be inwardly certain and publicly fragile.

Characters

People and voices in the poem

Lucrece

The poem’s central consciousness, separating bodily violation from inward consent and making suffering public.

Sextus Tarquinius

The king’s son, who understands the obligations he breaks and threatens to control the evidence.

Collatine

Lucrece’s husband, whose praise sets the action in motion and whose grief later claims its place.

Lucretius

Lucrece’s father, who receives her testimony within the family language of possession and loss.

Brutus

The concealed political intelligence who directs the oath and turns mourning into rebellion.

The narrator

A voice at once compassionate, rhetorical and intrusive, making us ask who may turn suffering into poetry.
