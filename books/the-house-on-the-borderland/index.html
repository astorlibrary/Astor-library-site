The House on the Borderland

Two Englishmen on a fishing holiday in the west of Ireland find a ruined house on the edge of a chasm and, in the rubble, a damaged manuscript. Its writer describes pig-faced creatures climbing out of the pit, and a vision in which he watches the sun die. William Hope Hodgson published the novel in 1908.

The discovered manuscript

Two friends, Tonnison and Berreggnog, on holiday in the west of Ireland, find the remains of a house and a damaged manuscript. Its unnamed writer, generally called the Recluse, describes his life in an isolated building with his sister and his dog, Pepper. The account includes attacks by swine-like creatures and investigations of a pit beneath the house.

The manuscript also describes experiences beyond the immediate siege: a vast plain, another house, changes in the apparent rate of time and visions of the solar system’s destruction. The framing travellers and the fictional editor are part of the presentation. The Recluse’s account reaches us incomplete, and nobody else confirms it.

First publication and composition

Chapman & Hall published The House on the Borderland in London in 1908. The first edition’s extended title described the manuscript as discovered in 1877 in ruins south of the fictional village of Kraighten. Hodgson’s Author’s Introduction carries the date 17 December 1907.

The edition examines surviving correspondence about the composition of Hodgson’s novels. Letters indicate that the manuscripts preceded their eventual publication by several years. It distinguishes the documentary evidence from the later identification of a manuscript called The House of Mysteries with The House on the Borderland, an attribution generally accepted but still dependent on inference.

The complete text

The text follows the Project Gutenberg transcription of the 1908 edition. The volume includes the Author’s Introduction, all twenty-seven numbered chapters, the intervening ‘Fragments’ and the closing poem ‘Grief’. No word of the novel has been added, removed or reordered.

Hodgson’s irregular punctuation, dashes, ellipses, capitalisation and spellings remain. Typographical repairs address broken spacing, quotation marks and the dateline of the introduction. Passages presented as doubtful or damaged manuscript material retain their original quotation marks. The textual note explains each of these decisions.

The fictional editor’s footnotes

The seventeen numbered notes in the source belong to the fictional editor who claims to prepare the manuscript for publication. They are part of Hodgson’s fiction, not explanatory footnotes newly written by Astor. Sixteen appear at the foot of the pages carrying their markers.

One source note has no surviving marker in the transcription used for this edition. Its wording is preserved in the textual note, and the numbering of the remaining notes is left unchanged. The edition does not invent a precise attachment point. This distinction keeps the original fictional apparatus separate from the new chapter summaries, context and introductory commentary.

Hodgson, Ireland and astronomy

William Hope Hodgson was born in Essex in 1877. His father’s work as an Anglican clergyman took the family to Ardrahan in County Galway during Hodgson’s childhood. Hodgson later spent years at sea, worked in physical culture and developed interests in photography before publishing novels and magazine fiction.

Two dedicated context essays examine the landscape around Ardrahan and the astronomy of the later chapters. The first considers the region’s geology and disappearing rivers; the second explains the scientific ideas relevant to the novel’s enormous timescales and celestial events. These provide documented contexts for the fiction without claiming that its supernatural experiences are scientific predictions.

Commentary and later reception

Chapter summaries review the narrative’s changing scenes and the relationship between the house, the underground spaces and the visionary passages. Historical context, character studies, key passages and an account of the narrative framework accompany the text. The glossary supports readers encountering unfamiliar terms or usages.

The reception history follows the novel beyond its original publication, including its reappearance in Arkham House’s 1946 collection of Hodgson’s four novels. Critical perspectives and afterlives address subsequent readings and adaptations. A chronology, sources and influences, and bibliography place this particular novel within Hodgson’s career and provide reference points for further study.
