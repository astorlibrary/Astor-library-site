The argument of Common Sense

Common Sense begins with first principles. Society, Paine says, is produced by our wants and government by our wickedness, so government is at best a necessary evil, and the much-praised English constitution is a machine in which the crown always has its way. Monarchy has no warrant in nature or in scripture, and hereditary succession has given nations fools and minors, and England eight civil wars and nineteen rebellions since the Conquest.

Only then does Paine turn to America. The fighting at Lexington in April 1775 has closed the period of debate. Britain protected the colonies for the sake of her own trade; “Europe, and not England, is the parent country of America”; and there is something very absurd in supposing a continent to be governed by an island. Paine proposes a continental charter and a Congress chosen by districts, and argues that America is strong enough, and young enough, to form its own government now.

Paine in Philadelphia

Thomas Paine was born at Thetford in Norfolk in 1737, the son of a Quaker staymaker. He worked as a staymaker and as an excise officer, and was dismissed from the excise in 1774, after a campaign in London for better pay for its officers. Benjamin Franklin, whom he had met in London, gave him a letter of introduction, and he arrived in Philadelphia on 30 November 1774 and was soon editing the Pennsylvania Magazine.

According to Paine’s own account, he had already begun Common Sense when Franklin suggested in October 1775 that he write a history of the troubles. Benjamin Rush found him a printer bold enough to take it, the bookseller Robert Bell, and the pamphlet went on sale on 10 January 1776 at two shillings, without the author’s name. Readers guessed at Franklin or at Samuel or John Adams; Franklin wrote in February that Paine was the reputed and, he thought, the real author.

Printers and pirates

Paine soon fell out with Bell, who, according to Paine’s biographer Moncure Conway, kept much of the money Paine had meant for the cause. The enlarged edition of 14 February, printed by William and Thomas Bradford, added new passages in the body of the work, a table costing a navy ship by ship, an Appendix answering the king’s speech, which had reached Philadelphia on the day of first publication, and an address to the Quaker leaders who had declared for the Crown. Bell answered with editions of his own, padded with Large Additions by other writers, and he also printed Plain Truth, a reply by Candidus, attributed to James Chalmers, which called independence ruinous, delusive and impracticable.

Paine took no profit. He priced the pamphlet so low that, he said later, he was out of pocket on Bradford’s books. In April 1776 he estimated that 120,000 copies had been sold in three months, and Conway thought that probably half a million were sold in the end. Washington wrote of its sound doctrine and unanswerable reasoning, and Paine answered its critics, among them Cato, the Rev. William Smith, in the letters he signed The Forester.

The American Crisis

In 1776 Paine joined a Pennsylvania division of the Flying Camp, and then became a volunteer aide to General Nathanael Greene at Fort Lee. He began the first Crisis at Newark during the retreat across New Jersey, and it appeared in the Pennsylvania Journal on 19 December 1776, a week before Washington’s attack on Trenton; Conway repeats the tradition that Washington had it read to his troops. Its opening, “These are the times that try men’s souls”, is Paine’s most famous sentence.

Fifteen more papers followed before December 1783, every one signed Common Sense: open letters to Lord Howe and General Sir William Howe, to the British peace commissioners, to the people of England and to Lord Shelburne; appeals after the defeats at the Brandywine and Charleston; arguments for taxes and for the quotas Congress asked of the states. Paine served meanwhile as secretary to the Congress’s Committee of Foreign Affairs from 1777 until January 1779, when his public attack on Silas Deane forced his resignation, and in 1781 he went to France with John Laurens to seek French money.

Union, and afterwards

The later papers look beyond the war. The thirteenth, published on 19 April 1783, eight years after Lexington, announces that the times that tried men’s souls are over and calls the union of the states the most sacred thing in the constitution of America. The last, of December 1783, warns that without a union able to act together on trade the states are a medley of individual nothings.

Paine then began a second revolutionary career in Europe. Rights of Man (1791–1792), his reply to Edmund Burke, led to his outlawry in Britain; he was elected to the French National Convention, imprisoned in Paris during the Terror, and wrote The Age of Reason, an attack on revealed religion that made him many enemies. He returned to the United States in 1802 and died in New York on 8 June 1809.

The texts and this edition

Common Sense was reprinted across the colonies and in Britain in 1776, and its text varies from printing to printing. The Bradford edition of 14 February is the enlarged text Paine arranged himself, described on its title page as a new edition with several additions in the body of the work. The Crisis papers have been collected many times; Paine spoke of thirteen, and Conway’s edition prints the thirteen numbered papers with the Crisis Extraordinary and the two Supernumerary papers.

This Astor edition prints the complete enlarged text of Common Sense dated 14 February 1776, with its Appendix and the Address to the People called Quakers, and all sixteen pieces of The American Crisis: the thirteen numbered papers, the Crisis Extraordinary and the two Supernumerary papers, following the war from the retreat of 1776 through independence and peace. Section summaries and explanatory footnotes accompany the texts, and new material covers Paine’s life, printing and piracy, the political and military context, contemporary rebuttals and later reception, with historical context, critical perspectives and a glossary.
