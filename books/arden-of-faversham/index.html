Arden of Faversham

In February 1551 Thomas Arden of Faversham was murdered in his own house by men his wife, Alice, had hired. Holinshed’s Chronicles told the story, and an anonymous play printed in 1592 put it on stage. Some scholars find Shakespeare in the scene where Alice quarrels with her lover, Mosby; the introduction sets out the cases for him, for Thomas Kyd and for several hands.

About the work

Alice Arden’s conspiracy and its failures.

The plot against Thomas Arden runs through detected poison, locked doors, misfiring weapons and fog before succeeding at home; the quarrel with Mosbie stands at the play’s psychological centre.

The work

What happens.

Alice Arden is in love with Mosbie and plots the death of her husband Thomas. The conspiracy expands through the servant Michael, Clarke the painter, the dispossessed Greene and the hired killers Black Will and Shakebag.

Attempts fail through detected poison, interruption, locked doors, misfiring weapons and fog. Suspense repeatedly collides with black comedy while each escape can look like providential warning.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

Alice directs the conspiracy but moves between determination, fear, repentance and renewed commitment. Her quarrel with Mosbie is the psychological centre of the play and a central passage in the authorship debate.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

Land and status

The first exchange introduces property before it turns fully to adultery. Franklin brings Arden news of a royal grant of former abbey lands, but Arden is preoccupied with Alice and Mosbie. This juxtaposition matters because the play supplies grievances against Arden beyond his wife’s desire to be rid of him. His position as a landowner places him in relationships with people whose material interests differ from his own.

Greene joins the conspiracy because of a dispute over land, while the sailor Reede confronts Arden about the loss of his holding and its consequences for his family. These encounters do not excuse the murder. They do give the central household a specific economic setting and explain why the conspiracy can recruit people who have no personal investment in Alice’s affair.

The conspirators’ motives

The participants do not all want the same result from Arden’s death. Alice wants freedom to marry Mosbie; the hired killers seek payment; Michael is encouraged through the prospect of marrying Susan, Mosbie’s sister. Promises of money, affection and advancement keep the plot moving, but they also make its members dependent on one another’s reliability. An agreement made in one scene can become a source of anxiety in the next.

Black Will and Shakebag repeatedly encounter practical obstacles to the work for which they have been hired. Travel, weather, bystanders and Arden’s changing movements interfere with their plans. These delays give the audience information the intended victim does not possess and create repeated occasions on which the conspirators must improvise. The eventual killing inside the house follows a long sequence of failed attempts in less controllable settings.

The household and the evidence

Once Arden is dead, the conspirators have to manage physical traces as well as their accounts of what happened. The moved body, the blood in the house and the footprints in the snow are how the murder is found out. Alice’s attempts to explain Arden’s absence must compete with what other characters have seen and with what the search reveals.

The closing account of punishments places the staged murder within a larger record of discovery and retribution. It is useful to distinguish this reported conclusion from the action the audience has witnessed. The play dramatises private conversations and competing motives that a chronicle could not independently certify, even while presenting itself as a tragedy based on a known local crime.
