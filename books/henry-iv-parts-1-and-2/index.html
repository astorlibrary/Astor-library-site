Henry IV, Parts 1 and 2

Across two plays, a prince learns to turn reputation into authority while a king discovers that possession of the crown cannot make it peaceful. Between them stands Falstaff: companion, actor, critic of honour and the person Hal will finally leave behind.

Henry IV, Parts 1 and 2, William Shakespeare

Part 1 creates three competing centres: Henry’s anxious court, the Percy rebellion and Falstaff’s Eastcheap. Hal can move between them. At Shrewsbury, he defeats Hotspur and saves his father, but military victory does not settle the kingdom or complete his transformation.

Part 2 slows the world down. Rumour distorts news, age catches Falstaff and the king, and rebellion ends through Prince John’s calculated deception. Hal inherits the crown, confirms the authority of the Chief Justice and rejects Falstaff. Read together, the plays make kingship a long education in performance, law and loss.

One arc, two different plays

The second play repeats people and places, but it deliberately changes their energy.

Part 1

A world of rivals

Hal and Hotspur are compared as possible sons and possible futures. Falstaff answers Hotspur’s martial honour with jokes, evasions and a body unwilling to become an abstraction on the battlefield.

Shrewsbury

Reputation remade

Hal kills Hotspur and publicly answers his father’s disappointment. Falstaff survives by pretending to be dead and then claims credit for Hotspur’s death, turning battle back into contested storytelling.

Part 2

A world after the climax

Shrewsbury is over, but no one feels restored. Part 2 dwells on illness, age, debt, false news and the administrative work of containing another rebellion.

Succession

The crown changes the room

Hal becomes Henry V and the play immediately tests whether his reform is personal theatre or public principle. Confirming the Chief Justice and rejecting Falstaff make both possibilities visible.

Ten facts across the sequence

Publication and performance history help explain why the plays work both separately and together.

  • Henriad The two parts sit between Richard II and Henry V in the sequence often called the second tetralogy or Henriad.
  • Popular quarto Part 1 was reprinted many times before the 1623 Folio, evidence of strong demand.
  • Hotspur advertised The first Part 1 title page foregrounds Hotspur, Shrewsbury and Falstaff’s “humorous conceits”.
  • Oldcastle trace Evidence suggests the comic knight was once associated with Sir John Oldcastle before becoming Falstaff.
  • Two quarto states Part 2’s Qa and Qb differ, and the Folio introduces further textual choices.
  • Two openings Part 1 opens with Henry planning crusade and facing rebellion; Part 2 opens with Rumour making false news.
  • Two rebel endings Hotspur dies in combat; the later rebels surrender after Prince John’s promise and are arrested.
  • Two fathers Henry is Hal’s kingly father; Falstaff acts as unruly companion, tutor and substitute father.
  • Jerusalem Henry dies in the Jerusalem Chamber, fulfilling a prophecy through the name of a room.
  • Falstaff rejected The sequence ends its central friendship before Henry V reports Falstaff’s death.

    • Hal’s education

    Hal does not simply change from bad to good; he learns how different audiences make public identity.

    Tavern

    Learning to play a role

    Hal and Falstaff improvise the king and prince before Hal faces his father. Eastcheap gives him practice in voices, timing and the management of a crowd.

    Court

    Answering expectation

    Henry compares Hal unfavourably with Hotspur. Hal’s promise to redeem his reputation is political because reputation determines whether subjects can imagine him as a ruler.

    Battle

    Making reform visible

    At Shrewsbury, Hal’s courage becomes public action. Yet the play keeps Falstaff nearby to expose how quickly heroic history selects, reshapes and misreports what happened.

    Crown

    From intimacy to institution

    As Henry V, Hal cannot rule as though tavern loyalty and royal office were the same thing. His first decisions are staged as tests: justice must continue, and Falstaff must no longer presume on private friendship.

    Characters across both parts

    Each major figure gives honour, history and authority a different voice.

  • King Henry IV A usurper who can rule but never silence the memory of how he became king.
  • Prince Hal A self-conscious performer whose promised reform culminates in a genuinely consequential transfer of power.
  • Sir John Falstaff A comic genius and social exploiter who refuses heroic language, recruits the vulnerable and expects private affection to defeat public change.
  • Hotspur The martial rival whose speed, honour and impatience make him both admirable and politically limited.
  • Prince John Hal’s colder brother, effective at Gaultree because he treats words as instruments rather than bonds.
  • Lord Chief Justice A figure of office who insists that law applies even to the heir.
  • Mistress Quickly and Doll Tearsheet Tavern women whose work, care and exposure keep the comic world connected to economic and legal danger.
  • Shallow and Silence Country justices whose memories and hospitality turn ageing into comedy, sadness and self-invention.

    • Sources, text and the historical frame

    Chronicles supply events; Shakespeare’s double play creates the relationships through which those events become drama.

    Hall and Holinshed

    National history reorganised

    English chronicles supplied Henry’s unstable reign, the Percys, Shrewsbury and the later rebellion. The plays reorganise those records around fathers and sons, court and tavern, public image and theatrical speech.

    Print

    Separate publication lives

    The quartos circulated the plays separately, while the First Folio presents them as first and second parts. A combined edition asks readers to notice continuity without smoothing away Part 2’s slower and more troubled design.

    History and invention

    Falstaff changes the scale

    Falstaff is not a neutral record of a historical companion to Prince Henry. He lets the sequence test chivalric honour, recruitment, war reporting and the human consequences of a prince’s carefully timed reform.

    Stage and screen as a pair

    Adaptation often turns the two plays into one large performance centred on Hal and Falstaff.

    1965

    Orson Welles, Chimes at Midnight

    Welles combined the Henry IV material with passages from other history plays and placed Falstaff at the centre. Its battle of Shrewsbury and final rejection make comedy, mud, violence and loss part of one visual argument.

    2012

    BBC, The Hollow Crown

    Two films preserve each part while giving the sequence a continuous cast. Jeremy Irons, Tom Hiddleston and Simon Russell Beale play Henry, Hal and Falstaff across the movement towards succession.

    2024

    Player Kings

    Robert Icke combined the plays for the stage, with Ian McKellen as Falstaff. Like every compression, it had to decide how much of the political, provincial and tavern worlds could surround the central relationship.
