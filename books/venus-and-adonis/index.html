Narrative poem · 1593

Venus and Adonis, William Shakespeare

Venus is the goddess of love but cannot command another person’s desire. Her language is inexhaustible; Adonis often answers with silence, brief objection or an attempt to leave. Comedy grows from that mismatch without making pursuit harmless.

This Astor Library edition presents the complete poem with summaries and notes, then follows Shakespeare’s Ovidian source, the six-line stanza, the 1590s minor epic, arguments about procreation, animal appetite, hunting, metamorphosis and reception.

The study material keeps the gender reversal and consent in view together. It asks how changing the expected pursuer alters our response, why it does not cancel coercion and what Venus does with grief after the boar ends a courtship Adonis never accepted.

The shape of the work

An interrupted hunt becomes an argument about desire.

Five movements carry the poem from comic pursuit to death, flower and prophecy.

01 · Interruption

Venus stops the hunt

She seizes Adonis’s horse, pulls him down, praises his beauty and tries to keep him with her.

02 · Refusal

Every argument meets a no

Venus moves through command, flattery, tears, fainting and procreation. Adonis tries to distinguish love from appetite.

03 · Animals

The horses answer differently

The stallion follows a jennet. Venus treats immediate attraction as natural proof; Adonis refuses the analogy.

04 · Warning

The boar enters the language

Adonis plans the dangerous hunt. Venus urges the harmless hare and imagines wounds the boar may inflict.

05 · Metamorphosis

A body becomes a flower

Venus finds Adonis killed. A purple-and-white flower rises in his place, and she prophesies pain in future love.

Read closely

Pursuit, analogy and the wound.

Each route begins with particular language and follows what changes around it.

Consent

Reversal does not remove coercion

The desiring speaker is an older, powerful woman and the beautiful reluctant object a young man. The reversal can be comic, especially when the goddess of love cannot secure love. Her verbal abundance makes his refusal small on the page without making it less real.

Venus removes Adonis from his horse, pins him, steals kisses and treats resistance as an invitation to try again. Changing the expected gender pattern does not cancel the pursued person’s repeated no.

Nature

What the horses cannot prove

The stallion and jennet enact the spontaneous appetite Venus wants Adonis to accept. She turns the scene into evidence that beautiful creatures owe their beauty to sexual union and reproduction.

Adonis is not persuaded because analogy is not consent. The animals show appetite exists; they cannot decide what one human owes another person’s desire. Her language of use and waste makes beauty sound like capital that must be invested.

Death

The boar changes the poem’s tone

Venus has imagined Adonis’s body as something to kiss, possess and preserve. When she finds it wounded, the tusk is described through the language of a fatal kiss. The image makes final violence answer earlier pursuit.

Her prophecy enlarges one frustrated desire into a history of love itself: affection will be unstable, jealous and liable to loss. The ending asks whether she has learned from refusal or made the future speak her disappointment.

Book, history, form

Ovid, print and the six-line stanza.

Context matters here because it changes what we can notice in the writing.

Source

Ovid changed

In the Metamorphoses, Venus and Adonis are lovers before the fatal hunt. Shakespeare makes refusal the central action, turning adaptation into an argument about a love story with only one willing lover.

Genre

The Elizabethan minor epic

The fashionable 1590s form joins classical erotic narrative to worked rhetoric and changing tone. The poem appeared during prolonged closures of London’s theatres.

Stanza

Six lines at a time

Alternating rhyme creates movement and counterargument; the couplet clinches a joke or sharpens an image. Venus repeatedly believes her case is complete, only for refusal to begin it again.

Keep in view

Desire, refusal and the speaking world.

Venus

Goddess of love, but not master of another person’s desire; witty, forceful, inventive and finally grief-stricken.

Adonis

A beautiful young hunter whose brief responses remain a consistent refusal of Venus and the future she proposes.

The narrator

Alternately amused, sensuous, sympathetic and ominous, delighting in rhetoric while exposing self-interest.

The stallion

An image of appetite escaping restraint, treated by Venus as a natural law Adonis should imitate.

The jennet

The mare whose reciprocal desire makes the animal episode a poor model for the human pursuit.

The boar

The creature Venus fears and Adonis chooses, joining the poem’s erotic and violent imagery.
