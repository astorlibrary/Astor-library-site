William Shakespeare

Cymbeline

A banished husband, a wager on a wife’s fidelity, two lost princes and a threatened Roman war meet in one of Shakespeare’s boldest late plays. Imogen crosses Britain in disguise while false evidence repeatedly defeats trust—until a famously crowded final scene puts the scattered story back together.

Cymbeline, William Shakespeare

Imogen has secretly married Posthumus, angering her father, King Cymbeline, and rejecting the Queen’s son Cloten. Exiled in Rome, Posthumus accepts Iachimo’s wager that Imogen can be seduced. Iachimo cannot win honestly, so he enters her chamber concealed in a trunk and turns private details into manufactured proof.

The action then opens outward: Imogen travels in disguise, meets the king’s lost sons without knowing them, mistakes Cloten’s body for Posthumus’s, and is drawn into Britain’s conflict with Rome. The final scene resolves identities, deaths, disguises and political conflict in a cascade of revelations.

A late play made from many forms

Fairy tale, chronicle history, domestic tragedy, comedy and romance occupy the same theatrical world.

  • First Folio position The play appears last among the Folio tragedies, immediately before the volume’s concluding material.
  • British king Cymbeline comes from legendary British history, although Shakespeare freely reshapes the chronology.
  • Italian wager The plot against Imogen draws on a tale associated with Boccaccio’s Decameron and later retellings.
  • Roman Britain The tribute dispute gives private betrayals a national and imperial frame.
  • Three names Imogen becomes the boy Fidele; Cloten wears Posthumus’s clothes; mistaken identity changes what bodies seem to prove.
  • Lost heirs Guiderius and Arviragus have grown up away from court as Polydore and Cadwal.
  • A moving setting The action travels among Britain, Rome, Milford Haven and a Welsh cave.
  • Recognition scene The last act gathers an unusually large number of revelations into one sustained public reckoning.

    • How the story unfolds

    The plot repeatedly replaces knowledge with convincing appearances, then tests how those errors can be repaired.

    Act 1

    Marriage and exile

    Cymbeline banishes Posthumus for marrying Imogen. In Rome, Iachimo provokes him into a wager about Imogen’s fidelity. The wager treats a woman’s honour as male property before Imogen has any knowledge of it.

    Act 2

    The false victory

    Iachimo hides in Imogen’s chamber and observes its objects and the mole on her body. His bracelet and precise description persuade Posthumus that she has betrayed him. Evidence is accurate; the story attached to it is false.

    Act 3

    Flight towards Wales

    Posthumus orders Pisanio to kill Imogen. Pisanio refuses, helps her become Fidele and sends her towards Milford Haven. There she finds Belarius, Guiderius and Arviragus without knowing that the young men are her brothers.

    Act 4

    A body in borrowed clothes

    Guiderius kills Cloten, who is wearing Posthumus’s clothes. Waking beside the headless body, Imogen believes her husband is dead. The play makes costume appear more reliable than the face the audience cannot see.

    Act 5

    Battle and recognition

    Britain defeats Rome, Posthumus survives battle and Imogen’s identity returns to public view. Confession answers deception, the lost princes are recognised, and Cymbeline chooses peace and tribute after military victory.

    Characters and relationships

    Nearly every relationship is tested by distance, disguise or a persuasive lie.

  • Imogen Cymbeline’s daughter, steadfast but never passive; she argues, travels, improvises and survives several versions of her own erasure.
  • Posthumus Leonatus An orphan raised at court whose love gives way to jealousy when Iachimo offers theatrical proof.
  • Iachimo A visitor from Rome who turns observation into fraud and private space into a stage for false evidence.
  • King Cymbeline A legendary British ruler whose family crisis and dispute with Rome converge.
  • The Queen A political schemer who wants Cloten to marry Imogen and secretly experiments with poison.
  • Cloten The Queen’s violent, entitled son; his borrowed clothes produce the play’s darkest misrecognition.
  • Belarius A banished lord who abducted the king’s sons and raised them away from court.
  • Guiderius and Arviragus The lost princes, raised as Polydore and Cadwal, whose courage restores them to Britain’s future.
  • Pisanio Posthumus’s servant, whose refusal to obey a murderous command makes him one of the play’s moral centres.

    • Sources, text and interpretation

    Shakespeare joins legendary history to a European tale of slandered fidelity.

    Holinshed

    Britain and Rome

    Raphael Holinshed’s Chronicles supplied a framework for Cymbeline, tribute and British history. The play does not reproduce the chronicle faithfully: it brings events and inventions together for theatrical effect.

    Boccaccio

    The wager plot

    The story of a husband wagering on his wife’s fidelity, a deceiver entering her room and knowledge of her body serving as false proof has close parallels in the Decameron. Shakespeare makes the tested woman the adventurous centre of the play.

    First Folio

    The only early printed text

    No earlier quarto is known. Editors therefore begin with the 1623 Folio, whose placement among the tragedies keeps the play’s genre productively unsettled.

    Genre

    Tragedy turned towards repair

    Jealousy, supposed death and political violence point towards catastrophe. Recognition reverses that movement, but the ending does not erase what Imogen has endured or the speed with which Posthumus believed the lie.

    Stage and afterlife

    The play’s mixture of intimacy, battle, disguise and revelation gives directors unusually wide tonal choices.

    By 1611

    Simon Forman watches the play

    Forman’s surviving account records central parts of the plot, including the wager and the concealed visitor in Imogen’s chamber. It is valuable evidence that the play had reached the stage by 1611.

    Victorian stage

    Imogen at the centre

    Nineteenth-century productions often treated Imogen as the play’s great attraction and reshaped the surrounding plot. Her endurance and fidelity made her a celebrated role even when the play itself was heavily adapted.

    Modern performance

    Britain, empire and gender

    Recent productions have found new force in the Roman tribute, the construction of British identity and the pressure placed on Imogen’s body. Doubling and gender-conscious casting can make the play’s unstable identities visible.
