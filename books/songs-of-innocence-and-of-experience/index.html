William Blake

Songs of Innocence and of Experience

Blake wrote, etched, printed and coloured these songs himself: the Songs of Innocence in 1789, then the Songs of Experience, joined to them in 1794 under the subtitle Shewing the Two Contrary States of the Human Soul. This edition prints the complete collection with reproductions of his plates.

Two books of songs

The Songs of Innocence open with a Piper who is asked by a child on a cloud for a song about a Lamb, and who writes his songs down so that every child may hear them. They are songs of lambs and shepherds, children at play, a newborn baby called Joy, and a God who sits by every cradle, but they already include a little black boy taught that his body is a cloud, a small chimney sweeper sold by his father, and the charity children of London filing into St Paul’s.

The Songs of Experience answer them. A Bard calls to a fallen Earth, and Earth replies that a jealous father of men keeps love in chains. A sweep in the snow blames God, Priest and King, a rose is destroyed by an invisible worm, a garden of love is full of graves, and a tiger burns in the forests of the night. Several poems share a title or a situation with a song of Innocence, so that the two books read as a set of pairs.

Illuminated printing

Blake called his method illuminated printing. He drew the words and the pictures together, in reverse, on small copper plates of about five inches by three, using a medium that resisted acid, and then etched away the rest so that text and design stood in relief. The plates were printed in a single tint and coloured by hand in watercolour, with Catherine Blake helping to print and colour them. Blake said that the process had been revealed to him in a dream by the spirit of his brother Robert, who died in 1787.

As a result there was never an edition in the ordinary sense. Blake printed copies as they were wanted, from 1789 until his death in 1827, and each was coloured separately, the later ones more richly than the first. In 1793 his prospectus offered the Songs of Innocence and the Songs of Experience at five shillings each.

Moving poems and varying copies

Blake did not keep the two books fixed. The first issue of Innocence had thirty-one plates, including The Little Girl Lost, The Little Girl Found, The School Boy and The Voice of the Ancient Bard, which he later moved into Experience, and in some late copies The Voice of the Ancient Bard went back to Innocence. The order of the plates differs in almost every copy, and late in life Blake wrote out a list showing how the Songs ought to be paged and placed.

One poem, A Divine Image, which answers The Divine Image of Innocence line for line, was etched but left out of every copy issued in Blake’s lifetime; most editions print it at the end.

Blake’s London

William Blake was born in 1757 in Broad Street, Soho, the son of a hosier, and at fourteen was apprenticed to the engraver James Basire. He made the Songs of Innocence while living in Poland Street, Soho, and finished the Songs of Experience in Lambeth in the revolutionary 1790s. Many of the Songs of Experience were first drafted in a notebook that had belonged to his brother Robert, which Blake turned upside down and began using from the back around 1793; The Tyger, The Chimney Sweeper and London all began there.

London itself is named directly: the chimneys swept by children, the charity children at St Paul’s, the chartered Thames, the blackening churches and palace walls, and the midnight streets of London, where Blake hears mind-forg’d manacles in every voice.

Readers

In Blake’s lifetime the Songs attracted little notice. Charles Lamb had heard of the poems without seeing them, but knew the one about the tiger, which he called glorious. They were first printed in ordinary type in 1839, twelve years after Blake’s death, in an edition by J. J. Garth Wilkinson.

Alexander Gilchrist’s Life of William Blake (1863) brought them to a wide public, and in 1905 John Sampson published a verbatim text taken from Blake’s engraved plates. The Tyger and London are now among the best-known poems in English.

The text and this edition

Because Blake’s copies differ in order, colouring and small details of the text, every edition has to choose which arrangement to follow and how to present his spelling and punctuation.

This Astor edition prints the complete collection with line numbering and 55 reproductions of Blake’s illuminated plates, with explanatory footnotes and textual notes. The supporting material covers illuminated printing, the movement of poems between the two collections, Blake’s sources, historical context, the paired poems, critical perspectives and reception, with a glossary, a bibliography and teaching pathways.
