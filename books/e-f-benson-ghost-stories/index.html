E. F. Benson Ghost Stories

This volume brings together two of E. F. Benson’s major supernatural collections in full, The Room in the Tower and Other Stories (1912) and Visible and Invisible (1923): twenty-nine stories in all, seventeen from the first book and twelve from the second.

The stories

Benson wrote these stories, his preface of 1912 says, to give some pleasant qualms to a reader sitting up late, so that he might glance into the dark corners of the room before going to bed. They range widely. There are vampires in The Room in the Tower and Mrs. Amworth, a slug-like Thing that walks in darkness in Negotium Perambulans, a witch at a Highland pool in Gavon’s Eve and the god Pan in The Man Who Went Too Far; there are premonitions of death in The Bus-Conductor and In the Tube, and murderers who will not lie quiet.

Many are told after dinner or by the fire, often to a listener who begins by doubting. Benson likes to set educated English people among beliefs they laugh at, in a Highland village that takes the black hares for its neighbours or a Cornish parish with a legend carved on its altar-rails, and then to prove the beliefs right. He is often funny too: at Church-Peveril the ghosts are as ordinary as the post, and in Mr. Tilly’s Séance a man run over by a traction-engine goes on to his séance and catches the medium cheating.

E. F. Benson

Edward Frederic Benson was born on 24 July 1867 at Wellington College, where his father, Edward White Benson, was headmaster. His father became Archbishop of Canterbury, and died in 1896 in the church at Hawarden, during the absolution at morning prayer. Benson was educated at Marlborough and King’s College, Cambridge, studied archaeology at the British School at Athens, and in 1893 made his name with Dodo, a comic novel of society. He spent the early months of 1895 and 1896 with his sister Margaret at Luxor, where she had leave to excavate the temple of Mut at Karnak.

Egypt stayed with him. In his memoir, Our Family Affairs, 1867–1896, he wrote that “to this day my nightmares are set on the Nile”, and At Abdul Ali’s Grave is set at Luxor. After the First World War Benson settled at Lamb House in Rye, which had been Henry James’s home; he took a sub-lease of it in 1920, was mayor of Rye for three years from 1934, and died in London on 29 February 1940. Rye became Tilling in his comic novels about Mapp and Lucia, and two of these stories, Machaon and Roderick’s Story, are set in a Tilling house whose garden room has a bow window looking down a cobbled street, like the window at Lamb House he describes in his preface to Miss Mapp.

Motor cars and telephones

Two of the stories were first printed in the Pall Mall Magazine in 1906. The Dust-Cloud, in January, was announced as E. F. Benson’s Motor Story, and The Bus-Conductor, in December, was subtitled A Modern Ghost Story. The labels fit the whole collection. A dead man’s car raises dust on a Norfolk road and sounds its siren; a hanged murderer confesses down a telephone; a scientist tries to play back a dead brain with a gramophone needle; a bus and a speeding motor crash in Sloane Square; a man foresees a suicide at the Dover Street tube station.

Benson’s explanations are modern as well. His theorists compare a haunted room to a phonograph record, the passing of feeling between minds to a Marconi message, the brain to a gramophone plate and time to a tunnel. In Machaon a medium in trance leads the narrator to X-ray treatment for his servant’s cancer, and the surgeon who gives it admits that he has no faith in the method and yet it is curing his patient. Set among motors, telephones and the Tube, the old terrors come close to the reader’s own life.

Psychical research

The Society for Psychical Research was founded in 1882 to collect evidence about thought-transference, clairvoyance, spiritualism and apparitions, and its first president was the Cambridge philosopher Henry Sidgwick, whose sister Mary was Benson’s mother. In 1886 three of its leading members, Edmund Gurney, F. W. H. Myers and Frank Podmore, published Phantasms of the Living, which argued that telepathy was a fact and that apparitions of people at the moment of death were seen too often to be chance.

Benson’s stories speak the society’s language. Dr Teesdale in The Confession of Charles Linkworth knows himself to be a sensitive; Weston in At Abdul Ali’s Grave is a member of the society who once exposed a fraudulent medium, and quotes Myers; The Terror by Night tells of a death felt at a distance, and In the Tube of a phantom of the living. Benson mocks the business freely as well. In Mr. Tilly’s Séance the medium cheats, the spirits talk twaddle, and the society’s investigators reject the one séance that was genuine.

Visible and Invisible

Benson’s second book of ghost stories appeared in 1923, and the war has left its marks on it. And the Dead Spake begins on the evening of 4 August 1914, and in an air raid its scientist plays a fragment of a dead soldier’s brain that sings Tipperary; the woman Roderick Cardew loved lost both her sons in France. Séances are everywhere, from the one in Tilling where the medium’s control is a nun given to platitudes to Mrs Cumberbatch and her trumpet, and in the last story a dying man scorns the half-guinea medium who tells a lady in black that her Thomas is very busy and happy, helping people.

Visible and Invisible also ends by arguing with itself. In Roderick’s Story the narrator, a writer settling into a new house in Tilling, has been writing spook stories about a vampire, an elemental and the reincarnation of an execrable personage, and his dying friend reads them and calls them all shadows and no light, and false. Roderick’s own story, of the woman he loved, seen in her true beauty at the moment of her death, is the light he says is missing, and it closes the book.

Later readers

Surveying supernatural fiction in 1927, H. P. Lovecraft named Benson among the writers who had lately done well by the weird short story. He praised The Man Who Went Too Far, and chose from Visible and Invisible the Cornish monster of Negotium Perambulans and the half-human creatures of The Horror-Horn.

On screen, The Bus-Conductor had a longer life. Ealing’s Dead of Night (1945), a film made of ghost stories told by the guests in a house, credits Benson among the authors of its stories, and in its first tale Miles Malleson, as a hearse driver and then a bus conductor, speaks Benson’s line: just room for one inside, sir.

The texts and this edition

The Room in the Tower and Other Stories was published in London by Mills & Boon in 1912, with a short preface saying that some of the tales had appeared before in magazines and the rest were new, and that The Man Who Went Too Far was the germ of Benson’s novel The Angel of Pain; a second edition followed the same year. Visible and Invisible was published in London by Hutchinson in 1923.

This Astor edition brings the two collections together in full: 29 complete supernatural stories, seventeen from The Room in the Tower and twelve from Visible and Invisible. They are The Room in the Tower; The Dust-Cloud; Gavon’s Eve; The Confession of Charles Linkworth; At Abdul Ali’s Grave; The Shootings of Achnaleish; How Fear Departed from the Long Gallery; Caterpillars; The Cat; The Bus-Conductor; The Man Who Went Too Far; Between the Lights; Outside the Door; The Terror by Night; The Other Bed; The Thing in the Hall; The House with the Brick-Kiln; And the Dead Spake——; The Outcast; The Horror-Horn; Machaon; Negotium Perambulans; At the Farmhouse; Inscrutable Decrees; The Gardener; Mr. Tilly’s Séance; Mrs. Amworth; In the Tube; and Roderick’s Story.

Story summaries and explanatory footnotes accompany the texts, and new material covers the historical context and the psychical-research background, with a glossary, the publication history of the stories and their reception.
