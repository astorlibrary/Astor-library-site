What happens.

Charles I: Selected Writings presents a documentary history of a contested reign from the disputes of the 1620s to the king’s execution in 1649.

The seventeen selections include private letters to Henrietta Maria, two royal answers to the Petition of Right and the Declaration of Sports.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

The Petition of Right and constitutional disagreement

The Petition of Right arose from disputes over taxation, imprisonment and the use of royal power. Presented in 1628, it challenged taxation without parliamentary consent, arbitrary imprisonment, the billeting of soldiers and the imposition of martial law in peacetime. Charles’s responses therefore belong to a specific negotiation about the rights of subjects and the government’s need for money; they are not simply general declarations about monarchy.

The two answers included in this selection allow the form of a royal response to be examined as well as its argument. Official wording had consequences because king and Parliament were disputing what counted as a binding concession. Reading these documents in order helps explain why apparent agreement did not remove the differences that continued into the following decade.

Private correspondence made public

Letters and instructions need a different kind of reading from proclamations and parliamentary answers. A message to Henrietta Maria presumes knowledge shared by husband and wife; an official declaration addresses a wider audience and is designed for circulation. Neither form provides unmediated access to the writer’s thoughts. Recipient, purpose and the circumstances of preservation affect what can reasonably be concluded from it.

The royal papers captured after the Battle of Naseby in June 1645 made this distinction politically urgent. Correspondence intended for restricted use passed into enemy hands and was published by Parliament. The resulting public evidence was also selected and presented as an argument against the king. The edition’s attention to provenance is especially useful when a private document has reached the reader through an adversary’s publication.

Trial, execution and the royal image

At his trial in Westminster Hall in January 1649, Charles challenged the court’s authority and refused to enter a plea. The exchanges consequently concern jurisdiction as well as the accusations against him. The death warrant records the decision to execute him and the commissioners who authorised it; it should be distinguished from the king’s own reported words on the scaffold on 30 January.

Eikon Basilike belongs to the struggle over how that death would be remembered. Published in 1649 as a presentation of the king’s private meditations and sufferings, it became associated with the image of Charles as a Christian martyr. John Gauden’s claim to authorship makes attribution a substantial historical problem. The selection prints it separately, so this famous royal voice is kept apart from documents Charles certainly wrote or spoke.
