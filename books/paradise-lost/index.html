John Milton

Paradise Lost

Milton’s epic begins after a war in Heaven and ends with two people walking out of the only home they have known. Cosmic rebellion, political argument, marriage, temptation, grief and the possibility of renewal are held within one immense blank-verse design.

Epic poem · 1667 / 1674

Paradise Lost, John Milton

Satan’s journey gives the poem some of its most memorable speeches, but Adam and Eve’s ordinary acts of trust, disagreement and blame carry its largest human consequences. Milton does not make Genesis a simple sequence of familiar events. He turns it into an argument about freedom, foreknowledge, persuasion and love.

This Astor Library edition presents the complete poem with book-by-book summaries and explanatory notes. Longer material opens the biblical and epic traditions, Milton’s politics and blindness, the 1667 and 1674 editions, blank verse, character, imagery, criticism and reception.

The support helps readers follow long sentences without flattening them and read Satan’s rhetoric without mistaking force for truth. It keeps the cosmic scale connected to the poem’s most intimate question: how two people speak to each other before and after trust fails.

The shape of the work

From defeated rebellion to a world outside Eden.

Five large movements hold the twelve-book poem together.

01 · Books I–II

Hell reorganises defeat

Satan and the fallen angels wake, build Pandemonium and debate their next action. Satan crosses Chaos towards the new world.

02 · Books III–IV

Heaven and Eden come into view

God foresees the Fall, the Son offers redemption, and Satan enters Eden to watch Adam and Eve.

03 · Books V–VIII

Raphael tells the larger history

The angel recounts revolt, war, the Son’s victory and creation; Adam describes awakening and meeting Eve.

04 · Books IX–X

Persuasion becomes blame

Eve and Adam eat by different paths. Judgement, shame and accusation follow while Sin and Death build their road.

05 · Books XI–XII

History enters the garden

Michael shows Adam future violence, the Flood and redemption. Adam and Eve leave together with grief and responsibility.

Read closely

Sentence, persuasion and conversation.

Each route begins with particular language and follows what changes around it.

Invocation

The opening sentence declares its scale

The poem begins “Of Man’s first disobedience”, but its governing verb—“Sing”—does not arrive until line six. Before stating its action, the sentence has moved through forbidden fruit, death, suffering and promised restoration.

The “Heavenly Muse” belongs to Sinai and biblical creation as well as classical epic. Milton’s purpose to “justify the ways of God to men” gives readers a standard against which to test every account of liberty, obedience and justice.

Rhetoric

Satan’s language is powerful because unstable

Satan turns defeat into the vocabulary of courage. He names permanent loss as a campaign and calls Heaven tyrannical because he cannot possess its throne. The speeches are memorable because grievance sounds like principle.

Milton’s similes can make him gigantic or heroic, then introduce hidden diminishment. “Darkness visible” captures the method: language still has brilliance, but what it reveals is misery.

The Fall

Conversation fails before appetite wins

Book IX first separates Adam and Eve through an argument about work, trust and untested virtue. Satan turns Eve’s reasoning, independence and desire for knowledge towards a conclusion she had not first sought.

Adam is not deceived about the command. “To lose thee were to lose myself” sounds loving but places attachment above judgement. After they eat, speech moves rapidly from intimacy to concealment and blame.

Book, history, form

Epic, politics and a poem made by voice.

Context matters here because it changes what we can notice in the writing.

Genre

A biblical epic

Milton adopts invocation, councils, catalogues, warfare, journeys and extended similes from Homer and Virgil, then revises heroism around patience, sacrifice and inward government.

History

Written after political defeat

Milton defended republican government before the Restoration. Arguments about liberty, tyranny, kingship and counsel carry that history without becoming a simple political key.

Voice

Dictation and revision

Milton was blind and dictated the epic to assistants. Its long sentences and changing pauses reward reading aloud; the move from ten books to twelve records major revision.

Keep in view

The voices inside the argument.

The narrator

An ambitious poetic voice invoking divine aid and reflecting on blindness, vocation and the act of telling.

Satan

A defeated rebel whose rhetorical command coexists with envy, self-deception and diminishing freedom.

God the Father

The voice of foreknowledge and justice at the centre of the poem’s theological problem.

The Son

The agent of victory in Heaven and volunteer for humanity’s eventual redemption.

Adam

Thoughtful and affectionate, but knowingly choosing disobedience rather than separation.

Eve

Intelligent, curious and verbally alert, exceeding the simplified role Satan assigns her.
