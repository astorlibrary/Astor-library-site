The journey and its interruptions

J., George and Harris decide that a holiday on the river will improve their health. They take Montmorency, a fox terrier, and set out on the Thames with the intention of combining exercise and rest. Packing, finding food, managing a tow-line and arranging a night’s shelter repeatedly become more difficult than their confident preparations suggest.

The account follows the river journey while making room for remembered incidents, arguments, stories about acquaintances and information about places along the route. The result is not simply a day-by-day travel log. Jerome’s narrator moves between the present trip and anecdotes prompted by what the men see or attempt to do.

The original publication

Three Men in a Boat was serialised in Home Chimes before its publication by J. W. Arrowsmith in 1889. The Jerome K. Jerome Society’s formal bibliography records the serial from September–December 1888 and January–June 1889. Arrowsmith’s own later advertisement dates the first book publication to August 1889.

The original subtitle is To Say Nothing of the Dog. This Astor edition preserves the complete 1889 text, including its spelling and punctuation, and reproduces the original illustrations by A. Frederics. Their presence is an edition feature: the drawings themselves accompany the text, in addition to the separate discussion of their publication history.

J., George, Harris and Montmorency

The travellers draw on Jerome and two friends, George Wingrave and Carl Hentschel. The supporting account identifies these men while explaining the changes made in the fiction. Hentschel, associated with Harris, was a teetotaller despite the drinking attributed to the fictional character. A biographical model therefore does not make every episode a factual record of that person’s conduct.

Montmorency was invented. Jerome’s later account of the dog is treated separately from the lives of the three men, and the distinction is preserved in the edition’s discussion. This information is useful when the easy familiarity of the narration makes the whole journey appear to be straightforward autobiography.

The Victorian Thames

The historical sections follow the places and practices that occur in the narrative: locks, towing, camping, meals, boats and the cost of a river holiday. These details explain what the travellers are trying to do before the comedy develops from their failures. They also make it possible to distinguish a historical practice from something a modern reader might assume about recreational boating.

The edition supplies factual material on prices, laws and customs associated with the journey. Jerome’s digressions into local history are considered alongside the places that prompt them. Readers can follow the route as a journey through an inhabited and managed river landscape, including its towns, landing places and other river users.

Illustration, printing and reception

A. Frederics’s original pictures were text illustrations, rather than a set of separately inserted plates. Their reproduction here preserves a feature of the 1889 book that is easily lost in editions presenting only the prose. The illustration history explains their place in the original publication without implying that modern copies are printed from the original physical blocks.

The reception history treats figures and recollections with care. Arrowsmith’s 1909 advertisement records 202,000 copies produced during the book’s first twenty years; that is a production figure, not an independently established sales total. Likewise, famous hostile review quotations known from Jerome’s later memoir are identified as his retrospective testimony when their original newspaper issue, date and page have not been established.

Reading with the Astor material

Chapter-end summaries and Context material provide the annotation. No new editorial footnotes have been inserted into the narrative. A reader can check the progress of the journey, consult the historical background to an episode and then return to Jerome’s text and Frederics’s drawings.

The supporting material also covers Jerome’s career, publication, illustration and reception, and the circumstances behind the familiar characters. Its reference points are the actual river, the nineteenth-century book and the documentary record of its circulation. Together with the complete illustrated text, those sections support reading Three Men in a Boat both as a comic narrative and as a book made for the late-Victorian reading public.
