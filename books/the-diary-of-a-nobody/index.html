George and Weedon Grossmith

The Diary of a Nobody

‘Why should I not publish my diary?’ asks Charles Pooter, a City clerk who has just moved into The Laurels, Brickfield Terrace, Holloway. For fifteen months he records trouble with tradesmen, a humiliating ball at the Mansion House and his son Lupin’s schemes. It began as a series in Punch in 1888–89.

Charles Pooter’s diary

Charles Pooter, a City clerk, moves with his wife Carrie into the Laurels, a rented house in Holloway. He begins recording his daily life, arguing that an ordinary person has as much reason to keep a diary as a celebrated one. His entries describe household improvements, work, dinner invitations, friends and disagreements with his son, Lupin.

The sequence covers fifteen months of events, including visits from Gowing and Cummings, Lupin’s changing occupations and relationships, and Pooter’s attempts to maintain dignity during social mishaps. The book retains the diary form: what readers know comes through the details Pooter selects and the account he gives of his own conduct.

From Punch to the 1892 book

The Diary of a Nobody first appeared in twenty-six instalments in Punch between May 1888 and May 1889. J. W. Arrowsmith of Bristol published the expanded book in 1892. The book added roughly four further months of diary entries and included twenty-six illustrations by Weedon Grossmith.

The Astor editorial material follows the differences between serial and book publication and the novel’s later reception. The early book editions attracted limited attention; the 1910 issue, with prefatory tributes from Lord Rosebery and Augustine Birrell, helped establish a wider readership. Publication history is therefore part of the account of how the book acquired its later reputation.

The text and its presentation

This edition follows a public-domain transcription of an Arrowsmith book edition. It includes Mr Pooter’s introduction and the original chapter arguments, the short summaries printed beneath the chapter headings. The final chapter retains the source’s heading ‘Chapter the Last’, appearing as the twenty-fourth chapter in the contents.

The text has not been modernised. Historical spellings, punctuation and unusually frequent italics remain, as do Pooter’s misquotations, mistakes and mishearings. They are part of the book as printed and have not been corrected. The textual note distinguishes the original book’s contents from the summaries and explanatory material added for this edition.

The Grossmith brothers

George Grossmith and his younger brother Weedon both had careers in performance. George worked as an entertainer and became associated with leading comic roles in Gilbert and Sullivan’s operas. Weedon trained as an artist before developing a career as an actor, playwright and theatre manager.

Both brothers are credited as authors of the novel. The introductory biography connects their theatrical and professional backgrounds with the circumstances of the book’s writing and publication. Weedon Grossmith’s twenty-six drawings for the 1892 volume are discussed in the introduction but not reproduced; the ornaments in this edition are modern.

Holloway, work and domestic expenditure

The historical context examines Holloway as a London suburb and the City clerk as an occupation with particular expectations of respectability, dress and conduct. Pooter’s house, commuting, office relationships and social ambitions belong to this specific late nineteenth-century setting.

Money, furnishings, clothing, entertainment and domestic service recur in the diary. The notes explain period references and vocabulary so that an expense, invitation or household decision can be understood in its immediate circumstances. The commentary also attends to the difference between the position Pooter believes he holds and the responses he records from employers, tradespeople, friends and family.

Chapter apparatus and reference material

Each chapter is followed by editorial sections headed ‘The Story So Far’ and ‘Context’. These are additional to the Grossmiths’ original chapter arguments: one reviews what happens in the chapter, while the other supplies relevant historical information. Explanatory footnotes appear at the foot of the page.

The front matter includes biography, publication details, a textual note, chronology and material on the narrative framework, language and reception. A glossary provides reference support for unfamiliar expressions. Together these features allow the complete diary to be read alongside information about Victorian London, the authors’ careers and the novel’s changing readership, while leaving its original wording intact.
