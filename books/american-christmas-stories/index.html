American Christmas Stories

‘A Visit from St. Nicholas’, usually credited to Clement Clarke Moore, appeared without a name in the Troy Sentinel in December 1823 and gave Santa Claus eight named reindeer. It is collected here with eight complete American Christmas stories written between 1872 and 1905, from Bret Harte to O. Henry, and six chapters of history.

The nine literary texts

The eight stories are Bret Harte’s ‘How Santa Claus Came to Simpson’s Bar’; Kate Douglas Wiggin’s The Birds’ Christmas Carol; William Dean Howells’s ‘Christmas Every Day’; Willa Cather’s ‘The Burglar’s Christmas’; Paul Laurence Dunbar’s ‘One Christmas at Shiloh’; L. Frank Baum’s ‘A Kidnapped Santa Claus’; Owen Wister’s A Journey in Search of Christmas; and O. Henry’s ‘The Gift of the Magi’.

The volume also prints ‘A Visit from St. Nicholas’, attributed to Clement Clarke Moore. Its first appearance in 1823 supplies the collection’s opening date; the stories extend from Harte’s 1872 tale to O. Henry’s 1905 story. All nine literary texts are complete, rather than excerpts chosen to illustrate the historical chapters.

How the volume is arranged

Part I, ‘The American Christmas’, consists of six historical chapters: ‘A Holiday by Statute’, ‘The Poem That Made Santa Claus’, ‘Nast Draws Him’, ‘The Week Between’, ‘The Christmas Number’, and ‘The Kettle and the Counter’. They examine legislation, verse and images of Santa Claus, plantation holidays, periodical publishing, charity and commerce.

Part II presents the eight stories, each introduced by a headnote identifying its first publication, the text used in this edition and relevant later history. Part III follows the material into film and theatre and examines familiar Christmas customs that developed after the period covered by the anthology.

Christmas as a historical subject

The opening chapters distinguish different stages in the holiday’s legal recognition. The edition discusses Louisiana’s 1837 legislation, the 1870 federal holiday provision for the District of Columbia and its later extension to federal employees elsewhere. These are separate developments rather than a single national founding date for Christmas.

Other sections trace how publishers, illustrators, shops and charitable organisations contributed to public observance. The chapter on the Christmas and New Year interval in the slave South uses historical evidence to examine who received a holiday and under what conditions. The chronology gives readers a dated framework for these developments alongside the literary publications.

Writers, places and publishing

The selection ranges from a California mining settlement to New York domestic life, with additional settings and social circumstances supplied by the other stories. Its authors wrote for different outlets and purposes. Wiggin’s novella was connected with fundraising for a free kindergarten; Howells’s story appeared in a children’s periodical; Cather published her Christmas story while working in Pittsburgh.

The headnotes document these publishing circumstances rather than treating every story as an expression of a uniform American celebration. Newspaper supplements, magazines and separately published books reached different readers. Their histories also explain why publication dates, copyright dates and the dates on book title pages sometimes need to be recorded separately.

Complete texts and editorial checking

Each story is printed from an identified copy-text, with its spelling, punctuation and dialect retained. The introductory note records two particularly consequential source checks. An abridged version of Harte’s story circulating online removes its opening third; the source was replaced to restore the complete story.

Several digital versions of ‘The Gift of the Magi’ contain damaged ligatures and an incorrect amount of change. The edition uses a checked text rather than reproducing those scanning errors. Historical language is left in the primary texts, with relevant explanation in the headnotes. The editorial apparatus distinguishes an author’s original wording from the context supplied for present-day readers.

Context and reference apparatus

Stone-coloured panels headed ‘Context’ provide historical facts tied to the material just read, including laws, prices, addresses and magazine issues. Seven chapters include an ‘Anachronism watch’ item that identifies which objects or customs belong to which period. The volume deliberately uses this historical apparatus rather than adding plot-summary boxes.

A Christmas glossary, chronology, sources and further reading support the main chapters. The closing sections on stage, screen and later customs extend the account beyond first publication while keeping the anthology’s literary range clear: eight stories and one poem from 1823 to 1905, accompanied by original historical reporting and textual documentation.
