John Skelton

Selected Poems of John Skelton

John Skelton, priest and tutor to the young Henry VIII, wrote in short, tumbling rhymed lines that are still called Skeltonics. The fourteen poems here run from his elegy on the Earl of Northumberland to ‘Philip Sparrow’, a schoolgirl’s lament for her pet bird, and his attacks on Cardinal Wolsey. The text is Alexander Dyce’s of 1843, and every cut is marked.

About the work

Fourteen works from the early Tudor court.

The account below sets out the selection, from formal elegy to Skeltonic satire, and names the longer poems given as declared extracts.

The work

What happens.

Skelton was a poet, priest, royal tutor and satirist at the early Tudor court. His short, heavily rhymed lines became so recognisable that they are now called Skeltonics, but the selection also shows his command of formal elegy, devotional writing and longer courtly structures.

The volume includes On the Death of King Edward the Fourth, Upon the Dolorous Death of the Earl of Northumberland, Upon a Dead Man’s Head, Womanhood, Wanton, Ye Want, Mannerly Margery Milk and Ale, The Bowge of Court, Philip Sparrow, The Tunning of Elinour Rumming, Speak, Parrot, Colin Clout and Why Come Ye Not to Court?, with lyrics from The Garland of Laurel.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

Philip Sparrow, Speak, Parrot, Colin Clout, Why Come Ye Not to Court? and the Garland lyrics are presented as clearly identified extracts. The headnotes state every cut and keep selection from masquerading as completeness.

Dyce’s textual apparatus is removed and line numbers are added for reference. Original spelling remains part of the encounter with Skelton’s voice, while a glossary and footnotes keep that choice usable.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

Short rhymes and longer formal structures

The term Skeltonic is useful for the short lines and extended runs of rhyme found in much of Skelton’s best-known verse, but it does not describe everything he wrote. The Bowge of Court uses seven-line rhyme-royal stanzas, and the elegies require attention to their formal organisation as well as their historical occasions. Changes of line length and stanza form are part of the range represented by the selection.

Colin Clout explicitly anticipates an objection to the roughness of its verse and answers that an irregular surface can carry substantial matter. Its sequences of rhyme work alongside changes of speaker, reported complaint and abrupt questions. Following the sentence across the short lines is particularly useful: a line ending supplies emphasis without necessarily marking the end of a thought.

A dream of the court and a gathering at an alehouse

The Bowge of Court presents courtly dependence through a dream of a ship and the people encountered aboard it. Personified qualities appear as named figures, and the speaker has to work out what each of them wants. The account of the dream is also an account of uncertainty: apparent friendship, promises of favour and information about rivals cannot simply be taken at their stated value.

The Tunning of Elinour Rumming moves to another setting, concentrating on an ale seller and the women who come to drink. Clothes, bodily appearance, household goods and items offered in payment accumulate in its descriptions. The contrast with the court poem makes it possible to compare the poet’s treatment of social display across very different surroundings, while retaining the distinct form and purpose of each work.

Religious language, mourning and satire

Philip Sparrow begins from grief for a pet bird and uses words and forms associated with religious observance. Its English verse incorporates Latin phrases, names of birds and an extensive display of remembered reading. The shifts in scale, from the small dead creature to the elaborate verbal response, are features of the poem itself; they should not be mistaken for a simple anecdote about its author’s life.

The political and religious satires likewise move between learned expression and reported everyday speech. A complaint voiced within a poem is not automatically Skelton speaking without mediation. Attention to quotation, questions and changes of address helps distinguish the speaker’s position from the claims he reports. The edition’s headnotes and declared extract boundaries provide the setting needed to read these different forms together without treating every piece as complete.
