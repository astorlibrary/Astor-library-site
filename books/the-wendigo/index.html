The hunting party

Five men travel into the Canadian wilderness in search of moose. Among them are Dr Cathcart, his nephew Simpson and the guide Joseph Défago. When the hunters separate into smaller parties, Simpson accompanies Défago into an area where the guide becomes increasingly disturbed by something beyond the visible camp.

During the night Défago disappears. The tracks that Simpson follows become difficult to explain, and the sounds and subsequent appearances reported by the party do not produce a settled account of what has happened. The complete tale follows the search and its aftermath, keeping the hunters’ differing reactions within a narrative of testimony, observation and fear.

First publication and the reading text

The Wendigo first appeared in The Lost Valley and Other Stories, published by Eveleigh Nash in 1910. The edition’s publication account identifies that collection as the first appearance and does not assert an earlier magazine publication. It also traces the story’s later reprinting and reputation as an individual work.

The Astor reading text is set from Project Gutenberg ebook 10897, first released in 2004 and presenting the 1910 story. A copy of the first edition has not been independently collated for this printing. The distinction is stated because the date of the original story and the identity of the electronic text used to typeset it are separate bibliographical facts.

Canada and Blackwood’s travels

The story’s country north of Rat Portage belongs to a real Canadian landscape. Material on geography, hunting, camps and movement through the bush supplies practical background to the expedition. Distances, unfamiliar terrain and dependence on a guide affect what the party can observe and how a search can be conducted.

The biographical material considers Blackwood’s Canadian experience and accounts of his travel. His biographer’s account of an 1898 hunting journey is identified as such, rather than presented as direct proof of the circumstances in which this story was composed. The edition keeps a possible connection between experience and fiction distinct from a demonstrated source.

Windigo traditions and the historical record

The supporting sections distinguish Blackwood’s fictional creature from windigo traditions in Indigenous communities. They give historical context and consider the ways in which such traditions were reported to non-Indigenous readers. A literary story published in London in 1910 is therefore not treated as an authoritative description of a single, uniform belief.

A separate account examines the Fiddler case in Canada, including the proceedings following the arrests of Jack Fiddler and Pesequan in 1907. Those events preceded publication of The Wendigo, but no direct connection between the case and Blackwood’s composition has been documented. The edition preserves that limit instead of turning chronological proximity into a claim of influence.

Description, reception and adaptation

Blackwood does not supply a complete physical portrait of the creature. The story depends on what the men hear, on tracks and on the difficult evidence surrounding Défago. Later antlered or deer-skull images familiar from popular culture should therefore not be read back into the tale as though they were Blackwood’s own description.

The reception material follows the work into later discussion and adaptation. H. P. Lovecraft’s praise is given in relation to what he actually wrote, including his distinction between this story and The Willows. The edition does not convert a critic’s ranking into an invented quotation, or use an unprovable claim of uninterrupted publication as a substitute for a documented account of reprinting.

How the supporting material is arranged

Summaries record the development of the expedition and the events surrounding Défago’s disappearance. Contextual sections then address Blackwood, the Canadian bush, historical sources, windigo traditions and the story’s later reputation. This division allows readers to check the action before following a particular historical question into the supporting material.

The edition keeps statements of evidence explicit. The electronic source of the reading text is identified; a first-edition collation is not claimed; a documented historical case is not declared the story’s source; and modern creature imagery is distinguished from the written account. These details make the volume useful for readers comparing supernatural fiction with the historical and geographical material from which later accounts of it have been constructed.
