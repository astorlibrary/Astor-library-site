About the work

Fifteen poems from the Cuala Press.

The account below follows the collection from its 1920 printing to publication in 1921, and records the readings Yeats later altered.

The work

What happens.

Printed by the Cuala Press in 1920 and published in 1921, Michael Robartes and the Dancer contains fifteen poems from a decisive period in Yeats’s work.

The collection includes Easter, 1916, The Second Coming, The Rose Tree, On a Political Prisoner and A Prayer for My Daughter.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

The original Cuala edition was limited to four hundred copies. The National Library of Ireland confirms both the size of the printing and the All Souls’ Day 1920 colophon.

This edition follows the Cuala text without modernising spelling or punctuation. Yeats’s Preface and two Notes are preserved, while a table records significant readings that he later changed.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

The sequence beyond its best-known poems

The book opens with the dialogue Michael Robartes and the Dancer, followed by Solomon and the Witch, An Image from a Past Life and Under Saturn. A concentrated group of political poems follows: Easter, 1916, Sixteen Dead Men, The Rose Tree, On a Political Prisoner and The Leaders of the Crowd. Towards Break of Day and Demon and Beast precede The Second Coming. The closing three poems are A Prayer for My Daughter, A Meditation in Time of War and To be Carved on a Stone at Bally-Lee, the title used in the Cuala printing.

This order matters when reading the volume as a collection. Dialogues about love and knowledge stand beside arguments about political commitment; the final inscription names Yeats, his wife George, the materials used to repair their tower and the local forge. The book ends with a particular building and a wish for its inscription to survive the building’s eventual ruin.

Political remembrance and its qualifications

Easter, 1916 records a change in Yeats’s judgement of people he had previously encountered in ordinary social life. Its final stanza names MacDonagh, MacBride, Connolly and Pearse, but remembrance does not settle the poem’s questions about sacrifice, prolonged commitment or the necessity of the deaths. The movement between acknowledgement and doubt is present in the wording of the poem itself.

The neighbouring poems approach political death through different forms. Sixteen Dead Men addresses an unnamed opponent in a short argument about the dead and the living. The Rose Tree gives Pearse and Connolly a dialogue about a tree deprived of water. Keeping these poems together makes it possible to compare the claims made by individual speakers with the more openly questioning account in Easter, 1916.

A book made by the Cuala Press

Cuala belonged to the printing and craft enterprise established by the Yeats sisters after their separation from Dun Emer in 1908. Elizabeth Corbet Yeats managed the press, while Susan Mary, known as Lily, directed the embroidery work. The publication therefore belongs to the history of Irish women’s printing and publishing as well as to W. B. Yeats’s bibliography.

Yeats’s own Preface acknowledges that his philosophical references may need explanation. It introduces the purported exposition of Geraldus by Michael Robartes and directs readers towards two notes. Those notes form part of the original book’s presentation of the poems; they are distinct from the later Astor annotations. The Preface also describes the time required to set, print and dry a hand-printed book, providing a contemporary account of the production process behind this small collection.
