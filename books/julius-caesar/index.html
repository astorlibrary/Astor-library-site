Conspiracy, assassination and civil war

Julius Caesar opens in Rome after Caesar’s victory over Pompey’s sons. Public celebration is interrupted by officials who challenge the crowd’s shifting loyalties. Cassius then approaches Brutus, seeking support for a conspiracy against Caesar. Brutus agrees to participate because he fears what Caesar might become if given still greater power.

The assassination takes place in the third act, leaving two acts to examine its consequences. Brutus allows Antony to address the funeral crowd after speaking himself. Antony’s speech changes the public response, and the action moves towards the opposing military camps of Brutus and Cassius, and Antony and Octavius. The battles at Philippi end the conspirators’ campaign.

The first printed text

Julius Caesar was first printed in the 1623 First Folio. No earlier quarto printing is known. The Folio is consequently the principal early printed witness behind subsequent editions, although modern editors make decisions about punctuation, spelling, stage directions and particular readings.

The play is usually dated to 1599 and associated with the early Globe theatre. The surviving record of a Roman tragedy seen by Thomas Platter in London that September is commonly connected with Julius Caesar. The date of first performance and the date of first printing should be kept distinct: the text reached print more than two decades after its probable first staging.

Plutarch and Roman history

Shakespeare drew on Thomas North’s English translation of Plutarch’s Lives, published in 1579, particularly the lives of Caesar, Brutus and Antony. The source supplied incidents, character descriptions and speeches that the play reshaped for performance. Plutarch himself wrote long after the late Roman Republic, so neither source nor play is a contemporary record of Caesar’s death.

The historical assassination took place on the Ides of March, 44 BCE. The play condenses events and brings political argument into encounters between a relatively small number of speakers. Its dramatic chronology allows the public funeral, personal quarrels and military decisions to follow one another closely. Reading it beside its sources helps identify what Shakespeare selects and rearranges.

Speeches and the public audience

Brutus explains his action to the citizens in prose, asking them to assess his motives and his concern for Rome. Antony speaks after him, working within the permission he has been given while drawing attention to Caesar’s wounds, his will and the conspirators. The crowd’s responses are written into the scene, so the speeches can be studied as exchanges with an audience.

The citizens are also present at the beginning of the play and in the killing of Cinna the poet. That episode makes a mistaken identification a matter of life and death: the poet’s name is confused with a conspirator’s. It gives a concrete consequence to the disorder that follows the funeral, beyond the claims made by either political leader.

Private scenes and supporting characters

Portia questions Brutus about his changed behaviour and asks to share knowledge of his affairs. Calpurnia urges Caesar to remain at home after a disturbing dream. In each case a private conversation precedes a public decision, and the reasons offered within the household are later challenged or displaced.

The quarrel between Brutus and Cassius places their alliance under pressure after the assassination. They dispute money, accusations of corruption and military policy. These scenes provide evidence for comparing the principles characters announce with the decisions they make under practical strain. The commentary and selected quotations in the Astor edition support that comparison scene by scene.

Reading this edition

The scene recaps keep the sequence of events clear, while the contextual material explains Roman names, institutions and circumstances relevant to the action. Key quotations and the discussion of themes are starting points for close reading.

For study, it is useful to distinguish Caesar the historical figure, the Caesar presented by other characters and the man who appears on stage. Similar care is needed with Brutus: Cassius’s recruitment, Brutus’s private reasoning and Antony’s later account represent different perspectives within the play. The Astor supporting material allows those perspectives to be compared against the text.
