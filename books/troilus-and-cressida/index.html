Problem play / tragedy

Troilus and Cressida, William Shakespeare

This Astor Library Edition presents the complete play with scene summaries, line numbering and explanatory notes. The guidance distinguishes the Trojan and Greek plots, follows the rapid movement between the city and the military camp, and identifies the mythological and historical names that Shakespeare expects his audience to recognise.

The contextual material explains how Shakespeare brought together two different inheritances: the celebrated warriors of the Trojan War and the medieval love story of Troilus and Cressida. Attention is also given to the difficult publication history of the 1609 quarto, the play’s unusual position in the 1623 First Folio and the continuing argument over whether it should be approached as comedy, history, tragedy or satire.

Close-reading sections examine Cressida’s constrained choices, Pandarus’s mediation, Ulysses’s political rhetoric, Hector’s divided judgement and the destruction of heroic reputation. The aim is not to force the play into a tidier genre than it possesses, but to help readers see how thoroughly its language of value is challenged by what happens on stage.

Plot and structure

Five stages in the action.

These sections summarise the main developments in the plot and identify changes in power, knowledge and dramatic conflict.

01 · Movement one

Love inside a stalled war

Troilus withdraws from battle because he is consumed by love for Cressida, and Pandarus promises to bring them together. In the Greek camp, Agamemnon’s commanders confront military drift and Achilles’s refusal to fight. Ulysses proposes using Ajax as Hector’s opponent in order to provoke Achilles through wounded pride.

02 · Movement two

A night of vows

Pandarus arranges a private meeting between Troilus and Cressida. Their declaration of constancy is intense but shadowed by anxious forecasts of betrayal: each imagines that their names may become future examples of falseness. The lovers spend the night together while the machinery of war prepares to separate them.

03 · Movement three

Cressida exchanged

Cressida’s father Calchas, who has defected to the Greeks, asks that his daughter be exchanged for the Trojan prisoner Antenor. Troy agrees without consulting her. Troilus can promise secrecy and future visits but cannot prevent the transfer. At the Greek camp, the commanders greet Cressida with a public sequence of kisses before Diomedes takes charge of her.

04 · Movement four

The show of honour

Hector and Ajax fight without a decisive result, after which enemies feast together and inspect one another’s reputations. Troilus enters the camp with Ulysses and watches, unseen, as Cressida gives Diomedes a token that Troilus recognises. The scene reaches the audience through several competing observers, making certainty about her motives difficult.

05 · Movement five

Heroism stripped bare

The death of Patroclus brings Achilles back into battle. Hector kills Patroclus, but later sets aside his armour while pursuing a richly dressed opponent. Achilles’s Myrmidons surround and kill the unarmed Hector, denying him the single combat on which heroic fame depends. Troilus promises revenge, while the diseased Pandarus closes the play by bequeathing his afflictions to the audience.

Critical reading

Three analyses of language and form.

Each analysis uses evidence from specific scenes, speeches and dramatic choices.

Close reading

Cressida watched and judged

Cressida is often described by other characters before she can define herself. Pandarus advertises her, Troilus idealises her, the Trojan council exchanges her, and the Greek commanders test her in public. Even the scene most often taken as proof of her betrayal is watched and interpreted by Troilus, Ulysses and Thersites from concealment.

None of this erases her promise to Troilus or the pain caused by its breaking, but it changes the conditions under which judgement is made. Her wit is partly defence, and her rapid accommodation to the Greek camp may be read as appetite, fear or the work of survival. The scene refuses to grant the audience a private access to her mind.

Close reading

Ulysses and the uses of order

Ulysses’s speech on ‘degree’ offers an imposing vision of a universe held together by hierarchy. Removed from its setting, it can sound like the play’s settled political doctrine. Within the scene, however, it is also a diagnosis delivered by a strategist who wants permission to manipulate Ajax and Achilles.

The gap between grand principle and tactical behaviour matters. The Greeks praise order while conducting a campaign marked by rivalry, vanity and deception. Ulysses sees more clearly than many around him, but his intelligence does not restore the values his speech celebrates. It simply makes him a more effective operator within the disorder.

Close reading

The undoing of heroic language

Both armies possess famous names, yet the play repeatedly places reputation beside bodily appetite, commercial exchange and disease. Thersites describes the war as lust dressed up as honour, while Helen herself becomes a disputed object whose value is measured against the lives already lost.

Hector understands that keeping Helen cannot be morally defended, but he supports the war after appealing to honour. Achilles secures fame through an ambush rather than the fair fight associated with epic heroism. The play does not merely replace noble heroes with villains; it shows intelligent people continuing to serve standards that their own actions have emptied out.

Text, sources and performance

Publication and performance context.

This section covers the play's date, sources, early printed texts and relevant performance history.

Sources

Homeric war and Chaucerian love

Shakespeare does not simply dramatise the Iliad. He draws the lovers from Chaucer’s Troilus and Criseyde and takes much of the war narrative from later histories of Troy, including material available through Caxton and Lydgate. By joining traditions that had treated their subjects with great seriousness, he creates a drama in which inherited fame is continually subjected to doubt.

Printing

A book with conflicting signals

The 1609 quarto exists with different preliminary matter. One title page associates the play with performance at the Globe, while an address to the reader in some copies presents it as a work untouched by the ordinary stage. The First Folio prints it among the tragedies, but its position and absence from the preliminary catalogue add to its unsettled textual history.

Genre

Comedy without release, tragedy without closure

The play has been called a problem play, satire, comedy, tragedy and history. It contains comic mediation and sexual intrigue, but no marriage or restored community; it includes the death of Hector, but does not end with Troy’s fall or Troilus’s death. Its resistance to a satisfying finish is part of its judgement on a culture that keeps talking after its values have failed.

Characters

Principal characters and dramatic functions.

Troilus

A young Trojan prince whose idealised love for Cressida gives way to anguish and martial fury. His sincerity is real, but his vision of Cressida often leaves little room for her circumstances or independent self.

Cressida

Calchas’s daughter, courted by Troilus and then exchanged into the Greek camp. Her intelligence and guarded speech operate within a world in which powerful men repeatedly decide where she will go and what she signifies.

Pandarus

Cressida’s uncle and the tireless intermediary between the lovers. Comic, intrusive and increasingly unwell, he turns private desire into social transaction and leaves the audience with the play’s bitter final address.

Hector

Troy’s foremost warrior. He recognises the weakness of the case for retaining Helen yet submits his judgement to honour, and his pursuit of a prize on the battlefield helps expose him to Achilles’s fatal ambush.

Ulysses

A Greek commander and acute reader of political behaviour. He diagnoses the army’s fractured hierarchy and uses theatrical schemes, controlled information and provocation to manage those around him.

Thersites

A corrosive commentator who attacks Greek and Trojan claims to heroism. His language of disease and appetite often identifies truths others conceal, though his contempt is so general that it offers no principle for rebuilding what he destroys.

Study questions

Questions for analysis and discussion.

Each question requires evidence from the text and can be used for discussion, notes or essay planning.

01

How free is Cressida?

Map every decision made about Cressida’s movements and relationships, then compare those pressures with the promises she makes. How does the concealed observation of her encounter with Diomedes shape the judgement invited from an audience?

02

Should Ulysses be believed?

Read the speech on degree alongside the plot he constructs against Achilles. Does his behaviour support his account of necessary order, expose it as useful rhetoric, or reveal a more complicated relation between principle and power?

03

Why does the play end with Pandarus?

Consider what would change if the drama ended with Hector’s death or Troilus’s vow of revenge. What does Pandarus’s address do to the boundary between Troy and the theatre, and why are inheritance and infection its final images?
