About the work

The poems of 1798 and 1800 in their first forms.

The account below sets out the selection from both early editions, from the Ancyent Marinere and Tintern Abbey to the Lucy poems and Michael, each printed as it first entered Lyrical Ballads.

The work

What happens.

First published anonymously in 1798, Lyrical Ballads brought Wordsworth and Coleridge together at a decisive moment in English Romantic poetry.

The selection includes Coleridge’s original 1798 Rime of the Ancyent Marinere alongside Wordsworth’s We Are Seven, The Thorn, The Idiot Boy and Tintern Abbey.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

The Lucy poems, Nutting, The Old Cumberland Beggar and Michael represent the enlarged 1800 edition. Each work appears in the form in which it first entered Lyrical Ballads.

Original notes and summaries trace unfamiliar words, people, books, landscapes and events, while supporting essays explain the collaboration, publication, reception and later history of the poems.

Text and context

Early printings kept, later revisions set aside.

Each poem keeps the spelling, punctuation and capitalisation of its 1798 or 1800 text, and the apparatus is plain that this is a thirty-six-poem selection rather than the complete contents.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

What changed between the early books

The short Advertisement to the 1798 volume describes most of its poems as experiments in the use of conversational language. It asks readers to judge their treatment of people, incidents and feeling without relying entirely on established expectations about poetic style. This is a different document from the substantial Preface associated with the two-volume edition of 1800.

The physical history of the first edition also records a late change. The National Library of New Zealand’s copy identifies replacement leaves through which Coleridge’s Lewti was removed and The Nightingale inserted, with a corrected contents leaf. Such evidence shows that the contents and appearance of the book were decisions made during publication. The Astor selection should be read according to its stated 1798 and 1800 sources, rather than treated as a later collected edition of either poet.

Speakers who disagree about an event

In We Are Seven, an adult questioner and a child cannot agree on how to count a family that includes two dead children. The disagreement persists to the final stanza. The poem supplies the child’s account of visiting the graves and the adult’s repeated arithmetic, allowing the exchange itself to carry the argument.

The Thorn presents a different problem of testimony. Its speaker repeatedly describes a tree, a mound and Martha Ray, but relies on local reports when explaining what may have happened to her child. Statements of certainty coexist with admissions of ignorance. These poems give readers practical reasons to ask who is speaking, what that speaker has seen and which conclusions belong to other people.

The early Mariner and the rural poems

The 1798 Ancyent Marinere begins the original volume with a wedding guest detained by a stranger’s story. Its archaic spelling and supernatural voyage differ markedly from the ordinary conversations elsewhere in the book. It also predates the marginal prose gloss Coleridge added in 1817. Reading the early poem keeps those two stages of its textual history separate.

The 1800 volume extends the range of rural narratives. In Michael, a shepherd’s financial obligation leads him to send his son Luke away, while an unfinished sheepfold preserves a visible trace of their agreement. Nutting recounts the speaker’s destruction of a woodland enclosure and his later response to it. These works place work, property, memory and responsibility within particular actions and places. They reward attention to the circumstances of each poem rather than a single general description of Romantic writing as the appreciation of scenery.
