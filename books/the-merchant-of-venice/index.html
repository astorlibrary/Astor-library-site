William Shakespeare

The Merchant of Venice

A loan, a marriage test and an elopement meet in a trial whose celebrated rescue is also an act of dispossession. The play can be funny, intimate and formally comic, but no responsible reading can ignore the antisemitism voiced by its Christians or the forced conversion imposed upon Shylock.

Comedy

The Merchant of Venice, William Shakespeare

Bassanio wants to court the wealthy Portia but needs money to present himself as a suitable suitor. His friend Antonio agrees to help, although his capital is tied up in ships at sea. Antonio therefore borrows three thousand ducats from Shylock, a Jewish moneylender whom he has repeatedly insulted. If the debt is not repaid within three months, Shylock's bond allows him to claim a pound of Antonio's flesh.

At Belmont, Portia's suitors must choose among gold, silver and lead caskets. Bassanio chooses correctly and marries her, while Shylock's daughter Jessica leaves her father's house with Lorenzo. When Antonio's ships are reported lost, the action converges upon a Venetian court. Portia enters disguised as a lawyer, defeats Shylock through an exacting reading of the bond and then uses another law to place his life and property at the court's mercy. The victory ends with confiscation and compulsory conversion.

This Astor Library Edition presents the complete play with scene summaries, line numbering and explanatory notes. It follows the language of bonds across money, friendship, marriage and law; gives the casket and trial plots their source history; and addresses the play's antisemitism directly, without flattening Shylock into either a modern hero or the inhuman stereotype his enemies want him to be.

The shape of the play

How the action moves

Five changes that hold the plot together. Each one alters who possesses power, knowledge or the right to tell the story.

01 · Movement I

Antonio enters the bond

Antonio cannot explain his melancholy, while Bassanio admits that he needs further money to pursue Portia. Shylock agrees to lend three thousand ducats without interest but proposes the pound of flesh as a supposedly playful forfeit. Antonio accepts because he expects his ships to return well before the bond falls due.

02 · Movement II

Caskets and departures

The Prince of Morocco chooses gold and the Prince of Aragon chooses silver; both are defeated by the values they bring to the test. In Venice, Jessica leaves Shylock's house disguised as a boy and elopes with Lorenzo, taking money and jewels. Shylock's private loss is immediately made into public entertainment by the Christian characters.

03 · Movement III

Bassanio wins Portia as Antonio loses time

Bassanio rejects the ornament of gold and silver, chooses the lead casket and finds Portia's portrait. Their celebration is interrupted by news that Antonio's ventures have failed and Shylock intends to enforce the bond. Portia sends Bassanio to Venice with far more than the sum owed, while she and Nerissa prepare their own journey in disguise.

04 · Movement IV

The trial turns

Shylock refuses repayment and demands the legal penalty to which Antonio agreed. Portia, appearing as the learned Balthazar, appeals to mercy before ruling that the bond permits flesh but no blood. She then invokes a law against an alien who seeks a citizen's life. Shylock is stripped of control over his wealth and ordered to become a Christian.

05 · Movement V

Rings and returns

Portia and Nerissa, still disguised, persuade Bassanio and Gratiano to surrender the rings they had promised never to lose. At Belmont they use the missing rings to test their husbands before revealing the deception. Antonio learns that three of his ships have safely arrived, but the apparent comedy of restoration leaves Shylock absent and Antonio outside the final pairings.

Read closely

Three ways into the writing

These are not themes to collect. They are patterns to follow through particular scenes, voices and choices.

Act 1, Scene 3

The making of the bond

The loan scene begins as negotiation but repeatedly returns to memory. Shylock recalls Antonio calling him “misbeliever” and “cut-throat dog”, spitting upon him and attacking his business. Antonio does not deny the abuse; he says he is likely to repeat it. The pound of flesh therefore emerges from an existing history of humiliation, even while Shylock presents it as a “merry bond”.

No one in the scene reads risk impartially. Antonio's confidence in his ships makes the forfeit seem impossible, Bassanio senses danger without fully refusing its benefit, and Shylock uses the language of friendship to frame a penalty that is anything but friendly. The scene establishes the play's central habit: affection, hatred and money share the same vocabulary, so a bond can mean legal contract, bodily danger and personal attachment at once.

Act 3, Scene 1

“Hath not a Jew eyes?”

Shylock begins from shared embodiment: Jews and Christians have the same senses, wounds, illnesses and vulnerability to weather and weapons. The repeated questions make denial sound absurd. Yet the speech does not stop at a plea for recognition. It turns Christian revenge into the model Shylock says he will follow, binding the demand for common humanity to his determination to pursue Antonio.

That turn should not be used to cancel the speech's force. Shylock has learned vengeance from people who claim moral and religious superiority while abusing him. At the same time, suffering does not make every choice he makes just. The scene is strongest when both facts remain present: the culture has helped produce the violence it condemns, and Shylock is still answerable for choosing to reproduce it.

Act 4, Scene 1

Mercy preached and withheld

Portia's “quality of mercy” speech imagines mercy as voluntary, reciprocal and greater than royal power. Shylock is unmoved because the court has given him no reason to trust a Christian appeal that asks him alone to surrender a lawful claim. Portia then saves Antonio not by transcending law but by reading it even more literally than Shylock: the contract grants flesh, she argues, but not blood.

The reversal tests Portia's own principle. Once Shylock is defeated, the court threatens his life, divides his estate and accepts Antonio's demand that he convert. Mercy is offered only after resistance has become impossible. A close reading should therefore follow the distance between Portia's radiant account of mercy and the coercive settlement enacted in its name. The contradiction belongs to the scene, not merely to modern discomfort with it.

Book, theatre, history

The worlds around the play

Dating, source and performance matter most when they return us to something the text is doing.

England

Antisemitism without an openly recognised Jewish community

England's formal expulsion of Jews in 1290 remained in force during Shakespeare's lifetime, although a small number lived in the country under concealment or qualified identities. Most playgoers knew Jewish people through Christian teaching, inherited caricature, travel writing and the stage. The execution of the Portuguese physician Roderigo Lopez in 1594 and the continuing popularity of Marlowe's The Jew of Malta formed part of the play's hostile environment. This context explains the prejudice; it does not excuse or neutralise it.

Venice

Credit beneath the language of friendship

The commercial world of the play depends upon risk, loans, maritime ventures and delayed returns. Antonio condemns lending at interest, but his own trade and Bassanio's courtship both rely upon credit. Belmont appears to offer release from calculation, yet Portia is inherited wealth, her suitors are tested through precious metals and marriage is repeatedly discussed in the language of value. Venice and Belmont are not clean opposites: money structures both worlds, even when one calls the transaction love.

Performance

The changing weight of Shylock

Shylock was written within a theatrical tradition that expected an audience to laugh at and fear a Jewish moneylender. Later performers altered that inheritance: Edmund Kean's 1814 portrayal became famous for giving Shylock tragic dignity, while twentieth- and twenty-first-century productions have had to stage the play after the Holocaust and amid continuing antisemitism. A serious production cannot solve the work by making every Christian monstrous or Shylock blameless. It must show how individual complexity operates inside a plot that finally expels and converts him.

Keep in view

People carrying the argument

Portia

Portia is wealthy and verbally brilliant but initially bound by her father's casket test. Once Bassanio succeeds, she becomes the play's most effective actor, directing money, disguise and law. Her intelligence saves Antonio, yet the trial also reveals how quickly her power can move from mercy to domination.

Shylock

Shylock is a moneylender, a father, an injured outsider and an attempted killer. He remembers abuse that Antonio treats as ordinary and speaks the play's clearest account of shared human feeling. His insistence on the bond is both personally chosen and socially produced; neither half of that truth should erase the other.

Antonio

Antonio's unexplained sadness hangs over the opening and returns in his isolation at Belmont. His devotion to Bassanio is financial, bodily and possibly erotic, but it coexists with open hatred of Shylock. He is willing to die for one man while showing remarkably little mercy to another.

Bassanio

Bassanio is affectionate, persuasive and accustomed to living through Antonio's generosity. He reads the caskets well, distrusting ornament, though his journey to Belmont began as a plan to repair his finances through marriage. The ring plot tests whether he can honour Portia without treating Antonio's claim upon him as absolute.

Jessica

Jessica escapes a restrictive home, converts and marries Lorenzo, but the play never settles the cost of that freedom. She takes Shylock's money and jewels, including a turquoise associated with her dead mother Leah. At Belmont she gains a Christian household while remaining marked by other characters as Shylock's daughter.

Nerissa

Nerissa is Portia's companion, confidante and partner in disguise. She reads Portia's suitors shrewdly and creates a parallel ring test for Gratiano. Her presence stops Portia's plot from appearing solitary: the women plan, travel, perform legal identities and control the final disclosures together.

Carry through the text

Questions that stay open

Use them to test a scene, a performance choice or an argument of your own.

01

What does the play allow Shylock to say that its ending refuses him?

Compare the loan scene, the report of Jessica's flight, the “Hath not a Jew eyes?” speech and the trial. Attend to moments when Shylock is permitted interiority, memory and grief, then examine how the final judgement removes his property, religion and voice. This prevents either his sympathetic speeches or his violent demand from being read in isolation.

02

Does Portia practise the mercy she describes?

Place the mercy speech beside every stage of Shylock's defeat. Ask when mercy is voluntary, who is allowed to request it and what choices remain once it is granted. Portia's legal brilliance is real, but the scene's ethical difficulty lies in the difference between saving Antonio and deciding what may then be done to Shylock.

03

What kind of bond joins Antonio and Bassanio?

Follow their language of sacrifice, debt and love from the opening loan to the ring scene. Portia's marriage does not simply replace Antonio's claim; the last act brings the claims into competition. Antonio once risks his flesh for Bassanio and later pledges his soul as security for Bassanio's fidelity, making emotional loyalty sound repeatedly like a dangerous contract.
