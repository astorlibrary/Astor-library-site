About the work

Two family tragedies and two early texts.

The account below follows Lear’s division of Britain and Gloucester’s deception into storm and civil war, and explains how the 1608 Quarto and 1623 Folio differ.

The work

What happens.

Lear divides Britain according to public declarations of love. Goneril and Regan provide the performance he expects; Cordelia refuses and is disinherited. Having surrendered territory and authority, Lear attempts to retain the title, household and obedience of a king.

Gloucester is deceived by Edmund into rejecting Edgar. The two family tragedies meet in the storm, civil war, the blinding and madness, and the play puts authority, inheritance, nature and justice through all of them.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

The first quarto

The 1608 quarto presents problems within the printing itself. Corrections were made while the sheets were being produced, so surviving copies can preserve different readings on what appears to be the same page. Verse is sometimes printed as prose, and some prose is divided into verse lines. Comparison therefore involves the state of a particular copy as well as the broad distinction between quarto and Folio.

A second quarto was printed in 1619 but retained a 1608 date on its title page. It largely reprints the first quarto, introducing corrections and further errors. The false date was identified by later bibliographical research. References to Q1 and Q2 consequently distinguish two printings that cannot be dated reliably from their title pages alone.

The Folio’s missing scene

The Folio omits the whole scene commonly numbered 4.3 in conflated editions. In that scene, a Gentleman reports Cordelia’s response to news of her father. Its inclusion gives the reader an account of her feelings before Lear and Cordelia meet again. The passage is a concrete example of why a conflated text has a different sequence of information from either early version read separately.

Shakespeare also changed the inherited story. Holinshed’s account, drawing on Geoffrey of Monmouth, allows the youngest daughter to help restore her father to power. The separate Gloucester plot draws on a story in Sidney’s Arcadia involving a father deceived and blinded through the actions of an illegitimate son. These sources help identify what Shakespeare combined, retained and altered; they do not make the resulting tragedy a report of historical events.

Dover

Gloucester asks Edgar to guide him to the edge of a cliff. Edgar describes a great height while keeping his father on level ground, then changes his voice and pretends to encounter a man who has survived a fall. The audience can watch the discrepancy between Gloucester’s belief and the action being performed.

This scene offers a practical way to use the edition’s attention to blindness, language and performance. Establish what the stage shows, what Edgar describes and what Gloucester can perceive. Then follow the changes in Edgar’s assumed identity and his explanation of the supposed rescue. The passage’s effects depend on how speech supplies a landscape that the actors do not physically occupy, and on the different knowledge held by father, son and audience.
