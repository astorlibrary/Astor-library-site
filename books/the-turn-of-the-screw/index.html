Henry James

The Turn of the Screw

A young governess takes charge of two orphaned children, Miles and Flora, at a country house called Bly, on condition that she never troubles their uncle in London. She begins to see a man and a woman about the grounds, and learns that the last valet and the last governess are both dead. Henry James published the story in 1898, and readers still disagree about whether the ghosts are there.

The story told at Bly

A young governess accepts employment at a country house called Bly. Her employer, the uncle of Miles and Flora, lives in London and requires her to manage the children without troubling him. At the house she works with Mrs Grose, the housekeeper. Miles returns from school after being expelled, but the reason for his dismissal is initially withheld.

The governess sees a man whom Mrs Grose identifies from her description as Peter Quint, a former servant who is now dead. She also reports seeing Miss Jessel, her predecessor. She becomes convinced that these figures are seeking access to the children. The account follows her attempts to watch, interpret and intervene, with the children’s safety increasingly bound up with her certainty about what she has seen.

The frame and the manuscript

Before the events at Bly begin, a group of guests exchange ghost stories at a Christmas gathering. Douglas promises to read a manuscript written by a woman he knew: the governess. A separate narrator reports the gathering and transmits her account. These layers of narration belong to James’s fiction and are part of the complete text.

The governess therefore speaks retrospectively, describing both events and her earlier thoughts about them. Mrs Grose supplies information about the household, but readers receive her responses through the governess’s record. Keeping these different kinds of evidence distinct is useful when following the plot: an event described by the narrator, a report from another character and a conclusion drawn from either need not carry the same weight.

First publication and later revision

The Turn of the Screw appeared in 1898, first as a serial in Collier’s Weekly and then in the volume The Two Magics, where it was paired with Covering End. Its publication in a periodical preceded its independent life as a frequently reprinted novella.

James returned to the story when preparing his collected New York Edition, published in 1908 for this volume. The later presentation includes an authorial preface and revisions. Those publication stages are useful reference points when comparing quotations across editions. Henry James and the governess’s position

Henry James was born in New York in 1843 and spent much of his adult life in Europe, settling in England. His fiction includes novels, novellas and short stories, often concerned with what people know about one another and how they acquire that knowledge. The Turn of the Screw gives those questions a particularly restricted setting: one house, a small household and a narrator charged with responsibility.

The governess has authority over the children while remaining an employee. Her employer’s prohibition on contacting him removes an obvious source of advice. Mrs Grose knows the household’s history, yet the two women occupy different positions within it. These arrangements are stated in the narrative and help explain how information is exchanged, withheld or interpreted at Bly.
