Thomas Lord Cromwell

Thomas Cromwell, a blacksmith’s son from Putney, travels through Europe, serves Cardinal Wolsey, rises to be Henry VIII’s chief minister and is executed in 1540. The play was printed in 1602 as by ‘W. S.’, which later got it into the Shakespeare apocrypha.

About the work

From a blacksmith’s house to condemnation without trial.

The account below follows the play’s Cromwell through Europe and the court, and marks where the drama departs from the historical record.

The work

What happens.

Young Cromwell will not accept the limits of his birth, and goes after learning, travel and advancement. Service with English merchants at Antwerp begins a European journey that eventually leads through Wolsey’s household into royal government.

As his influence grows, Cromwell repays earlier generosity: Friskiball, an innkeeper and the Banister family receive help in return for help once given. Debts and gifts become the play’s measure of character.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

Gardiner arranges false testimony, and Cromwell is condemned without trial. The royal reprieve arrives too late. The play changes offices, leaves things out and alters events to make Cromwell’s career a model rise and fall.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

Learning, work and the European journey

The opening gives Cromwell’s ambition a practical setting. The hammering in his father’s smithy interrupts his reading, and the workmen answer his complaints with jokes about what learning is worth. The opposition is between two activities within the same household: the trade that supports him and the books through which he hopes to change his position. Hodge supplies a comic view of the young scholar’s aspirations.

The subsequent movement between England and continental Europe brings the play into the world of merchants, travellers, bills and personal introductions. Advancement depends on acquiring useful knowledge and on finding people willing to offer employment, credit or protection. These early episodes matter later, when the successful Cromwell has the means to repay obligations incurred before he possessed office or influence.

Merchants, creditors and the Banister plot

The secondary story of Banister gives debt two sharply different human agents. Bagot uses the ruined merchant’s liabilities as an opportunity for profit, whereas Friskiball responds to hardship with assistance. Their actions supply concrete instances of the difference between having a legal claim on another person and choosing how to exercise it. The movement of the Banister family between London and Antwerp connects this subplot to Cromwell’s travels.

Friskiball’s later reversal of fortune allows the same relationship to be tested from the opposite direction. Cromwell can then demonstrate whether prosperity has altered his memory of assistance received. Following these encounters in sequence makes the recurring gifts and repayments easier to understand than treating them as incidental interruptions to the political biography.

A minister’s history adapted for the theatre

The historical Thomas Cromwell served Henry VIII as a royal minister and played a central part in the religious and administrative changes of the 1530s. His execution took place on Tower Hill on 28 July 1540. The play organises that career around selected personal encounters and a conspiracy, giving a much simpler explanation of his fall than the political record supports.

In the final movement, statements attributed to Cromwell become evidence against him, while expressions of generosity to his servants are interpreted as signs of a guilty conscience. The audience has already seen conduct that can be read differently. So the handling of testimony is central to the ending: we watch the case against Buckingham being put together.
