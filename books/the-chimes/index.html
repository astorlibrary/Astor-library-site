Charles Dickens

The Chimes

Trotty Veck, a London ticket-porter, reads in the newspaper that the poor have no right to a New Year, and half believes it. On New Year’s Eve the goblins of the church bells show him what will happen to his daughter and her friends. Dickens wrote it in Genoa in 1844, the year after A Christmas Carol.

About the work

Trotty Veck, the bells and the arguments of 1844.

The account below follows the story from Trotty’s post outside the church to the vision in the bell tower, and records its publication on 16 December 1844 as Dickens’s second Christmas book.

The work

What happens.

Toby “Trotty” Veck is an elderly ticket-porter who waits for work outside a London church. Poor but good-natured, he begins to accept the claim that people like him are born bad.

Mr Filer uses statistics against the poor, Alderman Cute proposes punishment as social policy and Sir Joseph Bowley presents dependence on wealthy patrons as benevolence. By nightfall, Trotty has lost faith in himself and his class.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

The bells call him into the church tower and show a possible future in which those arguments govern ordinary lives. Meg, Richard, Lilian and Will Fern bear the consequences.

First published on 16 December 1844, The Chimes was Dickens’s second Christmas book. Its four divisions—First Quarter through Fourth Quarter—are Dickens’s own.

The surviving manuscript and first readings

The manuscript of The Chimes survives in the Forster Collection at the Victoria and Albert Museum. John Forster, Dickens’s friend and later biographer, inherited manuscripts and other working material; his library and collections reached the museum through his 1876 bequest. The manuscript places the story within a substantial surviving record of Dickens’s composition, proof correction and publication.

Dickens’s letters from Genoa refer to the bells heard during his stay there in 1844. Before publication he returned to London, corrected proofs and read the new work to groups of friends. Daniel Maclise recorded a reading in Forster’s rooms in a pencil drawing. Dickens’s letter to Catherine dated 2 December 1844 reports the powerful response of listeners to readings of the story, providing contemporary evidence of its effect before it reached the general public.

New Year, accounts and hospitality

The story’s full subtitle identifies bells ringing an old year out and a new one in. That calendar setting appears throughout the ordinary business surrounding Trotty’s vision. Shops display goods for the coming year, almanacs calculate its seasons, and Sir Joseph Bowley insists that every financial account should be settled before the year turns.

When Trotty admits owing money for rent and to Mrs Chickenstalker’s shop, Bowley treats the debt as evidence of personal failure. The detail is exact: Trotty owes Mrs Chickenstalker a small grocery bill. Later, Mrs Chickenstalker appears in the final gathering and proves to have a connection with Will Fern’s family. The same shopkeeper therefore belongs both to the world of everyday credit and to the hospitality with which the narrative ends.

The ending also gives the new date a practical consequence: the bells announce that Meg and Richard’s wedding day has arrived.

The story beyond its first readership

The Chimes circulated in forms beyond the ordinary reading copy. The Charles Dickens Museum holds a presentation copy given by Dickens to Hans Christian Andersen. It also preserves sheet music associated with the Christmas books, including ‘The Chimes Quadrille’. Such objects document the story’s movement through personal gifts, domestic music-making and the publishing trade.

The illustrations accompanying the original publication also form part of that history. Maclise’s frontispiece and his drawing of the private reading are different objects: one visualises the book’s supernatural subject, while the other records the author performing his manuscript for friends. These surviving documents help distinguish composition, first publication and later circulation. The Astor edition’s notes and contextual material discuss the literary work itself; the historical objects are evidence of how nineteenth-century readers and listeners encountered it.
