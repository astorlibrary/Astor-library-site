William Shakespeare

The Winter's Tale

A king's groundless jealousy destroys his household; sixteen years later, another generation begins the work of repair. The play travels from trial and bereavement to festivity and wonder, but its recovered family must still live with the people who cannot return.

Late romance

The Winter's Tale, William Shakespeare

Leontes, King of Sicilia, becomes convinced that his pregnant wife Hermione is having an affair with his lifelong friend Polixenes. His certainty arrives without proof and quickly becomes state policy. He orders Camillo to poison Polixenes, imprisons Hermione, rejects the newborn Perdita and puts his wife on trial. Even Apollo's oracle cannot stop him. Only the reported deaths of his son Mamillius and Hermione break the force of his delusion.

Perdita is carried to Bohemia by Antigonus, who is killed after abandoning her. Sixteen years pass. Raised by a shepherd, Perdita falls in love with Polixenes's son Florizel, while the rogue Autolycus steals, sings and trades among the sheep-shearing celebrations. When Polixenes violently opposes the match, the young couple flee to Sicilia. There Perdita's identity is recovered, the two kings are reconciled and Paulina reveals what appears to be a statue of Hermione, which moves and speaks.

This Astor Library Edition presents the complete play with scene summaries, line numbering and explanatory notes. It follows Leontes's language as suspicion hardens into evidence, explains Shakespeare's extensive revision of Robert Greene's Pandosto, and gives the sixteen-year interval, the Bohemian festival and the statue scene the space they need. The result keeps the wonder of the ending without pretending that wonder restores Mamillius, Antigonus or the lost years.

Plot and structure

Five stages in the action.

These sections summarise the main developments in the plot and identify changes in power, knowledge and dramatic conflict.

01 · Movement I

Leontes invents a betrayal

Polixenes agrees to extend his visit after Hermione asks him to remain. Leontes interprets their ordinary warmth as proof of adultery and treats his own agitation as insight. Camillo refuses an order to poison Polixenes and helps him escape to Bohemia, which Leontes reads as further confirmation of a conspiracy.

02 · Movement II

The court cannot stop the king

Hermione gives birth in prison, and Paulina brings the infant to Leontes in the hope of reaching his conscience. He instead commands Antigonus to abandon the child. At Hermione's trial, an oracle declares her innocent and Leontes a jealous tyrant. He rejects it until Mamillius dies; Hermione then collapses and is reported dead.

03 · Movement III

A bear, a shepherd and sixteen years

Antigonus leaves the baby on the Bohemian coast with identifying tokens and gold. He exits pursued by a bear, while the ship that brought him is destroyed in the storm. A shepherd and his son discover Perdita and raise her. Time then appears as Chorus and carries the action forward by sixteen years.

04 · Movement IV

Bohemia in flower

Perdita presides over a sheep-shearing feast and receives the disguised Polixenes and Camillo. Her conversation about flowers, breeding and art reveals a natural authority that rank has not taught her. Polixenes eventually unmasks himself and threatens the Shepherd and Perdita. Camillo helps Florizel and Perdita escape towards Sicilia.

05 · Movement V

Proof, pardon and the statue

The tokens left with Perdita establish her as Leontes's daughter, allowing her to marry Florizel and restoring friendship between Sicilia and Bohemia. Paulina then leads the company to an image of Hermione that seems aged by sixteen years. Music sounds, the figure descends and Hermione embraces Leontes and Perdita. The play closes in astonishment rather than explanation.

Critical reading

Three analyses of language and form.

Each analysis uses evidence from specific scenes, speeches and dramatic choices.

Act 1, Scene 2

“Too hot, too hot!”: jealousy makes its own evidence

Leontes's jealousy has no Iago and no prior disclosure to make it reasonable. It begins as a break in perception. Hermione and Polixenes touch hands, speak warmly and remember childhood; Leontes converts each detail into sexual proof. His language fragments into questions, repetitions, obscene imaginings and private asides. The mind does not move from evidence to conclusion. It reaches the conclusion first and then makes the visible world serve it.

This suddenness is the point rather than a missing motivation that an actor must repair. Leontes experiences interpretation as certainty and royal power prevents anyone else from correcting him. Camillo's disbelief, Hermione's openness and Polixenes's ordinary conduct all become suspicious because they contradict the story already formed. The scene shows tyranny at its most intimate: a private fantasy acquires guards, prisons, a trial and the power to separate a newborn child from her mother.

Act 4, Scene 4

Perdita and Polixenes argue about nature

Perdita refuses to plant streaked carnations and gillyflowers because human art has altered them. The disguised Polixenes answers that grafting is itself an art authorised by nature: cultivation can improve what nature produces. Their exchange is gracious, precise and apparently philosophical. It also quietly concerns Perdita herself, a royal child cultivated in a shepherd's household and judged by others according to uncertain ideas of birth.

Polixenes soon destroys the calm authority of his own argument. Once Florizel announces his intention to marry Perdita, the king treats inherited rank as fixed and threatens violence against the young woman he has just praised. His defence of mixture in flowers does not survive mixture in the social order. Perdita, meanwhile, retains the dignity he loses. The scene lets pastoral beauty expose, rather than escape, the coercion of the court.

Act 5, Scene 3

The statue moves

Paulina controls the statue scene through timing, instruction and music. She warns the visitors not to touch, draws attention to the figure's wrinkles and asks them to awaken faith. Leontes studies the image until art and memory become difficult to distinguish. When Hermione moves, the language does not settle whether the event is supernatural animation or the end of a sixteen-year concealment arranged by Paulina.

The uncertainty protects the scene's emotional truth. Hermione may be restored, but Mamillius remains dead, Antigonus remains lost and Hermione's long silence cannot be recovered as though it never happened. She speaks first to Perdita, not to Leontes. The reunion can include forgiveness without making suffering useful or necessary. Its wonder lies in the possibility of renewed life after damage, not in an erasure of the damage itself.

Text, sources and performance

Publication and performance context.

This section covers the play's date, sources, early printed texts and relevant performance history.

Source

What Shakespeare changes in Pandosto

Robert Greene's Pandosto provides the jealous king, accused queen, abandoned daughter and cross-class courtship. Shakespeare makes the jealousy more abrupt and less excusable, creates Paulina, Antigonus and Autolycus, and reverses the direction of the kingdoms. Most importantly, Greene's queen truly dies and the jealous king later kills himself after desiring his unrecognised daughter. Shakespeare replaces that ending with recognition and apparent restoration, but keeps enough death and lost time to prevent the romance from becoming painless.

Form

Why Time breaks the play in two

The first three acts move with the pressure of tragedy; the fourth begins after a sixteen-year leap in a world of songs, flowers, commerce and young love. Time's Chorus openly admits that theatre is making the impossible transition. The break allows Perdita to grow beyond Leontes's reach and gives repentance a duration rather than a single speech. Sicilia and Bohemia are not separate plays: the freedom of the pastoral world develops under the shadow of a court's unfinished history.

Performance

The bear and the living image

Two stage problems have repeatedly shaped the play's performance history. “Exit, pursued by a bear” may be terrifying, comic or both, and productions have used actors, costumes, puppets, shadows and abstract forms rather than one settled solution. The statue scene poses the opposite challenge: stillness must become life without losing mystery. RSC productions have ranged from Peggy Ashcroft's influential Paulina in 1960 to David Farr's 2009 paper bear and later stagings that place race, family and reconciliation at the centre.

Characters

Principal characters and dramatic functions.

Leontes

Leontes turns suspicion into certainty with frightening speed. Because he is king, his private disorder becomes legal and physical danger for everyone around him. His sixteen years of mourning matter, but remorse does not itself entitle him to Hermione's return. The final scene tests whether repentance has made him capable of receiving grace rather than commanding it.

Hermione

Hermione meets accusation with controlled intelligence and refuses to beg for a life stripped of honour. At trial she names what has already been taken from her: husband, status, child and public reputation. Her stillness in the final scene echoes neither guilt nor passivity; it gives her control over the moment and terms of her return.

Paulina

Paulina speaks to Leontes when male courtiers soften or fall silent. She protects the infant Perdita, keeps Hermione's memory before the king and regulates his repentance for sixteen years. Her authority culminates in the statue scene, where she becomes director, witness and possible keeper of the play's central secret.

Perdita

Perdita has royal bearing without knowledge of royal birth. Her command of ceremony at the festival comes from attention rather than formal schooling, and she refuses Florizel once his choice appears likely to destroy him. Recognition restores her title, but the play has already established her worth before lineage confirms it.

Polixenes

Polixenes is Leontes's threatened friend and later the father who threatens Florizel and Perdita. His violent opposition to their marriage repeats, in another form, Leontes's wish to govern intimacy through royal certainty. Camillo's second act of disobedience helps another generation escape the damage caused by an angry king.

Autolycus

Autolycus is thief, ballad-seller, performer and expert reader of appetite. He brings the marketplace into pastoral Bohemia, turning songs and trinkets into profit. He does not cause the recognition, but his tricks repeatedly redirect the people carrying its evidence. The play enjoys him without mistaking charm for honesty.

Study questions

Questions for analysis and discussion.

Each question requires evidence from the text and can be used for discussion, notes or essay planning.

01

Why is Leontes jealous?

Resist the temptation to invent a single missing cause. Examine how his syntax changes, which innocent gestures he isolates and how contradiction strengthens rather than weakens his belief. The better question may be why his court cannot prevent a feeling without evidence from becoming a programme of government.

02

What would make Leontes worthy of forgiveness?

Set his grief and long repentance against the losses borne by Hermione, Perdita, Paulina and the dead Mamillius. The final reunion permits grace, but the text never declares that suffering has balanced an account. Distinguishing pardon from repayment keeps the ending emotionally generous without making Leontes's damage disappear.

03

Is Hermione's return a miracle or a disclosure?

Gather evidence for both possibilities: Paulina's secrecy, the aged appearance of the figure, Hermione's account of preservation, the music and the language of faith. A production must choose how much to reveal, but a reader need not close the question. The play uses uncertainty to make ordinary survival and supernatural restoration momentarily indistinguishable.
