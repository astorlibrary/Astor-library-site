Late romance / tragicomedy

The Two Noble Kinsmen, William Shakespeare

This Astor Library Edition presents the complete play with scene summaries, line numbering and explanatory notes. The support is designed to make the action clear without smoothing away its oddness: this is a play in which solemn ritual sits beside broad comedy, chivalric courtesy beside sudden violence, and apparent choice beside the rule of gods and circumstance.

The introductory material places the play within the late collaborative phase of Shakespeare’s career and explains John Fletcher’s part in its composition. It also follows the story back through Chaucer’s ‘Knight’s Tale’ to Boccaccio, showing what the dramatists retained, what they altered and why the newly invented story of the Jailer’s Daughter changes the weight of the whole play.

Further sections examine the language of kinship, the uneasy treatment of Emilia, the contrast between courtly and popular worlds, the three temple scenes and the play’s complicated ending. Questions for reading and performance encourage readers to test the claims made about nobility rather than simply accepting the judgements pronounced by Theseus and his court.

The shape of the play

How the action moves

Five changes that hold the plot together. Each one alters who possesses power, knowledge or the right to tell the story.

01 · Movement one

A wedding halted by grief

Theseus and Hippolyta’s wedding procession is interrupted by three queens whose husbands have been killed by Creon and denied burial. Their appeal turns celebration into military action. Theseus defeats Thebes, and the wounded cousins Palamon and Arcite are found on the battlefield and carried to an Athenian prison.

02 · Movement two

The prison window

From their cell, the cousins see Emilia walking in a garden. Their sworn unity collapses almost at once as each claims that his love is prior and more honourable. Arcite is released but banished from Athens; he returns in disguise and enters Emilia’s service. The Jailer’s Daughter, meanwhile, frees Palamon because she believes her devotion will be returned.

03 · Movement three

Courtesy sharpened into combat

Palamon hides in the woods while the Daughter loses him and falls into distress. Arcite finds his cousin, brings him food and armour, and prepares to settle their rivalry in a formally courteous duel. Theseus discovers them and initially condemns both men, but the appeals of Hippolyta and Emilia produce a public tournament instead.

04 · Movement four

Desire, illness and preparation

Each knight is allowed to gather three companions for the contest. Emilia studies portraits of the cousins but cannot make the choice that the men and Theseus demand. Away from the court, the Daughter’s disordered speech and behaviour are treated by a Doctor who instructs her former Wooer to impersonate Palamon, turning imagined love into a proposed cure.

05 · Movement five

Three prayers and one broken victory

Palamon prays to Venus for Emilia, Arcite asks Mars for victory, and Emilia asks Diana to remain unmarried or be given to the man who loves her best. Arcite wins the tournament, yet a divinely disturbed horse throws and fatally injures him. Before dying, he gives Emilia to Palamon, allowing every prayer to be answered while leaving the cost of that settlement painfully visible.

Read closely

Three ways into the writing

These are not themes to collect. They are patterns to follow through particular scenes, voices and choices.

Close reading

What happens to the cousins’ friendship?

Palamon and Arcite first describe their kinship as something stronger than imprisonment, defeat or fortune. Their language imagines two lives joined in a single honour. The sight of Emilia does not gently weaken that bond; it converts intimacy into possession and makes each man reinterpret the other as a traitor.

Their later duel preserves the manners of friendship even as it attempts to destroy the friend. They feed, dress and arm one another before fighting. On stage, that mixture of tenderness and aggression can make their rivalry look heroic, absurd or both at once. The play repeatedly asks whether chivalric form dignifies violence or merely decorates it.

Close reading

Emilia and the language of choice

The court speaks as though Emilia were the reward that gives male combat its meaning, but she is far less certain than the tournament requires. Her memory of Flavina, the girl she loved in childhood, establishes an emotional history untouched by either cousin. Her prayer to Diana expresses not indecision alone but a wish to escape the terms on which others have framed her future.

Productions must decide how much freedom Emilia has at the end. She can receive Palamon as the survivor chosen by providence, or appear trapped by a sequence of decisions made around her. Her silences, reactions and physical place during the tournament therefore matter as much as the formal speeches of the men.

Close reading

The Jailer’s Daughter outside the romance

The unnamed Daughter gives the play a second account of consuming love. Unlike the aristocratic knights, she cannot turn desire into an authorised contest. Helping Palamon escape endangers both her life and her father’s, while Palamon scarcely recognises that she has acted for him. Rank makes her devotion invisible to the courtly story.

Her songs, fragments and shifting images have often been compared with Ophelia’s language in Hamlet, but the surrounding action is different. Her suffering becomes material for diagnosis, performance and substitution when the Wooer is told to pretend to be Palamon. The proposed cure can seem compassionate, unsettling or brutally convenient, and the play never supplies an easy answer.

Book, theatre, history

The worlds around the play

Dating, source and performance matter most when they return us to something the text is doing.

Source

Chaucer retold for a Jacobean stage

The Prologue openly acknowledges Chaucer, an unusually direct declaration of literary debt in Shakespearean drama. Fletcher and Shakespeare retain the noble cousins, the tournament and the competing prayers, but add the Jailer’s Daughter, expand Emilia’s reluctance and bring popular dance into the action. The result places an inherited tale of chivalric order beside experiences that its order cannot comfortably contain.

Authorship

A play made by two dramatists

The 1634 quarto attributes the play to Fletcher and Shakespeare, and modern scholarship broadly accepts the collaboration. Differences in vocabulary, metre and dramatic habit allow editors to propose divisions of labour, although performance turns those separate contributions into one continuous event. The collaboration belongs to the period in which Fletcher was becoming a leading dramatist for the King’s Men.

Performance

Ceremony, dance and theatrical contrast

The play requires processions, battles, a prison, woodland scenes, madness, temples and a formal tournament. Its rural entertainment draws on contemporary morris dancing and briefly places working people at the centre of the stage. A production can emphasise spectacular romance, expose the coercion beneath its ceremonies, or let the court and country plots unsettle one another.

Keep in view

People carrying the argument

Palamon

A Theban prince and Arcite’s cousin. His devotion to Emilia turns him against the companion he had treated as another self. Venus grants his deeper wish, but only after defeat and the apparent approach of execution.

Arcite

Palamon’s cousin, released from prison and banished from Athens. He returns in disguise, earns a place near Emilia and wins the tournament through military excellence, only for his victory to be overturned by fatal accident.

Emilia

Hippolyta’s sister and the unwilling centre of the cousins’ contest. Her memories, hesitation and prayer to Diana resist the assumption that marriage to either man must be her natural desire.

The Jailer’s Daughter

An unnamed young woman who releases Palamon and is abandoned to fear, longing and mental distress. Her story exposes the difference between celebrated aristocratic passion and desire without social power.

Theseus

Duke of Athens, conqueror of Thebes and organiser of the tournament. His judgements shift quickly, yet his authority determines which forms of grief, love and violence receive public recognition.

Hippolyta

Theseus’s Amazon bride and Emilia’s sister. Her intervention helps prevent the cousins’ immediate execution, while her presence carries traces of a conquered history beneath the wedding celebrations.

Carry through the text

Questions that stay open

Use them to test a scene, a performance choice or an argument of your own.

01

Are Palamon and Arcite noble?

Trace the moments in which the play calls the cousins honourable and compare those claims with their treatment of one another and of Emilia. Does the drama confirm the court’s praise, undermine it, or keep both responses in play?

02

Whose wishes do the gods answer?

Compare the prayers to Mars, Venus and Diana with the final result. Consider whether the ending proves that divine order exists or reveals how little control the characters possess over the meanings assigned to their lives.

03

How should the Daughter’s recovery be staged?

Examine the Doctor’s instructions and the Wooer’s impersonation of Palamon. What would make the treatment appear caring, exploitative or uncertain, and how would each choice alter the audience’s response to the final scene?
