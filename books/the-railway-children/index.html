The family at Three Chimneys

After their father is unexpectedly taken away, Roberta, Peter and Phyllis leave their comfortable London home with their mother. They settle at Three Chimneys, a country cottage near a railway, and learn to live with much less money. Their mother writes stories to support the family while keeping the circumstances of their father’s disappearance from them.

The children become acquainted with the station porter Perks and an elderly passenger whom they wave to as his train passes. Their adventures include a railway embankment collapse, a rescue in a tunnel and the arrival of a Russian exile. These events connect the family’s private difficulty with the work and journeys of the people around them, until the mystery of their father can be addressed.

From serial to book

The Railway Children was serialised in The London Magazine in thirteen instalments, from January 1905 to January 1906. Wells Gardner, Darton issued the book in 1906. The edition records both forms of first publication rather than treating the date on the book title page as the beginning of the work’s public history.

The original book contained twenty black-and-white plates by C. E. Brock, including the frontispiece. Those illustrations belong to the history of the 1906 publication. This Astor edition discusses them but does not reproduce them; its additional material consists of historical and literary commentary around the complete text.

Railway work and travel

Nesbit’s railway is a place of employment as well as adventure. Perks’s responsibilities, the station routine, passing trains, signals and the need to stop an approaching engine give the story concrete practical circumstances. The children’s friendship with railway workers is sustained by repeated visits and the exchange of help, rather than one isolated rescue.

The contextual material explains Edwardian railways, the distinctions of class surrounding passenger travel and railway employment, and the technology and routines mentioned in the story. It also follows the children’s mistakes about what constitutes helping: Peter’s appropriation of coal and the attempted birthday gift to Perks both depend on differences between their intentions and another person’s understanding of the act.

Law, exile and the absent father

The father’s imprisonment and the Russian stranger’s history bring political and legal matters into a family narrative. The edition provides background on criminal justice, accusations of espionage and Russian exile, while preserving the order in which the children learn about these events. The historical material gives a basis for understanding references that the original audience could have recognised more readily.

The account also examines the Russo-Japanese War and the political atmosphere surrounding the novel’s first publication. These contexts are supplied as information about the period. They should not be confused with a claim that Nesbit’s plot is a direct transcript of one particular criminal case or that every incident has an identified real-life model.

Nesbit and the book’s later history

Edith Nesbit, who published as E. Nesbit, lived from 1858 to 1924. She wrote poetry and fiction for adults as well as the children’s books for which she became especially well known. The biographical material places The Railway Children alongside that wider career, rather than identifying the author solely with the railway story.

The publication and adaptation sections trace the book’s subsequent life in print, on television, in film and in performance. They distinguish the details of Nesbit’s novel from choices made by later adapters. A reader who knows a filmed version can therefore use the complete text to establish which incidents, settings and speeches are present in the original book.

What the editorial material supplies

The novel has no footnotes added to it. Chapter summaries and contextual material accompany the story at chapter ends, while character studies and key-passage discussions appear in the supporting sections. A chronology, glossary and publication and textual histories provide reference material for readers who need to check names, dates and unfamiliar terms.

The historical accounts address railways, law, class and political events in relation to particular passages. The character material follows Bobbie, Peter, Phyllis, their parents and the people they meet, keeping their actions within the story’s sequence. Together these additions support reading the whole 1906 novel and identifying the historical circumstances behind it, while the text itself remains separate from the modern commentary.
