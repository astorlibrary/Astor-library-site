Lawrence’s account of the Arab Revolt

Seven Pillars of Wisdom records Lawrence’s part in the Arab Revolt of 1916–1918. The narrative follows his association with Emir Feisal and campaigns involving Akaba, the Hejaz Railway and Damascus. It combines descriptions of journeys and military operations with recollections of individuals, political discussions and arguments about irregular warfare.

The historical apparatus treats it as a personal account written and revised after the events. Lawrence’s descriptions and figures are retained, including passages that differ from military records. Where the memoir disagrees with later evidence, the commentary points it out and the memoir is left as Lawrence wrote it.

The 1926 Subscribers’ text

This edition follows the text prepared for the 1926 Subscribers’ Edition, as issued for general circulation by Jonathan Cape in 1935. It is therefore distinct from the longer 1922 Oxford text and from Revolt in the Desert, Lawrence’s shorter abridgement published in 1927.

The copy-text is the Standard Ebooks transcription, derived from Colin Choat’s Project Gutenberg of Australia text and checked against digital scans. Lawrence’s punctuation, paragraphing and inconsistent spellings of Arabic names remain. The textual note explains that those inconsistencies were deliberate preferences Lawrence defended when his proofreader proposed standardising them.

What is included in the volume

The complete subscribers’ text includes Lawrence’s preface, the dedicatory poem, eleven book headnotes, the epilogue and his own back matter. Four authorial footnotes are marked with symbols, while the numbered notes elsewhere are editorial. The Greek expressions occurring in the narrative are supplied in transliteration.

The chapter removed before the 1926 printing and restored by Cape from 1940 is not included as part of the subscribers’ text; its history is explained in the editorial material. The original plates and folding maps are not reproduced. Lawrence’s account of commissioning the illustrations and his list of them remain in the back matter.

The book’s publication history

Lawrence’s account passed through several substantially different manuscripts and printings. The edition traces the lost 1919 draft, subsequent rewriting, the privately printed Oxford text of 1922 and the revision produced for subscribers in 1926. It explains why references to a ‘complete’ Seven Pillars need to specify which version is meant.

The expensive production of the subscribers’ volume led to the further abridgement published as Revolt in the Desert in 1927. Seven Pillars itself reached a general reading public after Lawrence’s death in 1935. The history of these versions is presented separately from the wartime chronology, so the dates of composition and revision are not confused with the dates of the campaign.

Lawrence before and after the campaign

Thomas Edward Lawrence was born in 1888. He studied history at Oxford, investigated Crusader castles and worked on archaeological projects in the Middle East before the First World War. He served in wartime intelligence in Cairo before travelling to the Hejaz in 1916 and becoming attached to Feisal.

The biographical material follows his involvement in postwar diplomacy and later service in the Royal Air Force and Tank Corps under assumed names. It also places his writing, translation work and technical interests within that later career. Lawrence died in 1935, shortly before the subscribers’ text was issued for general circulation.

Historical and reading apparatus

Summaries headed ‘The Story So Far’ and researched ‘Context’ material follow the twelve major divisions. The notes work book by book, with no box after each chapter. It gives readers a way to review the campaign’s changing locations, participants and objectives across a long narrative.

The introductory and reference material covers the Arab Revolt, First World War politics, military strategy, the Hejaz Railway, people and places, and the critical reception of Lawrence’s account. A chronology, glossary, explanatory notes and publication history provide practical support for reading the text while keeping its particular version and its limits as historical testimony clear.
