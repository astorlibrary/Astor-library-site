M. R. James: Collected Ghost Stories

M. R. James, Provost of King’s College, Cambridge, and later of Eton, wrote many of his ghost stories to read aloud to friends at Christmas. All thirty-three that he finished are here, from the four original volumes, the 1931 collection and three later stories, with seven pieces James wrote about the ghost story itself.

The extent of the collection

The volume gathers thirty-three completed ghost stories. Twenty-six come from James’s four original collections: Ghost Stories of an Antiquary, More Ghost Stories, A Thin Ghost and Others, and A Warning to the Curious and Other Ghost Stories. Four further completed stories were added to the collected edition of 1931.

The remaining three are The Experiment, The Malice of Inanimate Objects and A Vignette, published after the 1931 collection. The Astor contents therefore extend beyond a simple reprint of that collected volume. The distinction is useful when comparing editions described as collected or complete, because their actual contents can differ.

From separate volumes to collected stories

Ghost Stories of an Antiquary appeared in 1904, followed by More Ghost Stories in 1911, A Thin Ghost and Others in 1919, and A Warning to the Curious and Other Ghost Stories in 1925. The collections include such stories as Canon Alberic’s Scrap-book, The Mezzotint, Count Magnus, Casting the Runes and Oh, Whistle, and I’ll Come to You, My Lad.

The supporting publication histories identify first appearances rather than assuming that the date of a collection is the date of composition or initial circulation. Some stories were read to friends before publication; others appeared in periodicals or small separate printings. Those settings help explain how James’s fiction reached its first audiences.

James’s academic life and his fiction

Montague Rhodes James was born in 1862 and died in 1936. He was a scholar of medieval manuscripts and apocryphal literature, and served as Provost of King’s College, Cambridge, and later of Eton College. His scholarly work included the description and cataloguing of manuscript collections.

The fiction repeatedly involves readers, collectors and people investigating old buildings or objects. A manuscript, print, inscription, whistle or archaeological discovery can initiate a story. The Astor biographical and historical material explains James’s working knowledge of such things, while distinguishing documentary sources from claims that a particular event directly caused a fictional episode.

The text and the explanatory material

The edition preserves James’s spelling, punctuation and foreign-language forms. Story summaries provide a record of what happens, and researched context explains details that can otherwise be difficult to place. The glossary and chronology offer further reference points alongside the individual stories.

The supporting essays discuss antiquarian sources, actual locations, illustrations, Christmas readings and the history of reception. Discussion of illustrations in an original collection should be understood as publication history; the edition features listed here describe the Astor text and apparatus. Where a geographical identification or source connection is uncertain, it should remain distinguishable from a documented fact.

James on writing ghost stories

Seven pieces by James on the ghost story are included alongside the fiction. They allow readers to consult his own statements about the form and its composition. Such writing gives a different kind of evidence from later critical accounts: it records what James chose to say about the stories, their methods and the conditions of telling them.

The essays can be read beside individual tales without making every fictional detail an illustration of a fixed rule. James’s narrators differ in their access to events, and stories may be transmitted through conversations, documents or reports. The edition gives readers the fiction and the author’s commentary together so those differences can be examined directly.

Reception and adaptations

James’s stories have continued to circulate through anthologies, readings, radio and screen adaptations. Casting the Runes supplied the source for Jacques Tourneur’s 1957 film Night of the Demon. Television versions have also helped bring particular stories to audiences who first encounter them outside the printed collections.

The Astor material records publication and adaptation history while keeping the original stories central. Readers can use it to locate the source of an adaptation, identify the collection in which a story first appeared, or place a late story within James’s career.
