Daniel Defoe

Robinson Crusoe

Robinson Crusoe goes to sea against his father’s advice, is taken by pirates, becomes a planter in Brazil and in 1659 is shipwrecked alone on an island near the mouth of the Orinoco. He stays twenty-eight years. Defoe published the book in 1719 as Crusoe’s own story, with no author’s name.

About the work

Twenty-eight years on the island.

The account below follows Crusoe from York and Brazil to the 1659 shipwreck, the footprint and the rescue of Friday.

The work

What happens.

Born in York, Crusoe rejects his father’s advice to remain in the middle station. Voyages bring storms, enslavement at Salé, escape down the African coast and prosperity as a plantation owner in Brazil.

The shipwreck follows an expedition intended to obtain enslaved Africans from the Guinea coast. Alone, Crusoe salvages weapons and tools, builds a fortified dwelling, keeps a journal, grows barley and rice, makes pottery and reads survival through Providence and repentance.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

The discovery of a human footprint destroys his constructed security. Rescuing the man he names Friday ends isolation but creates a relationship organised around language, Christianity, service and colonial authority. A mutinied English ship later gives Crusoe the chance to present himself as governor.

The edition places the survival narrative inside maritime travel, Atlantic commerce, slavery, colonial expansion and the development of the English novel.

Text and context

A modernised text that keeps the journal’s dates.

Defoe’s long sentences survive, the twenty chapters are declared editorial, and the notes run from Alexander Selkirk to later adaptation.

Text

Editorial method

Spelling and punctuation are modernised where clarity requires it, while Defoe’s long sentences and dated journal entries are preserved. The familiar twenty-chapter structure is editorial rather than original.

Supporting material

Context and interpretation

Footnotes clarify nautical and archaic language; Story So Far and Context sections support each chapter. Longer material covers Defoe, Alexander Selkirk, Providence, property, Friday, realism, criticism and adaptation.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

Salvage, tools and repeated experiments

Crusoe’s survival begins with repeated trips to the wreck, from which he obtains food, weapons, ammunition, tools, clothing and materials. His island work consequently depends on manufactured objects carried by the ship. A saw, an axe, nails and gunpowder make possible activities he could not easily begin with what the island alone provides.

The narrative records unsuccessful work as well as useful inventions. Crusoe makes a large canoe before discovering that he cannot move it to the water. Growing grain involves learning the seasons and preserving seed after mistakes; making earthenware requires repeated attempts and an accidental discovery about firing clay. The time spent describing these processes distinguishes the complete novel from abridgements that chiefly retain the footprint, Friday and the mutiny.

A journal inside a remembered life

The island story is told twice at different levels. Crusoe first recounts the wreck and his preparations retrospectively, then inserts a dated journal that returns to his arrival on 30 September 1659. The journal records weather, labour, illness and supplies. Its limits are material: the keeping of written records depends on the ink and paper he has salvaged.

The retrospective narrator also reviews his earlier explanations of events. When grain appears near his dwelling, he initially interprets it as a miracle and subsequently remembers shaking out a bag that had contained grain. During illness, dreams and biblical reading alter the way he understands his circumstances. Tracking these corrections makes the religious argument more precise: the book repeatedly distinguishes an event, Crusoe’s first interpretation and the account he gives after further thought.

Commerce before and after the island

The Brazilian plantation is part of the novel’s financial structure throughout. Crusoe leaves it to join an expedition seeking enslaved people, and after his return to Europe he discovers that his interests in Brazil have accumulated substantial value. His eventual wealth does not come simply from the crops and livestock he develops on the island. It also depends on property, trade and the work conducted in his absence.

Friday is preceded by Xury, the boy who escapes captivity with Crusoe on the African coast. Crusoe later transfers Xury to the Portuguese captain under an arrangement that includes a promise of freedom after ten years if he becomes Christian. Remembering this earlier relationship helps readers follow the distinctions Crusoe makes between friendship, service, religious conversion and ownership. The complete narrative also continues after the island rescue, including the return through Europe and an overland encounter with wolves; the familiar castaway story is one large part of a longer life account.
