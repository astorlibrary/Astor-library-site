Henrik Ibsen

A Doll’s House

Years ago Nora Helmer forged her dead father’s signature to borrow the money that saved her husband’s life. Now Krogstad, the man who lent it, is about to lose his job at Torvald’s bank, and he still has the forged paper. Ibsen’s play was first performed in Copenhagen in 1879; Astor uses R. Farquharson Sharp’s translation of 1910.

The Helmer household

The play takes place over three Christmas days in a Norwegian household. Torvald Helmer’s new appointment at a bank appears to promise greater financial security for his wife Nora and their children. Nora, however, has concealed a loan she obtained years earlier to finance a journey undertaken for Torvald’s health.

The lender, Krogstad, threatens to expose the forged signature used to secure that loan. Mrs Linde, an old friend seeking work, and Doctor Rank, a regular visitor, also enter the household. Ibsen confines the action to the Helmers’ rooms, where conversations, visits and the arrival of letters progressively reveal the circumstances behind the family’s apparent prosperity.

Publication and first performance

Et dukkehjem was published on 4 December 1879. Its first printing comprised 8,000 copies. The premiere followed at the Royal Theatre in Copenhagen on 21 December, with Betty Hennings playing Nora. The edition treats the printed play and its production history together, giving readers the chronology of its first appearance before following later performances.

The first public British production took place at London’s Novelty Theatre on 7 June 1889, with Janet Achurch as Nora and Charles Charrington as Helmer, using William Archer’s translation. That production belongs to the play’s reception history; the English text printed in this Astor edition is Sharp’s later translation.

The English translation and numbering

This edition uses R. Farquharson Sharp’s English translation, published by J. M. Dent in Everyman’s Library in 1910. Sharp is credited as translator and Haydn Wood as editor. The translation is identified explicitly because Ibsen’s play circulates in several English versions with differences of vocabulary, phrasing and theatrical emphasis.

The prose is numbered by printed line throughout all three acts. These numbers belong to this edition’s layout and provide a means of referring to particular passages. Explanatory notes appear on the page, with attention to places where Sharp anglicises Ibsen’s Norwegian monetary figures or wording. The notes supply the original information alongside the translated text.

Norwegian law, money and marriage

The historical material explains the legal and financial circumstances relevant to Nora’s borrowing. It distinguishes the restrictions faced by married women in Norway at the time of the play from later reforms, rather than importing English legal arrangements into a Norwegian setting.

The commentary also addresses money, employment and household arrangements. These details matter to the action: Torvald’s bank position, Krogstad’s concern about his employment, Mrs Linde’s need for work and Nora’s private repayments all have specific financial circumstances. Act-end context panels accompany the summaries, connecting such references to the scenes in which they occur.

Laura Kieler and the alternative ending

A section on the writer Laura Kieler examines the documented parallels between her circumstances and Nora’s story. It preserves an important qualification: Kieler later denied committing forgery and sought a public clarification from Ibsen. Her life is therefore not presented as an exact factual version of the play.

The edition also explains the alternative ending Ibsen supplied for German performance. His February 1880 letter describes his objection to the change and his reason for making it himself rather than allowing another writer to alter the play. This material helps distinguish the original conclusion from a historically documented adaptation of it.

Reading and performing the play

The supporting matter covers Ibsen’s background, the play’s publication, theatrical staging, critical responses and screen history. Discussion of Doctor Rank retains the limits of the dialogue: the play’s medical language and its period associations can be explained without turning an unstated diagnosis into a fact announced by Ibsen.

For readers, the summaries establish the sequence of events across the three acts. For students and performers, numbered lines and on-page notes provide practical reference points for close examination of speeches, pauses and exchanges. The historical chapters keep the play’s Norwegian circumstances and the later decisions of translators and theatre practitioners visible as separate parts of its history.
