Thomas Middleton

The Puritan

A rich widow in Watling Street swears she will never marry again, and a crew of penniless London tricksters set out to change her mind and marry her daughters. The play was printed in 1607 as by ‘W. S.’ and ended up in the Shakespeare apocrypha; it is now generally given to Thomas Middleton. The spelling is modernised.

About the work

A widow’s vow and Pyeboard’s profitable frauds.

The account below follows the conspiracy from the overheard vow to the exposure at the church door, played out across named London streets and parishes.

The work

What happens.

A wealthy widow vows never to remarry, her elder daughter Frances follows, and the younger Moll privately decides to find a husband quickly. The expelled scholar Pyeboard overhears and designs a profitable fraud with Skirmish, Captain Idle and Corporal Oath.

A stolen chain, false prophecy, staged quarrel, sleeping draught and counterfeit conjuring allow the men to appear useful, desirable and supernatural. The widow chooses Idle and Frances chooses Pyeboard before exposure at the church door overturns the expected ending.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

The comedy depends on performance and the precise geography of London while targeting wealth, gullibility, pretended piety and the precarious lives of those who survive by invention.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

The Children of Paul’s and the first printing

George Eld printed the play in 1607 under the longer title The Puritan; or, the Widow of Watling Street. Its title page names the Children of Paul’s as the performing company. This matters because it puts the play with a company of boy actors, and not with Shakespeare’s own company. The initials W. S. cannot be assessed in isolation from that information.

The play’s later publication history explains why it continued to be encountered as Shakespeare. It appeared in the enlarged Third Folio in 1664 and in subsequent collections before being treated among the disputed works. The modern attribution to Middleton rests on the play’s style and its likeness to his other work; the initials settle nothing.

The widow’s household and competing marriages

The opening mourning scene immediately divides the household. Lady Plus makes emphatic declarations of grief, Sir Godfrey supplies examples of other widows, and the children respond in ways that expose their different expectations of life after the father’s death. Moll’s interest in marriage is particularly practical: her negotiations with Sir John Pennydub include a coach, servants, clothes and money of her own to spend.

Courtship therefore involves a series of proposed settlements, not a single romantic pursuit. The established suitors offer one set of possibilities; Pyeboard’s associates manufacture another. Changes of intention are followed closely enough for the audience to compare what the characters initially profess with the material advantages and predictions that subsequently persuade them.

Pyeboard’s tricks

The supposed supernatural evidence depends on actions the audience can follow. A chain is stolen before its recovery is presented as a demonstration of unusual knowledge. The conspirators prepare what their victims will see and then supply an explanation that makes the prepared event appear miraculous. Their success relies on control of information and timing, as much as on the victims’ readiness to believe.

Pyeboard’s skill also extends to ordinary social emergencies, including escape from officers seeking payment of debt. His inventive language can change a listener’s understanding of who he is and why he is present. The comedy repeatedly places this resourcefulness beside his precarious circumstances. Reading his financial difficulties alongside the conjuring plot gives a fuller account of his conduct than describing him only as a clever trickster.
