Jacobean tragedy · c. 1612–14

The Duchess of Malfi, John Webster

The play begins with a private act of choice. The widowed Duchess proposes to Antonio, her steward, and refuses the role of permanent mourning her brothers have imposed upon her. That marriage sets a household against a court built on surveillance, patronage and fear.

This Astor Library edition keeps the complete play at the centre. Scene summaries and explanatory notes make the action easier to follow, while longer material opens Webster’s sources, the Jacobean theatre, court service, tragedy, language and performance.

The study material does not turn the Duchess into a simple emblem or Bosola into a puzzle with one answer. It gives readers the evidence needed to argue about freedom, rank, secrecy, conscience and what remains of identity when public power has been stripped away.

The shape of the work

A secret household inside a watched court

Five turns take the play from self-directed marriage to the final attempt to answer violence with violence.

01 · Marriage

The Duchess chooses

Ordered never to remarry, the Duchess proposes to Antonio herself. Cariola witnesses a ceremony that is loving, legally serious and dangerously private.

02 · Surveillance

Bosola reads the body

Ferdinand places Bosola in the household as a spy. Apricots, pregnancy and a dropped horoscope turn ordinary physical signs into evidence.

03 · Exposure

Confidence becomes betrayal

Years pass and the couple have children. The Duchess trusts Bosola with Antonio’s supposed dismissal; he carries the truth to her brothers.

04 · Imprisonment

Power becomes theatre

Ferdinand uses wax figures, darkness and a dance of madmen to assault the Duchess’s mind before executioners kill her, Cariola and two children.

05 · Reckoning

Revenge misses its mark

Bosola turns against the brothers but kills Antonio by mistake. The Cardinal, Ferdinand and Bosola die, leaving the surviving son to carry the family forward.

Read closely

Three places where authority is tested

Each route begins with particular language and follows what changes around it.

Marriage

The Duchess writes her own scene

“The misery of us that are born great,” the Duchess observes: because nobody dares approach her, she must do the wooing. Rank gives her authority over Antonio, but also makes ordinary courtship impossible. She produces the ring and directs language that is romantic, legal and ceremonial at once.

Her insistence that she is “flesh and blood” rejects the cold monument of the permanently mourning widow. Yet Cariola’s unease keeps danger in view. This act of self-determination has begun inside the same hidden world that will later let her brothers act without restraint.

Service

Bosola reads bodies for powerful men

Bosola’s work as an intelligencer turns appetite, sickness, childbirth and appearance into things he can inspect and report. The apricot scene can be funny in performance, but its comedy is invasive: a woman’s pregnant body is being interpreted without her permission.

He understands the court’s ugliness better than almost anyone, yet continues to serve it. Poverty and neglected merit explain something about his position; they do not settle responsibility. His late revenge cannot restore the people he helped destroy and produces another fatal error.

Identity

“I am Duchess of Malfi still”

By the execution scene, the Duchess has lost her household, freedom, husband and apparent children. Her short statement refuses to let Ferdinand’s punishments determine who she is. Surrounded by theatrical horrors and evasive language, she speaks with a clarity her persecutors lack.

The scene does not offer one tidy model of courage. Cariola resists death desperately, while the Duchess has already endured a campaign intended to exhaust hope. Her composure is powerful because Webster leaves fear, bodily suffering and injustice visible around it.

Book, history, form

History, theatre and service

Context matters here because it changes what we can notice in the writing.

Source

History reshaped

The real Giovanna d’Aragona secretly married Antonio Beccadelli after her first husband’s death. Webster inherited the story through European retellings and gives the Duchess a dramatic presence stronger than the surviving record affords her.

Stage

A tragedy for two theatres

Blackfriars could intensify darkness, music and close psychological pressure; the Globe offered a larger public space for ceremonies and sudden reversals. The play’s effects belong to bodies and voices, not darkness on the page alone.

Court

Service and surveillance

Antonio and Bosola depend upon aristocratic employment. Offices, rewards and patronage shape moral choices throughout a court where access to power is bought through usefulness.

Characters

The people carrying the pressure

The Duchess

A widowed ruler whose private marriage challenges patriarchal and dynastic control.

Antonio Bologna

Her steward and husband: thoughtful and honourable, but increasingly vulnerable after exposure.

Daniel de Bosola

A malcontent scholar, spy and murderer whose intelligence repeatedly outruns his moral courage.

Ferdinand

The Duchess’s twin brother, driven by an unstable mixture of family pride, possessiveness and sexual disgust.

The Cardinal

Ferdinand’s brother, a churchman whose calm methods conceal a more settled corruption.

Cariola

The Duchess’s attendant, witness to the marriage and one of the few people inside her confidence.

Carry through the work

Questions that stay open

Use them to test a passage, a reading or an argument of your own.

01

What does secrecy protect?

Does the secret marriage preserve the Duchess’s freedom, or reproduce the hidden habits of the court around her?

02

When does explanation end?

At what point can Bosola no longer claim that service, poverty and necessity are responsible for his choices?

03

What survives power?

What remains of the Duchess’s authority after her political and physical power have been removed?

04

How should Ferdinand be played?

Is his obsession chiefly dynastic anxiety, misogyny, sexual jealousy or an unstable combination of all three?
