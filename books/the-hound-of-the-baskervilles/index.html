The case

A country doctor, James Mortimer, brings Sherlock Holmes an old family paper about Hugo Baskerville, a squire of the Civil War years who hunted a girl across Dartmoor and was found with his throat torn out by a black beast shaped like a hound. The legend says the hound has plagued the family ever since. Sir Charles Baskerville took it seriously, and three months ago he was found dead in the yew alley of Baskerville Hall. Mortimer kept one fact from the inquest: some distance from the body he saw the footprints of a gigantic hound.

Sir Henry, the heir, arrives from Canada to a warning made of words cut from The Times and a series of small thefts in his hotel. Holmes sends Watson to Devon with him, and Watson’s letters describe the moor, the convict escaped from Princetown, the butler who signals from a window at night, the naturalist Stapleton and his sister at Merripit House, and a tall figure seen standing on a tor against the moon.

Holmes returns

Conan Doyle had sent Holmes over the Reichenbach Falls in The Final Problem, published in The Strand Magazine in December 1893, and for nearly eight years there were no new Holmes stories. The Hound brought him back in August 1901, billed as another adventure of Sherlock Holmes. Doyle did not explain how Holmes had survived until The Empty House in the autumn of 1903.

Mortimer’s line about the footprints ended the first instalment, followed by (To be continued), and the story ran for nine months. It is the third of the four Holmes novels, after A Study in Scarlet and The Sign of Four.

Fletcher Robinson and the West Country

Doyle acknowledged a collaborator from the start. A footnote to the first instalment said that the story owed its inception to his friend Mr. Fletcher Robinson, who had helped with the general plot and the local details, and the book is dedicated to Robinson for his account of a West-Country legend.

Dartmoor in the novel is real ground made stranger. There are the tors, the granite, the bogs and the stone huts of prehistoric people, and Holmes’s map shows the convict prison at Princetown fourteen miles from the Hall. Baskerville Hall, Grimpen, Coombe Tracey and the Grimpen Mire are invented.

Science and the supernatural

The novel sets a family curse against a detective who has confined his investigations to this world. Its men of science are part of the atmosphere: Mortimer measures skulls, writes papers on atavism and asks whether disease is a reversion, and Holmes, looking at an old portrait, speaks of a throw-back, physical and spiritual. Doyle, himself a doctor, uses the fashionable science of heredity half seriously and half as comedy.

Holmes insists that the devil’s agents may be of flesh and blood, and the book gives every Gothic mystery a material cause. It also spends much of its length making the reader afraid: the moan across the moor, the hound’s cry at night, and a fog that creeps in from the mire at the climax.

How the story is told

For most of the book Holmes appears to be in London, and Watson does the detecting. Three chapters are given over to his letters to Holmes and the diary he kept, written without knowing how things will turn out, so the reader shares his guesses, including the wrong ones. When Holmes reappears, Watson learns that his reports have been read closely all along.

In the last chapter the story moves back to Baker Street at the end of November, where Holmes goes over the case by the fire from the murderer’s point of view. He admits one question he cannot answer, and then takes Watson to the opera.

On film

Film-makers have returned to the novel many times. The Twentieth Century Fox version of 1939 was the first to cast Basil Rathbone and Nigel Bruce as Holmes and Watson, and, according to the Sherlock Holmes Society of London, the first Holmes film kept correctly in period.

The 1902 text and this edition

The novel was serialised in The Strand Magazine from August 1901 to April 1902, with sixty illustrations by Sidney Paget, and appeared in book form from George Newnes in London on 25 March 1902. The first edition contained sixteen of Paget’s sixty illustrations; McClure, Phillips published the American edition in April. The 1902 book differs in places from other printings: in it Sir Charles dies on the 4th of June.
