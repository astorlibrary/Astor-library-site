The Haunted Man and the Ghost’s Bargain

Redlaw, a chemist and lecturer, is visited by a phantom that looks exactly like him and offers to take away his painful memories. He accepts, and finds that he now passes the same forgetting to everyone he meets. It was the last of Dickens’s five Christmas books, published in December 1848.

About the work

Redlaw’s bargain and the last of the Christmas books.

The account below follows the phantom’s gift from Redlaw to the people around him, and ends where the book’s stage history does, with the scene that became Pepper’s Ghost.

The work

What happens.

Redlaw, a solitary chemistry teacher, is haunted by memories of neglect, lost love and his sister’s death. On Christmas Eve a phantom in his own likeness offers what he believes he wants: the power to forget every sorrow, wrong and trouble he has known.

The bargain carries a second gift. The same loss of memory passes to those Redlaw meets. Pain disappears, but gratitude, tenderness and fellow-feeling disappear with it. Dickens makes suffering neither desirable nor easily redeeming; its moral value lies in what people learn to recognise in one another.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

Milly Swidger provides the counterexample. Her grief has enlarged sympathy rather than destroyed it. A neglected child remains immune because he possesses no loving memories to lose, making absence itself a terrible form of protection.

The concluding stage-history essay follows Dickens’s permission for Pepper to use a scene from the book in the optical illusion later called Pepper’s Ghost. Pepper recorded that the scene ran for fifteen months.

The publication’s family context

The Haunted Man appeared in the year Dickens lost his older sister Fanny, who died in September 1848. The Charles Dickens Museum records first-day sales of 18,000 copies. A surviving invitation from Catherine Dickens to Thomas Beard shows the family arranging a dinner at Devonshire Terrace on 3 January 1849 to celebrate the book’s success.

The invitation was written on mourning paper. It supplies a small but concrete document of the circumstances surrounding publication: a successful new Christmas book and a recent family bereavement belonged to the same household and the same season. That record provides biographical context for a story concerned with recollected suffering, without establishing that Redlaw’s experiences are a direct transcription of the author’s life.

The households affected by the bargain

Redlaw’s influence reaches people whose difficulties are already present before his intervention. The Tetterby household is crowded, short of money and occupied with the demands of children. Johnny’s responsibility for carrying the baby is a recurring detail of the family’s daily life. When Redlaw’s condition spreads, existing fatigue and dissatisfaction turn into hostility between people who had previously cared for one another.

The student known as Edmund also brings a separate family history into the story. His connection with the name Longford links his circumstances with people from Redlaw’s past. These relationships emerge through meetings and conversations rather than through the phantom’s explanation of the bargain. By the concluding meal in the old hall, the Swidgers, the student, the Tetterbys and the unnamed child have been brought together, giving the resolution a specific group of participants rather than leaving it as Redlaw’s private recovery.

A remembered inscription beneath the portrait in the hall closes the narrative with an explicit appeal to preserve memory.

Dickens’s abandoned reading version

The Charles Dickens Museum preserves a copy of The Haunted Man that Dickens prepared for oral performance. He cut pages from the original 1848 edition, mounted them in a larger book and added highlighting and annotations. The resulting object records work on adapting the printed story to a spoken programme.

The museum dates its likely preparation to 1858, when Dickens began his first paid public-reading tour, and records that he abandoned the selection as unsuitable. A prepared reading copy should therefore not be mistaken for proof that he regularly performed the work. This abandoned version belongs beside the better-known theatrical afterlife of Pepper’s Ghost: one involved Dickens reshaping his prose for his own voice, while the other used a scene from the story in an optical stage demonstration. They are distinct episodes in the work’s performance history.
