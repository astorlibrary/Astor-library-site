William Shakespeare

Pericles, Prince of Tyre

A prince flees a death sentence and spends years at the mercy of seas, rulers and chance. Its extraordinary reunions are not merely convenient surprises: the play makes loss last long enough for recognition itself to become a test of patience, memory and attention.

Romance

Pericles, Prince of Tyre, William Shakespeare

Pericles solves the riddle by which King Antiochus conceals an incestuous relationship with his daughter, and the correct answer places him in mortal danger. His flight carries him through famine-stricken Tarsus to Pentapolis, where he survives a shipwreck, wins a tournament and marries Thaisa. On the voyage home, Thaisa appears to die while giving birth to Marina and is placed in a chest cast into the sea.

Thaisa is revived by the physician Cerimon at Ephesus, while Marina grows up in the care of Cleon and Dionyza. Dionyza orders her murder, but pirates abduct Marina and sell her to a brothel in Mytilene, where her intelligence and moral authority repeatedly frustrate those who hope to profit from her. Years later, Marina restores the grief-stricken Pericles through conversation. A vision of Diana then directs him to Ephesus, where Thaisa is also recovered.

This Astor Library Edition presents the complete play with scene summaries, line numbering and explanatory notes. It treats the damaged early text honestly, explains the strong modern case for collaboration with George Wilkins, and follows the play's older Apollonius story through Gower and Laurence Twine. Its notes give particular attention to the changes in language and dramatic texture across the play without turning attribution into a contest over which scenes deserve to be valued.

The shape of the play

How the action moves

Five changes that hold the plot together. Each one alters who possesses power, knowledge or the right to tell the story.

01 · Movement I

The riddle that must not be answered

At Antioch, unsuccessful suitors are executed for failing to solve the king's riddle. Pericles understands that it reveals Antiochus's incest, but speaking the answer openly would also condemn him. He returns to Tyre, leaves Helicanus in charge and flees again when he realises that distance alone will not end Antiochus's pursuit.

02 · Movement II

Famine, shipwreck and a second courtship

Pericles brings grain to the starving people of Tarsus, earning the gratitude of Cleon and Dionyza. Another voyage ends in shipwreck, leaving him alone on the coast of Pentapolis. Fishermen recover his armour, and he enters King Simonides's tournament. His skill and restraint win the love of Thaisa, whom he marries.

03 · Movement III

Birth and apparent death at sea

News of Antiochus's death allows Pericles to sail home with the pregnant Thaisa. During a storm she gives birth to Marina and appears to die. The sailors insist that the body must be put overboard, so Pericles seals Thaisa in a chest with identifying objects and a letter. The chest reaches Ephesus, where Cerimon revives her.

04 · Movement IV

Marina survives the stories written for her

Pericles leaves Marina at Tarsus, where her accomplishments provoke Dionyza's jealousy on behalf of her own daughter. A hired murderer is interrupted by pirates, who sell Marina into a Mytilene brothel. She resists commodification through speech, changing the intentions of prospective customers and persuading the brothel-keepers to let her earn money by teaching.

05 · Movement V

Recognition after silence

Believing Marina dead, Pericles withdraws into profound grief and ceases to speak. Marina is brought aboard his ship in the hope that she can help him. Their cautious exchange reveals that they are father and daughter, and Pericles hears heavenly music. Diana then sends the family to Ephesus, where Thaisa's identity is recognised and the separated household is restored.

Read closely

Three ways into the writing

These are not themes to collect. They are patterns to follow through particular scenes, voices and choices.

Opening Chorus

Gower returns to tell an old story

The medieval poet John Gower opens the play as a speaker returned from the grave. His deliberately old-fashioned vocabulary and four-beat couplets distinguish his voice from the dramatic scenes he introduces. He does not apologise for the age or improbability of the story. Instead, he asks an audience to accept transmission itself—the passage of a tale through authors, books, speakers and centuries—as part of the pleasure.

Gower's presence allows the play to cross countries and years at remarkable speed. He reports action, interprets conduct, introduces dumb shows and sometimes tells us what a scene cannot afford to stage. This is not failed drama patched by narration. The alternation between telling and showing makes the audience conscious of imagination at work, particularly when a bare stage must become a ship, a storm, a tournament or a city reached after years of travel.

Act 3, Scene 1

A birth inside a storm

The storm scene forces incompatible events into the same few minutes. Pericles pleads with the elements, Lychorida brings him his newborn daughter, and the news of Thaisa's apparent death arrives with the child. The sailors' demand for immediate burial gives grief no ceremonial time. Their maritime necessity may sound brutal, but aboard a threatened ship it has the force of collective survival.

The name Marina turns catastrophe into memory: the child will carry the sea in her identity. Pericles also places spices, jewels and a written account beside Thaisa, trusting objects and text to speak if her body is found. The scene therefore begins the play's long work of recognition. People separated by water cannot depend upon appearance alone; names, stories, tokens and the willingness to hear an unlikely account will eventually bring them together.

Act 5, Scene 1

Marina brings Pericles back to language

Marina does not recognise Pericles at once, and he initially resists her. The discovery grows through questions rather than a sudden visual revelation. Her birthplace, lost mother, name and history sound impossible, yet each detail answers something in his own life. Pericles returns to speech by listening closely enough to let another person's story revise what he believes is fixed.

The scene carefully delays certainty. Marina's account resembles romance convention, but to Pericles it is evidence recovered from unbearable loss. His joy culminates in music that others cannot hear. Whether that music is divine, psychological or theatrical matters less than its timing: after years of silence, recognition retunes the world. The scene earns wonder through the precision of conversation before it asks an audience to accept a miracle.

Book, theatre, history

The play in context

Dating, source and performance matter most when they return us to something the text is doing.

Authorship and text

A collaborative play in a damaged witness

Most attribution studies find George Wilkins's hand in the first two acts and Shakespeare's in the last three, although the Folger editors and some other scholars urge greater caution. The 1609 First Quarto is itself unreliable and may preserve a reported or otherwise corrupted text. Differences in style cannot therefore be mapped mechanically onto authorship. A good edition distinguishes three separate questions: who wrote which material, what happened during transmission, and how the surviving play works in performance.

Story tradition

Apollonius becomes Pericles

The tale of Apollonius of Tyre circulated for centuries before the play. John Gower included it in Confessio Amantis, while Laurence Twine's prose version supplied additional material, particularly around Marina's threatened life in the brothel. Making Gower the Chorus turns a source into a character. The play openly declares that it is retelling something old, then uses theatre to renew familiar romance patterns of wandering, lost children, apparent death and family recognition.

Performance

How to put an entire sea on stage

Pericles asks for storms, shipwrecks, distant courts, famine, divine vision and movement around the eastern Mediterranean. Its episodic form has encouraged productions built through music, ensemble movement and visible storytelling. Terry Hands's 1969 RSC staging sharply separated Gower from the sensual world he narrated; the 2003 Cardboard Citizens collaboration connected exile with asylum and trafficking; and Tamara Harvey's 2024 RSC production used a company-led approach to the play's journeys and recoveries.

Characters

Principal characters

Pericles

Pericles begins as an intellectually confident young ruler but spends much of the play unable to govern circumstance. He feeds Tarsus, wins Thaisa without boasting and entrusts Tyre to Helicanus, yet repeated loss eventually silences him. His last movement is not conquest but renewed attention to another person's story.

Marina

Named for the sea on which she is born, Marina survives attempted murder, abduction and sale. Her chief power is verbal: she can perceive what others want from her and redirect the encounter without surrendering herself. The brothel plot tests the limits of the play's idealisation of chastity while showing intelligence as a practical means of survival.

Thaisa

Thaisa chooses Pericles after the Pentapolis tournament and gives birth during the storm. Revived by Cerimon but believing her husband lost, she becomes a priestess of Diana. Her long separation is not passive waiting: she constructs a life within apparent bereavement before the final recognition asks her to reclaim an earlier one.

Gower

Gower is narrator, moral commentator and stage manager. His antique verse gives the performance the air of a story handed down rather than newly invented. He can be severe in judging characters, but his deepest function is practical: he teaches an audience how to cross gaps in geography, time and probability.

Helicanus

Helicanus remains in Tyre when Pericles flees and refuses the crown even when courtiers press it upon him. In a play crowded with rulers who exploit dependence, he represents stewardship without appropriation. His loyalty gives Pericles a political home to which he may return, even when the family story has been broken elsewhere.

Dionyza

Dionyza accepts the young Marina into her household, then grows resentful when Marina outshines her daughter. She commissions the girl's murder and supplies Pericles with an elaborate false account of her death. Her crime is domestic rather than spectacular: guardianship is corrupted by comparison, status anxiety and the wish to control how virtue is seen.

Carry through the text

Questions that stay open

Use them to test a scene, a performance choice or an argument of your own.

01

What changes when the play is read as a collaboration?

Notice shifts in metre, vocabulary, scene construction and emotional pace, but do not assume that every awkward line belongs to Wilkins or every moving one to Shakespeare. The surviving quarto is damaged, and collaboration was ordinary professional practice. Attribution is most useful when it explains working methods and structure rather than becoming a scale of literary worth.

02

Why does the play need Gower?

Compare what Gower narrates with what the actors perform directly. Ask where his moral certainty helps, where it simplifies and what happens when drama suddenly exceeds his summary. His presence makes the play's age and artificiality visible, allowing an audience to believe not because the story is realistic, but because storytelling is being shared openly.

03

Is Marina protected by virtue, language or theatrical convention?

Study her encounters with Leonine, the pirates, the brothel customers, Lysimachus and Pericles. The play repeatedly declares her chastity powerful, but the actual scenes show quick interpretation and rhetorical control doing much of the work. The question opens a useful distinction between the ideal by which other characters praise Marina and the skills by which she keeps herself alive.
