About the work

The voyage, the crime and the retelling.

The account below follows the Mariner from the ice to Life-in-Death, and the storytelling that his punishment demands of him.

The work

What happens.

An Ancient Mariner stops a wedding guest and describes a voyage southwards beyond the equator into mist, snow and ice. When an albatross appears through the fog, the crew welcomes and feeds it. Without explaining why, the Mariner shoots the bird.

The ship escapes the ice but becomes trapped beneath a burning sun. The dead bird is hung around the Mariner’s neck, and a spectral vessel arrives carrying Death and Life-in-Death. He survives the loss of the crew and watches their bodies rise to work the ship.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

His return does not end the punishment. At unpredictable moments he must find another person and tell the story. The poem therefore presents narration itself as burden, penance and uncertain contact.

Coleridge’s later marginal gloss tries to explain the crime and the supernatural powers pursuing the ship. Its confident statements do not always agree with the verse, creating a second interpretive voice on the same page.

The poem in successive editions

The first printed title was ‘The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere’. Its deliberately old-fashioned spelling helped present the poem as a ballad from an earlier period. In the 1800 Lyrical Ballads, much of that spelling changed and the title became ‘The Ancient Mariner’. The poem also moved from the opening of the 1798 collection to a position near the end of the first volume, immediately before ‘Tintern Abbey’.

The material before the poem changed as well. Early versions supplied an ‘Argument’ outlining the voyage. In 1817 Coleridge replaced that introduction with a Latin epigraph from Thomas Burnet concerning unseen beings and the limits of human knowledge. The 1834 text printed by Astor belongs to the end of this process of revision. Its 625 numbered verse lines remain separate from the accompanying 1817 gloss and the edition’s new commentary.

The verse uses the alternating longer and shorter lines associated with the ballad, but the stanza length varies. Four-line stanzas occur beside expanded forms, and repetition appears within as well as between lines. Continuous numbering makes those variations easier to cite without counting the prose gloss as part of the verse.

Wordsworth’s recorded response

Wordsworth appended a notably mixed assessment to the poem in the 1800 edition of Lyrical Ballads. He said that Coleridge had wished to suppress it, while explaining his own reasons for retaining it. His objections concerned the Mariner’s limited individual character, his lack of agency, the connection between events and the accumulation of imagery.

The same note praised the poem’s feeling, its presentation of emotion and its verse. Wordsworth praised the variety achieved in the verse even while judging the metre unsuitable for a long poem. This response is useful evidence of the poem’s earliest reception within the collaboration itself: admiration and disagreement were present before the later marginal gloss, collected editions and subsequent critical reputation.

The Somerset collaboration

Coleridge moved to Nether Stowey in Somerset in 1796. William and Dorothy Wordsworth lived nearby at Alfoxden during the particularly productive period of 1797–1798. The Mariner belongs to the same period as ‘Frost at Midnight’, ‘Fears in Solitude’, ‘The Nightingale’ and the first part of ‘Christabel’.

Lyrical Ballads was published anonymously in 1798, combining work by Coleridge and Wordsworth. Their contributions did not use one uniform kind of narrator or subject: the supernatural voyage appeared beside poems about ordinary encounters, recollection and rural life. Coleridge later discussed their literary conversations in Biographia Literaria. He wrote that account nearly twenty years after the events, and the edition treats it as a memoir.
