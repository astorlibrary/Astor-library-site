Comedy / problem play

Measure for Measure, William Shakespeare

Duke Vincentio announces that he is leaving Vienna and gives Angelo temporary authority, but secretly remains in the city disguised as a friar. Angelo revives a neglected sexual law and sentences Claudio to death for making Juliet pregnant before their marriage has been formally completed. Claudio's sister Isabella, preparing to enter a convent, pleads for mercy. Angelo answers by offering her a corrupt bargain: Claudio may live if Isabella submits to him.

The Duke devises a bed trick in which Mariana, once betrothed to Angelo and then abandoned by him, takes Isabella's place. He also prevents Claudio's execution and substitutes the head of another prisoner. These devices lead to the final scene, where private crimes are made public and Angelo is forced to confront his own judgement. Yet the Duke controls nearly every revelation, Mariana must beg to marry the man who wronged her, and Isabella gives no spoken answer to the Duke's proposal.

This Astor Library Edition presents the complete play with scene summaries, line numbering and explanatory notes. Its supporting material follows the play's language of law, bodies, reputation and exchange; sets Shakespeare's revisions of Cinthio and George Whetstone beside the finished drama; and gives readers a clear route through the questions of consent, punishment, mercy and political authority that make the play so fiercely contested.

Plot and structure

Five stages in the action.

These sections summarise the main developments in the plot and identify changes in power, knowledge and dramatic conflict.

01 · Movement I

The Duke hands Vienna to Angelo

Duke Vincentio claims that Vienna's laws have become objects of ridicule because he has failed to enforce them. He delegates power to Angelo and leaves publicly, although he remains in disguise to watch what follows. Angelo immediately makes an example of Claudio, turning an old statute into a death sentence.

02 · Movement II

Isabella enters the argument

Lucio brings news of Claudio's arrest to Isabella at the convent. Her first appeal to Angelo is reluctant, but she finds a powerful language of mercy as the encounter continues. Angelo is moved in the worst possible way: he discovers desire, then treats his office and Isabella's vulnerable position as means of satisfying it.

03 · Movement III

The bargain and the prison

Angelo demands sex in exchange for Claudio's life and assures Isabella that his public reputation will defeat her account. Claudio initially accepts her refusal, then panics at the thought of death and asks her to yield. Listening in disguise, the Duke proposes that Mariana take Isabella's place and receive Angelo's promised token.

04 · Movement IV

The bed trick becomes a head trick

Mariana meets Angelo under cover of darkness, fulfilling the terms of their former betrothal without exposing Isabella. Angelo nevertheless orders Claudio's immediate execution. The Provost helps the Duke save him, and the conveniently timed death of the prisoner Ragozine provides a substitute head with which to deceive Angelo.

05 · Movement V

A public reckoning

The Duke returns in his own person and stages a hearing in which Isabella and Mariana accuse Angelo. He briefly appears to reject their testimony before revealing his disguise and Claudio's survival. Angelo is made to marry Mariana, Lucio is punished with marriage, and the Duke proposes to Isabella. The text ends without recording her answer.

Critical reading

Three analyses of language and form.

Each analysis uses evidence from specific scenes, speeches and dramatic choices.

Act 2, Scene 2

Isabella pleads for Claudio

Isabella begins by separating the condemned act from the person who committed it: punish the fault, she argues, without taking the offender's life. Her speech gathers strength through antithesis and repetition, moving from a private request to a general warning about authority. When she imagines “man, proud man” performing absurd tricks in borrowed power, Angelo's courtroom becomes a test of every ruler who mistakes office for moral superiority.

The scene is sharpened by the fact that Isabella's eloquence does not simply soften Angelo. It awakens the appetite that his severe public identity has concealed even from himself. Shakespeare lets a speech for mercy produce danger, not because Isabella argues badly, but because Angelo converts her words and presence into material for his own fantasy. The movement from public hearing to private soliloquy exposes the split between his judgement of others and his treatment of himself.

Act 2, Scene 4

“Who will believe thee, Isabel?”

Angelo's proposition is an act of sexual extortion expressed through riddles, conditionals and legal pressure. He makes Isabella name what he wants while trying to keep his own language clean. Once she understands, she trusts that truth can expose him. His reply—“Who will believe thee, Isabel?”—identifies the real weapon at his disposal: not desire alone, but a public reputation strong enough to discredit the person he harms.

The scene therefore turns on unequal control of evidence. Angelo has office, gender, status and an established name; Isabella has only testimony that he predicts will sound impossible. His threat remains disturbingly recognisable because he explains exactly how institutional credibility can protect abuse. A useful performance choice is whether Angelo discovers this argument in panic or delivers it with prior confidence: the former reveals rapid corruption, while the latter suggests that severity has always been a form of concealment.

Act 5, Scene 1

Judgement, pardon and Isabella's silence

The last scene resembles a trial, but the Duke has written the evidence, arranged the witnesses and decided when each fact will become known. Isabella is first made to look incredible; Mariana is unveiled; Angelo is sentenced, pardoned and married; Claudio is produced alive. This sequence restores social order through theatrical surprise, yet it also demonstrates how completely Vincentio controls the speech and knowledge of everyone around him.

Isabella's decision to join Mariana's plea for Angelo is one of the scene's hardest turns. It may show mercy freely chosen, loyalty to Mariana, obedience to the Duke's design or an impossible demand placed upon a traumatised woman. The Duke then proposes marriage and receives no answer preserved in the text. That silence is not a blank that can be filled with certainty. On stage it may register joy, shock, refusal, exhaustion or guarded calculation, and each choice changes the meaning of the comedy.

Text, sources and performance

Publication and performance context.

This section covers the play's date, sources, early printed texts and relevant performance history.

1604

A new reign and a problem of government

The play belongs to the opening years of James I's reign and was performed before the court in December 1604. Its interest in delegation, surveillance, neglected law and the moral character of a ruler would have been pointed in that setting. The Duke should not be reduced to a portrait of James, however. Shakespeare constructs a broader political experiment: a governor withdraws from visible responsibility, watches the results in secret and then claims the authority to repair damage he helped to create.

Sources

How Shakespeare changes the inherited story

Cinthio's Epitia narrative and Whetstone's Promos and Cassandra provide the corrupt magistrate, the condemned brother and the demand for sex. Shakespeare's decisive changes include Mariana and the bed trick, Claudio's concealed survival and Isabella's silence after the Duke proposes. These revisions prevent the story from becoming a simple account of a virtuous woman defeating a villain. They distribute responsibility among a compromised ruler, an abusive deputy and a social order that uses marriage as cure, proof and punishment.

On stage

The ending a production must interpret

The written text does not explain Isabella's silence, guarantee romantic conversion in Angelo or tell an audience how to feel about the Duke. Productions must make those absences visible. The Duke can appear wise, intrusive, politically calculating or increasingly overwhelmed by his own scheme; Isabella may accept his proposal, reject it or simply remain unable to respond. Modern stagings have also made the prison, sexual coercion and imbalance of institutional credibility central rather than treating them as obstacles on the way to comic marriages.

Characters

Principal characters and dramatic functions.

Duke Vincentio

The Duke dislikes public hostility but remains fascinated by control. Disguised as Friar Lodowick, he gathers confessions, gives spiritual counsel and manipulates events while avoiding open accountability. His plan saves Claudio and exposes Angelo, yet it also causes Isabella needless grief and turns other people's lives into a demonstration of his governing intelligence.

Isabella

A novice preparing to join the Poor Clares, Isabella values chastity as a religious commitment and a form of self-possession. Her moral language is exacting, but she is not emotionally static: she pleads, recoils, grieves, collaborates and finally asks mercy for Angelo. Her unanswered silence at the close is one of Shakespeare's most consequential absences.

Angelo

Angelo builds his identity upon restraint and then assumes that restraint proves virtue. Once attracted to Isabella, he uses the law he represents to threaten her, breaks his promise to spare Claudio and attempts to remove the evidence. His hypocrisy is not simply that he desires; it lies in condemning others while making his own desire coercive.

Claudio

Claudio is sentenced because Juliet is pregnant before their marriage formalities have been completed. His terror in prison gives death a physical immediacy that the rulers' arguments lack. When he asks Isabella to save him at the cost of her own choice, fear makes him both painfully human and capable of passing pressure down to someone with less power.

Mariana

Once betrothed to Angelo, Mariana was rejected after her dowry was lost at sea. The bed trick lets her reclaim a relationship recognised by the play's social logic, but it also binds her future to the man who abandoned and abused her. Her plea for Angelo's life may express love, economic necessity, religious mercy or all three at once.

Lucio

Lucio carries news, prompts Isabella's first appeal and keeps the play's high moral claims in contact with Vienna's sexual life. His slanders of the disguised Duke are comic because he speaks them to their subject, but his punishment exposes the ruler's wounded vanity. He is perceptive about other people's pretence while rarely accepting responsibility for his own.

Study questions

Questions for analysis and discussion.

Each question requires evidence from the text and can be used for discussion, notes or essay planning.

01

Does the Duke repair Vienna, or prove how much power he can exercise over it?

Trace what Vincentio knows, what he withholds and when he could have intervened sooner. Set the lives he saves against the fear he permits Isabella and Claudio to suffer. The question becomes stronger if the final scene is treated not as an automatic resolution but as a performance designed by the Duke to restore his public authority.

02

Where does the play locate Isabella's freedom?

Compare her choice of the convent, her refusal of Angelo, her anger with Claudio, her participation in the bed trick and her final silence. These moments do not produce one simple portrait of rigidity or liberation. They show a woman defending bodily and spiritual autonomy while powerful men repeatedly decide what story her actions will be made to serve.

03

Is mercy applied equally?

List every punishment threatened and every punishment carried out. Claudio, Angelo, Barnardine and Lucio receive strikingly different treatment, while Juliet, Mariana, Isabella and Kate Keepdown live with consequences that the formal judgements barely address. The pattern reveals that mercy in the play is never only a virtue: it is also a privilege exercised by whoever controls the sentence.
