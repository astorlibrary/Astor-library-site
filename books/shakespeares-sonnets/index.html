About the work

The 1609 sequence, complete.

The account below follows all 154 sonnets in the quarto order, from the opening marriage plea to the dark-haired woman and the closing Cupid myths.

The work

What happens.

From Sonnet 18, poetry becomes another defence against age and death. The central sequence follows an unequal relationship through admiration, absence, jealousy, betrayal, forgiveness and rivalry with another poet.

The poems examine love, beauty, mortality, rank, desire and the claim that verse may outlast the people it represents. Even the best-known sonnets are parts of a sequence that changes as it goes.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

One sonnet appears complete on every page. A short headnote explains the direction of its argument; notes at the foot of that page define difficult words, clarify passages and identify genuine textual uncertainty.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

Earlier printings and a later rearrangement

The 1609 quarto was not the first appearance in print of every poem. Versions of Sonnets 138 and 144 had appeared in The Passionate Pilgrim in 1599, a miscellany whose title page attributed the whole collection to Shakespeare although it included work by other writers. The earlier versions provide evidence of textual variation before the complete sequence was printed.

John Benson’s 1640 edition presented a substantially different arrangement, regrouping poems and supplying titles. Following the 1609 order keeps the arrangement of that first printing; later editions have sometimes reordered the sonnets. The dedication signed by Thomas Thorpe belongs to that publication history; it does not securely identify the person behind every address within the poems.

The usual form and its exceptions

Most poems have fourteen lines in iambic pentameter, with three alternating-rhyme quatrains followed by a rhyming couplet: ABAB CDCD EFEF GG. A quatrain can extend an example, qualify an assertion or introduce a new comparison. The couplet may state a conclusion, but it can also change the force of what came before. Punctuation and the movement of a sentence across line endings are therefore as useful as the rhyme scheme when following an argument.

Three conspicuous exceptions prevent this pattern from becoming an inflexible rule. Sonnet 99 has fifteen lines because its opening unit contains five. Sonnet 126 comprises twelve lines arranged as six couplets. Sonnet 145 is in four-beat lines; the others have five. These are features of the transmitted poems, not gaps or mistakes that an editor should automatically regularise.

Reading linked poems in small groups

Some of the most useful comparisons are between immediate neighbours. Sonnets 27 and 28 connect daytime labour with wakefulness at night. Sonnets 97, 98 and 99 return to separation through winter, April and flowers, but the season described and the speaker’s emotional account do not always coincide. The sequence supplies repeated material on which to test changes of emphasis.

Other poems place pressure on familiar conventions of praise. Sonnet 130 lists comparisons the speaker declines to make about his mistress, then affirms his attachment in the couplet. Sonnet 138 describes a relationship sustained by statements both parties know are unreliable. These poems are useful alongside more frequently quoted declarations because the collection includes scepticism, embarrassment, negotiation and self-accusation as well as claims of constancy. The labels used for groups of sonnets are aids to reading, not a substitute for the wording of each poem.
