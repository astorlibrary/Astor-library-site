About the work

From the Ghost’s command to the poisoned match.

The account below follows the play from the Ghost’s accusation to the fencing match Claudius prepares, as private grief becomes a crisis of evidence, surveillance, performance and succession.

The work

What happens.

Hamlet returns to Denmark after his father’s death and finds Claudius on the throne and married to Gertrude. A Ghost resembling the dead king claims that Claudius murdered him and commands revenge.

Hamlet adopts an antic disposition, tests the court and uses travelling actors to stage a murder resembling the Ghost’s account. Claudius’s reaction appears to confirm guilt, but action continues to produce uncertainty, accidental killing and wider revenge.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

By Hamlet’s return from England, Ophelia is dead, Laertes seeks vengeance and Claudius has prepared a poisoned fencing match. The play turns private grief into a crisis of evidence, surveillance, performance and political succession.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

Dating the three printed witnesses

The First Quarto appeared in 1603. It is much shorter than the later versions and includes different character names: Polonius appears as Corambis and Reynaldo as Montano. It also arranges some material differently. Describing it simply as an abridgement misses differences in wording and dramatic sequence whose origins remain disputed.

The Second Quarto survives with title pages dated 1604 and 1605. It was printed by James Roberts for Nicholas Ling and advertised its enlarged text as based on a true and perfect copy. That wording is the publisher’s claim on the title page. The Folio of 1623 supplies another text, with both omissions and additions in relation to Q2, as well as hundreds of smaller differences.

What a conflated reading text can contain

Conflation brings material from more than one early witness into a continuous edition, but it cannot print mutually exclusive readings in the same position. An editor still has to choose between alternative words and decide how to place passages absent from one version. The result should not be mistaken for a surviving manuscript in that combined form.

This distinction is particularly relevant to the Astor volume’s stated Q2 basis and inclusion of important Folio material. A useful comparison begins with a specific line or passage, records which early text contains it and then asks what changes when the alternative is read. Claims about Shakespearean revision, theatrical cutting or imperfect transmission require evidence beyond the mere existence of a difference.

The court’s papers and the play’s documents

Writing has a practical role throughout Hamlet. Polonius reads Hamlet’s letter to Ophelia before the king and queen. Hamlet writes in his tables after the Ghost’s departure. Later he obtains the commission carrying Claudius’s instructions to England and substitutes another, using his father’s signet to seal it. Letters delivered to Horatio report the interruption of the voyage and Hamlet’s return.

These documents differ in authorship, audience and control. The love letter becomes evidence when removed from its original exchange; the commission carries an order meant to be obeyed abroad; Hamlet’s replacement depends on the authority of a seal. Following who writes, carries, opens and reads each document offers a concrete route through the play’s treatment of knowledge and power. The account of the voyage is itself delivered retrospectively, so the reader should distinguish the actions Hamlet reports from events presented directly before the theatre audience.
