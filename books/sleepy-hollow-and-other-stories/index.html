About the work

Twelve American stories.

The account below names the writers and their works, from Irving to Lovecraft, none of them written for the holiday that later claimed them.

The work

What happens.

None of the twelve stories was originally written for Halloween. Their place in the holiday developed later as ghosts, witches, haunted houses, black cats and supernatural storytelling became part of American October.

Washington Irving is represented by The Legend of Sleepy Hollow and The Devil and Tom Walker; Nathaniel Hawthorne by Young Goodman Brown and The Minister’s Black Veil.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

The collection also includes Poe’s The Fall of the House of Usher, The Black Cat and The Tell-Tale Heart; Bierce’s The Boarded Window and The Damned Thing; Gilman’s The Yellow Wall-Paper; Freeman’s The Shadows on the Wall; and Lovecraft’s The Outsider.

Story notes return each work to its original publication setting, while the introduction and chronology explain how American Halloween customs developed during the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

Sleepy Hollow

The Legend of Sleepy Hollow appeared in 1820 as part of Washington Irving’s Sketch Book. Its rural New York community has a remembered Dutch past, local stories about the Revolutionary War and a schoolmaster from Connecticut who listens readily to supernatural accounts. The Headless Horseman is introduced through the community’s storytelling before Ichabod Crane encounters what he believes to be the apparition.

The autumn gathering at the Van Tassels’ supplies food, music, dancing and further stories before Ichabod’s journey home. Those details make the tale especially appropriate to an autumn collection, but Irving does not identify the occasion as a Halloween party. The narrative also preserves competing explanations for Ichabod’s disappearance. Its final reports and the teller’s postscript matter when distinguishing the written story from adaptations that settle the mystery differently.

Kinds of story

The collection’s haunted houses, apparitions and violent confessions do not all operate in the same way. Poe’s The Tell-Tale Heart and The Black Cat give their accounts through narrators implicated in the violence they describe. The Fall of the House of Usher instead brings an observer into another household. Comparing who tells the story, what that person has seen and what they attempt to explain is a useful route through the three selections.

Gilman’s The Yellow Wall-Paper, first published in 1892, records a woman’s experience of confinement and treatment through writing she conceals from those around her. Lovecraft’s The Outsider, published in Weird Tales in April 1926, follows an isolated narrator whose understanding of his situation changes through an encounter with other people. Their inclusion extends the collection beyond conventional tales of a ghost returning to a familiar home.

The stories and the holiday’s history

American Halloween took its present form gradually; it is not one tradition handed down unchanged. Seasonal parties, games, disguises, commercial decorations and organised trick-or-treating have different histories. Evidence from a nineteenth-century story therefore cannot establish that the full modern set of customs was already in place when it was written.

The anthology’s publication histories and chronology support two related sequences: the development of the short fiction and the later uses of that fiction in seasonal reading. A reader can follow the stories in their original contexts, compare particular devices across writers, or choose a group for an October reading session. The twelve complete texts remain the centre of the collection, with the historical material explaining the later seasonal connections.
