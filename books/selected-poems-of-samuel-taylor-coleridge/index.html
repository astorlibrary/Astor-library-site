The selection

The volume includes ‘The Rime of the Ancient Mariner’, both parts of ‘Christabel’, ‘Kubla Khan’, ‘Dejection: An Ode’, ‘Frost at Midnight’, ‘The Eolian Harp’ and ‘This Lime-Tree Bower My Prison’. It extends beyond these frequently anthologised works to political verse, short lyrics, satires and poems written or published late in Coleridge’s life.

Seven poems open the selection’s initial narrative and ballad group, from the Mariner to ‘The Three Graves’. Later contents include ‘France: An Ode’, ‘Fears in Solitude’, ‘Youth and Age’, ‘Work Without Hope’, ‘The Pains of Sleep’, ‘Limbo’ and the concluding ‘Epitaph’. The forty-three-poem count describes this selected volume, rather than a complete edition of Coleridge’s poetry.

The source text

The poems follow Arthur Symons’s Poems of Coleridge, published by Methuen in 1905. Symons drew on the text established by James Dykes Campbell for the Poetical Works of 1893. The Astor edition identifies this transmission explicitly and follows the selection’s order while adding its own editorial apparatus.

Spelling, punctuation and capitalisation are retained rather than modernised. The textual note records corrections to demonstrable transcription faults, including misread dates and verse lines broken by the electronic source’s fixed line length. It also distinguishes such corrections from the edition’s decisions about the presentation of glosses, footnotes and line numbers.

The Ancient Mariner and its gloss

Coleridge first published the Mariner in Lyrical Ballads in 1798 and revised it for later appearances. The marginal prose gloss was added in Sibylline Leaves in 1817. Symons transferred that gloss to an appendix; this edition returns it to an outer column opposite the stanzas it describes.

Coleridge’s own footnote acknowledging Wordsworth in Part IV is also printed at the foot of its page. These are authorial elements, distinct from the explanatory notes supplied by the Astor editor. Their placement allows the verse, Coleridge’s later commentary and the new annotations to be consulted together without confusing their authorship.

Numbering and explanatory apparatus

Line numbers appear at intervals of five and continue across the parts of an individual poem. They count the lines as printed in this edition. The textual note explains that differences from another edition’s numbering may arise from lineation, especially in irregular poems, and should not automatically be read as missing text.

Editorial footnotes are numbered afresh for each poem. Historical and contextual material accompanies the poetry, supported by a glossary and bibliography. The jointly written ‘The Devil’s Thoughts’ is identified as a work by Coleridge and Robert Southey; its inclusion does not make Southey the author of the other forty-two poems.

Coleridge’s life and literary circle

Samuel Taylor Coleridge was born at Ottery St Mary in 1772. His association with William and Dorothy Wordsworth in Somerset was central to the period in which he wrote the Mariner, the first part of ‘Christabel’ and several of the conversation poems. Lyrical Ballads appeared anonymously in 1798.

The edition’s biographical and contextual chapters also cover Southey, Charles Lamb, Coleridge’s journalism, his political interests and his later life at Highgate. A separate medical-history discussion addresses opium through the historical record. These chapters provide circumstances for reading individual poems without reducing every work to an episode in the poet’s biography.

Where the poems first appeared

The publication history follows the poems through collected books, pamphlets, newspapers and gift annuals. Important volumes include Poems on Various Subjects in 1796, Lyrical Ballads, the 1816 publication of Christabel with ‘Kubla Khan’ and ‘The Pains of Sleep’, and Sibylline Leaves in 1817.

Nine poems in the selection first appeared in daily newspapers. A dedicated section records that aspect of Coleridge’s work, alongside chronology, sources and influences, language and style, critical perspectives and reception history. The resulting apparatus connects particular poems to their first audiences and subsequent textual history, while preserving the selected poems themselves in full.
