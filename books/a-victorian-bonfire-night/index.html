A Victorian Bonfire Night

Until 1859 the Fifth of November was a holiday fixed by Act of Parliament, with its own service in the Prayer Book. This collection gathers Victorian stories, essays, folklore and newspaper reports from the years when it became something else: children with guys, bonfire societies, fireworks and bonfire toffee.

About the work

The Fifth of November across the nineteenth century.

Reprinted writing by Halliwell, Jerrold, Ainsworth, Paget, Chambers, Thiselton-Dyer and Hardy joins an 1888 newspaper survey and original chapters on the Plot, the Guy, fireworks, food and policing.

The work

What happens.

A Victorian Bonfire Night traces the Fifth of November through the nineteenth century, as statutory Protestant observance increasingly gave way to children’s customs, organised bonfire societies, commercial fireworks and public entertainment.

The primary texts include James Orchard Halliwell’s Fifth of November rhyme, Douglas Jerrold’s recollections, four chapters from William Harrison Ainsworth’s Guy Fawkes, Francis Edward Paget’s The Bonfire and Robert Chambers’s account of Guy Fawkes’s Day.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

T. F. Thiselton-Dyer’s regional customs, Thomas Hardy’s Egdon Heath bonfire chapter from The Return of the Native and an 1888 provincial newspaper survey extend the record across genre and place.

Original chapters examine the Plot, legislation and repeal, pope-burning, the Guy, fuel gathering, firework manufacture, the Papal Aggression, disturbances, parkin, bonfire toffee, policing and regional carnival.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

1605, 1606 and the making of an anniversary

The Plot and its commemoration belong to different stages of the history. The conspirators intended to attack the opening of Parliament on 5 November 1605. After the discovery, Parliament met in January 1606 and established an annual thanksgiving. Services and sermons therefore helped reproduce an official account of deliverance long before the Victorian period examined in this collection. The date became a recurring occasion on which political and religious loyalties were publicly expressed.

The statutory observance remained in force until 1859. Its repeal is a useful dividing point within the nineteenth-century material, but it does not imply that local festivities immediately stopped. Legal obligation, church observance, street entertainment and family custom followed different timetables. The chronology makes it possible to place a particular source on the appropriate side of this change.

Historical romance and the Victorian view of 1605

Ainsworth’s Guy Fawkes identifies itself as an historical romance. Its dedication is dated 26 July 1841, placing its treatment of the conspiracy firmly within Victorian publishing. A historical novel can use recognisable names, locations and political events while inventing conversations, motives and private encounters. The four chapters included here should consequently be read as evidence of nineteenth-century storytelling about the Plot, alongside the separate historical account.

This distinction also matters when reading a remembered childhood scene, a folklore collection or a newspaper survey. A reminiscence looks back from a later date; a collector selects and classifies customs; a reporter describes events for a particular readership. Their differences in purpose help explain why the anthology places several kinds of writing together rather than treating any single account as a national survey.

Hardy’s Egdon Heath and the scale of the custom

The Return of the Native opens its human action among Fifth of November fires on Egdon Heath. In the novel, neighbouring fires can be seen across the dark landscape, people gather and talk beside them, and a separate fire becomes a signal between Eustacia Vye and Wildeve. Hardy thus gives the observance both a communal setting and a particular place within the plot.

The Egdon material offers a concrete comparison with the regional customs elsewhere in the collection. Notice who collects fuel, who gathers, what the fire makes visible and what conversation takes place around it. The edition’s separate treatment of food, manufacture and policing then supplies other records against which a fictional gathering can be considered.
