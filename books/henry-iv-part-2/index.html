William Shakespeare

Henry IV, Part 2

Victory at Shrewsbury has not healed the kingdom. Rebellion continues, Henry IV’s health fails, and Prince Hal moves towards a crown that will transform every relationship around him. Falstaff waits for royal favour; the play asks what must be rejected when a public future begins.

Henry IV, Part 2, William Shakespeare

Rumour opens the play by telling Northumberland a false story about Shrewsbury. That beginning sets the method: news arrives late or distorted while sick bodies and exhausted institutions struggle to keep moving.

Falstaff travels among the law courts, taverns and Gloucestershire recruits. The rebels are defeated through Prince John’s cold promise and withdrawal. At Westminster, Hal’s taking of the crown becomes a painful misunderstanding before father and son reconcile. When Hal becomes king, his public rejection of Falstaff turns reform into an act with a human cost.

A sequel that changes the rhythm

The play revisits familiar figures but makes age, sickness and belatedness part of its form.

  • Rumour’s induction A personified Rumour begins the play wearing tongues and turning report into theatre.
  • Qa and Qb Two forms of the 1600 quarto survive; Qb includes material absent from Qa, including Act 3, Scene 1.
  • Folio differences The 1623 text alters, cuts and adds material, giving editors a complex textual history.
  • Old friends, older bodies Falstaff’s age, the king’s illness and Shallow’s memories make time newly visible.
  • A quiet rebellion The Archbishop’s rising ends through negotiation and arrest rather than a second Shrewsbury.
  • Justice scenes The Lord Chief Justice represents a law that must survive the change of king.
  • Country sequence Shallow and Silence expand the social world beyond court, tavern and battlefield.
  • Epilogue The speaker promises Henry V and acknowledges the disputed identity of Falstaff and Oldcastle.

    • Five movements towards a new reign

    The political plot and Falstaff plot draw closer until the coronation joins them.

    Acts 1–2

    News, debt and disappointment

    Northumberland learns the truth about Hotspur’s death. Falstaff quarrels with the Chief Justice, recruits hope from Hal’s future and returns to the Boar’s Head, where age and appetite no longer feel safely comic.

    Act 3

    The king cannot sleep

    Henry walks at night under the burden of rule while Shallow and Silence remember their youth. The scenes place a monarch’s sleeplessness beside ordinary lives shaped by time, boasting and loss.

    Act 4

    Gaultree and Westminster

    Prince John promises to hear the rebels’ grievances, lets them dismiss their forces and arrests them. In London, Hal thinks his father dead and takes the crown; the king wakes to read the act as ambition.

    Reconciliation

    Father, son and the uneasy crown

    Hal explains his grief, and Henry advises him before dying in the Jerusalem Chamber. The prophecy that he would die in Jerusalem is fulfilled through a room’s name, not a crusade.

    Act 5

    “I know thee not, old man”

    Henry V confirms the Chief Justice, signalling continuity under law, then rejects Falstaff in public. Falstaff insists the rebuke is part of a performance, but the audience can see that the old intimacy has ended.

    Characters and institutions

    The play tests whether office, friendship or memory can survive a transfer of power.

  • King Henry IV A ruler physically consumed by the crown he seized, unable to separate a son’s grief from political ambition.
  • Prince Hal / Henry V Less visible than in Part 1, but decisive at the end; he must make private change legible as public rule.
  • Sir John Falstaff Still brilliant and exploitative, living on credit and expectation while the future closes around him.
  • Lord Chief Justice The embodiment of lawful office, willing to punish the prince and then explain that duty to the king.
  • Prince John of Lancaster Politically effective and morally chilling in his handling of the rebels at Gaultree.
  • Northumberland Drawn again towards rebellion but divided by grief, caution and the persuasive Lady Northumberland.
  • Justice Shallow A Gloucestershire magistrate whose memories, recruits and hospitality expose both affection and self-deception.
  • Doll Tearsheet and Mistress Quickly Women whose labour and vulnerability complicate the apparent freedom of the tavern world.

    • Text, history and interpretation

    The play’s textual variations matter because they change political and emotional emphasis.

    1600 quarto

    Two states of an early edition

    The first printed edition survives in forms that modern editors call Qa and Qb. Their differences, together with the Folio, make the play a strong example of why Shakespeare’s texts cannot be reduced to one untouched original.

    Chronicle history

    Hall and Holinshed

    Shakespeare draws the political sequence from English chronicles, but the play’s deepest inventions are theatrical: Rumour’s opening, the tavern’s fading community and the private scene around the crown.

    Justice

    Law beyond personality

    The Chief Justice’s survival into the new reign offers an alternative to government by affection. Henry V’s endorsement of him makes continuity under law the hopeful counterpart to Falstaff’s rejection.

    Rejection

    Public necessity or betrayal?

    Hal has announced since Part 1 that he will change. The final scene nevertheless refuses an easy triumph: the audience knows both why the king acts and what his act does to the person who once helped him rehearse kingship.

    Stage and screen

    Productions often join the two Henry IV plays so that Hal’s transformation and Falstaff’s loss form one continuous arc.

    1965

    Chimes at Midnight

    Orson Welles shaped material from the history plays around Falstaff, played by Welles himself. The film makes the rejection the emotional destination of a relationship already shadowed by the coming crown.

    2012

    The Hollow Crown

    Richard Eyre’s BBC adaptations cast Jeremy Irons as Henry IV, Tom Hiddleston as Hal and Simon Russell Beale as Falstaff. The two films preserve the movement from Shrewsbury to coronation.

    Combined performance

    One long reckoning

    Condensed versions can make Hal and Falstaff the clear centre, but directors must decide what to keep from Gaultree, Gloucestershire and the women’s tavern scenes—the material that gives Part 2 its different moral weather.
