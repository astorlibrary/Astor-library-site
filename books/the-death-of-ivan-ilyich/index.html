A judge’s life and death

The story opens with the news. In a break during a trial, Ivan Ilych Golovin’s colleagues read that he has died, and each thinks first of the promotions his death will bring. At the funeral his old friend Peter Ivanovich struggles with a springy pouffe while the widow asks how to get more money from the government, and then he goes on to his game of bridge.

Tolstoy then goes back over the life. Ivan Ilych was a capable lawyer who did what people of good position did, married suitably, kept his marriage at arm’s length and furnished his house to look like everyone else’s. A knock against a window frame while he is hanging curtains brings on an illness his doctors cannot name. His family pretend he is only ill; the peasant servant Gerasim alone tells him the truth and nurses him. In his last days he asks whether his whole life has been wrong, and two hours before he dies he finds light where he expected death.

How Tolstoy wrote it

Tolstoy had the story in mind from 1881, when Ivan Ilyich Mechnikov, a judicial official in Tula and brother of the biologist Ilya Mechnikov, died at forty-five. Accounts of what he said on his deathbed about the emptiness of his life reached Tolstoy through his sister-in-law Tatyana Kuzminskaya. In April 1884 Tolstoy noted in his diary that he wanted to begin something new, perhaps the death of a judge. He started it as Ivan Ilych’s own diary, changed to the third person in the autumn of 1885, and dated the finished manuscript 25 March 1886.

It was his first substantial fiction since Anna Karenina. In the years between he had gone through the crisis described in A Confession and written little except religious and moral works, and Aylmer Maude later recalled critics greeting the story as the return of the artist. It appeared in 1886 in the twelfth volume of Tolstoy’s collected Works, published by his wife Sofia Tolstaya.

Law, rank and medicine

Ivan Ilych is a product of Alexander II’s Russia. The judicial statutes of 1864 created courts independent of the administration, with public trials and juries, and the new courts needed new men; Ivan Ilych becomes one, as an examining magistrate, then a prosecutor and finally a member of the Court of Justice. Tolstoy follows his career in grades and salaries: the tenth rank when he leaves the School of Law, 3,500 roubles a year when he feels hard done by, and 5,000 when a change at the ministry rescues him.

His illness belongs to the medicine of the 1880s. The doctors speak of a floating kidney, chronic catarrh and the vermiform appendix, nobody names what is killing him, and readers have guessed at cancer ever since. What the story records exactly is the experience: the taste in the mouth, the pain in the side, opium and morphine, and doctors who, like the judge himself, have decided in advance which questions are relevant.

Gerasim and the lie

What torments Ivan Ilych most is the lie that he is only ill. His wife, his daughter, his colleagues and his doctors keep it up out of the same decorum he lived by, and it reduces his dying to an unpleasant incident, like someone bringing a bad smell into a drawing-room. The one person who does not lie is Gerasim, the butler’s young assistant, who carries out his master’s excretions without disgust and holds his legs on his shoulders through the night, saying that we shall all come to it.

The last chapters move from satire to something harder to name. Ivan Ilych rages at God, reviews his life and finds that everything good in it lies in childhood. When he admits that his life was not the right thing, and feels sorry for his son and his wife, his fear leaves him. Readers disagree about the light he sees, whether it is grace, a dying man’s vision or Tolstoy’s doctrine, and the story leaves them to decide.

Readers

The first readers were overwhelmed. The critic Vladimir Stasov wrote in April 1886 that no nation anywhere had a work of such genius, and in July Tchaikovsky noted in his diary that he was more convinced than ever that Tolstoy was the greatest of writers.

Its later readers include philosophers and doctors as well as novelists. Vladimir Nabokov ranked it among Tolstoy’s most perfect works. Martin Heidegger cited it in a footnote to Being and Time (1927), in his discussion of how people treat death as something that happens to someone else, and it has long been read by students of medicine for its account of how the living fail the dying. Akira Kurosawa’s film Ikiru (1952) borrows its situation for a Tokyo official with cancer.

The Maudes’ translation and this edition

Louise and Aylmer Maude knew Tolstoy and spent decades translating him. Their version of this story appeared in the Oxford Centenary Edition of his works that they edited, in the volume Iván Ilých and Hadji Murád (1935). Their English is plain and careful, like Tolstoy’s own, and they keep the moment near the end when Ivan Ilych tries to say forgive me and it comes out as forego, their rendering of a slip between two similar Russian words.

Here the Maudes’ translation appears complete, with chapter summaries and explanatory footnotes. The supporting material covers Tolstoy, the judicial reforms of 1864, nineteenth-century medicine, the chronology of Ivan Ilych’s career, the story’s language, publication and critical reception, with character studies and a glossary.
