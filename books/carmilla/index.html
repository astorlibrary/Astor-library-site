Sheridan Le Fanu

Carmilla

In a lonely castle in Styria, Laura and her father take in a beautiful stranger whose carriage has overturned at their gate, and the young women of the district begin to die. Sheridan Le Fanu’s vampire novella ran in The Dark Blue in 1871 and 1872; this edition prints the 1872 book text with its Prologue.

Laura and her guest

Laura is nineteen and lives with her English father, retired from the Austrian service, in a castle in the Styrian forest. The nearest inhabited village is seven miles away, and her company is two governesses and the occasional visitor. One moonlit evening a carriage overturns at the gate, and a stately lady in black velvet, pleading a journey of life and death, leaves her stunned daughter in their care. Laura knows the girl’s face. At the age of six she woke in the night to find it beside her in bed, and felt two needles run into her breast.

Carmilla will say only that her name is Carmilla, that her family is ancient and noble and that her home lies to the west. She comes down at one in the afternoon, eats nothing, avoids the family prayers, and moves between girlish chatter and passionate declarations that thrill and frighten Laura. A restored portrait dated 1698 turns out to be her exact likeness. Peasant girls in the district sicken and die, Laura begins to fail, and General Spielsdorf arrives with the story of how his ward died after he took in a girl called Millarca. He tells it on the road to the ruined chapel of the Karnsteins, where Carmilla walks in.

Le Fanu and Doctor Hesselius

Joseph Sheridan Le Fanu was born in Dublin in 1814 into a family of Huguenot descent; his grandmother was a sister of the playwright Richard Brinsley Sheridan. He was called to the Irish bar in 1839 but made no serious attempt to practise, and worked as a journalist and newspaper proprietor. In 1861 he bought the Dublin University Magazine, which he also edited. After his wife Susanna died in 1858 he withdrew from society, and most of the fiction he is remembered for came afterwards, including The House by the Churchyard (1863) and Uncle Silas (1864). He died in Dublin in February 1873.

In a Glass Darkly (1872), the book in which Carmilla appeared the year before his death, presents each of its five tales as a case from the papers of Doctor Martin Hesselius, a German physician. In the Prologue to Carmilla an unnamed editor explains that Hesselius wrote a learned note on the narrative, declines to summarise it, and adds that the lady who wrote the narrative has since died. Readers are left with her account and without the doctor’s explanation.

Vampires in the Habsburg lands

Le Fanu’s vampire comes out of a real panic. In the 1720s and 1730s, Austrian officials in the part of Serbia then ruled by the Habsburgs reported villages where the dead were said to leave their graves and suck the blood of the living, among them Peter Plogojowitz of Kisiljevo and Arnold Paole of Medveđa. Their accounts of opened graves, stakes and burnings were reprinted across Europe, and in 1746 the Benedictine scholar Augustin Calmet gathered them into a treatise on the revenants of Hungary, Bohemia, Moravia and Silesia.

Le Fanu read Calmet closely, probably in Henry Christmas’s English translation, The Phantom World (1850). The woodman’s tale in chapter XIII, of a stranger who watches from the church tower as a vampire leaves its grave, takes its linen and beheads it, follows Calmet’s account of the Moravian village of Liebava, down to the vampire returning from his prowlings. Laura’s appeal to commissions innumerable is true to the record, though the empire itself drew the opposite conclusion: in 1755 the Empress Maria Theresa, advised by her physician Gerard van Swieten, made the desecration of graves as a defence against vampires a punishable offence. The story is set in Styria, an old Habsburg duchy whose capital, Graz, Le Fanu spells Gratz.

Christabel, and a love story

Critics have long read Carmilla beside Coleridge’s Christabel (1816), in which a motherless girl brings a mysterious lady, found in distress in the woods at night, into her widowed father’s castle and falls under her spell. Arthur H. Nethercot set out the parallels in 1949. Carmilla arrives in the same way, a beautiful guest in distress, welcomed by a courteous father, who comes to her young hostess at night.

What sets the story apart is the frankness of its central relationship. Laura describes a love growing into adoration and also into abhorrence, and wonders whether a boyish lover is visiting her in disguise; Carmilla calls Laura hers for ever. Many modern readers see here an early portrait of desire between women, which the vampire plot lets Le Fanu write and then punish with a stake. The last paragraph leaves the feeling unresolved: years later Laura still starts from reveries, fancying she hears Carmilla’s light step at the drawing-room door.

The Dark Blue and after

The Dark Blue printed Carmilla first, in four monthly parts from December 1871 to March 1872. John Christian Freund had founded the magazine in March 1871 and named it after the colours of Oxford, his university; leading Pre-Raphaelites were among its contributors, and it folded after two years. The story’s illustrations were by Michael Fitzgerald and David Henry Friston, who in 1887 drew the first pictures of Sherlock Holmes.

Its afterlife began with Bram Stoker, another Dublin writer, for whom Carmilla was one of the sources of Dracula (1897). An episode cut from that novel for length, published after his death as Dracula’s Guest (1914), takes its traveller to the tomb of a Countess Dolingen of Gratz, in Styria. Carl Theodor Dreyer took the bones of the vampire plot in his film Vampyr (1932) from Carmilla, and Hammer Films’ The Vampire Lovers (1970) began a trilogy about the Karnsteins.

The 1872 text and this edition

The text of 1872 closes the third volume of In a Glass Darkly and opens with the Hesselius Prologue. It has a few small slips, which are kept: the governess Madame Perrodon becomes Madame Paradon for a page in chapter VII, Laura’s mother is a Styrian lady in chapter I and of an old Hungarian family in chapter V, and Laura dates her writing eight years after the events in one chapter and more than ten in another.

This Astor edition prints the complete 1872 text, Hesselius Prologue included, alongside chapter summaries and explanatory notes. The supporting material gives historical context on vampires, Styria and the Habsburg world, with critical perspectives, a glossary and a publication history, and adds original material on Le Fanu, eighteenth-century vampire reports, the story’s literary sources, its original magazine publication and its long afterlife.
