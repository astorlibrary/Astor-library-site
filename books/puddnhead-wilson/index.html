Dawson’s Landing and the exchanged children

The novel begins in Dawson’s Landing, a fictional Missouri town on the Mississippi. Roxy, an enslaved woman, secretly exchanges her infant son with her master’s child. The boys grow up occupying one another’s legally assigned positions, while the town continues to treat their supposed ancestry as a reliable basis for identity and status.

David Wilson, a lawyer whose misunderstood joke earns him the name Pudd’nhead, collects fingerprints as a private interest. More than twenty years after the exchange, a murder brings his collection into court. The edition supplies chapter summaries to clarify the sequence of discoveries without substituting a shortened retelling for Twain’s narrative.

Serial and book publication

Pudd’nhead Wilson appeared in seven instalments of The Century Magazine from December 1893 to June 1894. Chatto & Windus published the first British book edition in 1894. The American Publishing Company of Hartford issued the first American edition on 28 November that year, using the longer title The Tragedy of Pudd’nhead Wilson and the Comedy Those Extraordinary Twins.

The American volume appended Those Extraordinary Twins, the comedy separated from the developing novel during composition. That companion work belongs to the publication history discussed here; this Astor volume presents the complete Pudd’nhead Wilson novel. Its contents comprise ‘A Whisper to the Reader’, twenty-one chapters and the conclusion.

Which text is printed

The copy-text follows the first American edition of 1894 through its Project Gutenberg transcription. A small number of readings are corrected against the Harper & Brothers edition of 1899. These interventions are documented, while Twain’s spelling, punctuation and dialect otherwise remain as printed.

The edition closes the spaces the original compositors inserted into contractions and resolves hyphens created solely by line breaks. It does not generally modernise the prose. The distinction matters particularly where a spelling belongs to dialect or a speaker’s individual language. Notes on the text and a separate textual introduction explain the relationship between the original setting and this edition.

Slavery, law and identification

Historical chapters examine slavery in Missouri, the legal classification of ancestry, inheritance and the threat of sale farther south. These subjects explain the consequences attached to the children’s exchange within the novel’s society. Dawson’s Landing is also considered as a river town with institutions and social practices connected to Twain’s experience of Missouri.

A separate section discusses fingerprints and identification. The edition places Wilson’s unusual collecting habit and its use at the trial alongside the developing history of identification methods. The novel’s events and the real history of fingerprinting are dated separately, so readers can follow both.

Twain and the novel’s composition

Samuel Langhorne Clemens was born in 1835 and grew up in Hannibal, Missouri. Before becoming established as Mark Twain, he worked in printing, piloted Mississippi steamboats and wrote for newspapers. His experience of a slaveholding river community supplies relevant background to a novel whose early chapters are set before the Civil War.

The composition history examines the transformation of material about twins into the published novel. Twain drafted the work while living in Italy in the early 1890s. The edition also places it within his later career, including the financial difficulties surrounding his publishing business, while distinguishing those biographical circumstances from the experiences of individual fictional characters.

Editorial and critical material

The front matter includes a short life of Twain, publication details, chronology, composition and sources, an introduction and an account of the narrative framework. Sections on language and dialect and on Pudd’nhead Wilson’s Calendar give separate attention to the characters’ speech and the aphorisms printed with the chapters.

Contemporary reception, stage and screen history, and the later critical record document the book’s reception over time. A glossary, explanatory notes and bibliography provide reference material alongside the chapter apparatus. Readers can therefore consult the legal, historical and textual information relevant to a passage without losing the distinction between Twain’s original novel and the new commentary.
