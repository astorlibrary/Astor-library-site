Fyodor Dostoevsky

The Brothers Karamazov

One night in a small Russian town old Fyodor Pavlovitch Karamazov is murdered, and his eldest son is arrested for the crime. First published serially between 1879 and 1880, Dostoevsky’s last novel centres on that murder and on the lives of the three sons, Dmitri, Ivan and Alyosha, and on their arguments about God, freedom and guilt.

A murder in the family

Fyodor Pavlovitch Karamazov is a rich old buffoon who abandoned his three sons as children. Dmitri, the eldest, a retired officer, violent and generous, believes his father has cheated him of his inheritance, and father and son are rivals for the same woman, Grushenka. Ivan is a brilliant young writer who cannot accept God’s world; Alyosha, the youngest, is a novice at the monastery near the town, devoted to the elder, Father Zossima. When the father is found dead and three thousand roubles gone, everything points to Dmitri.

The real murderer is the servant Smerdyakov, rumoured to be Fyodor Pavlovitch’s son, who has taken from Ivan the idea that without God everything is lawful. Dostoevsky follows the family through a few days of crisis and, two months later, through the eve of the trial, the trial itself and its aftermath, with a whole town around them: the monks, a poor captain and his dying son, a band of schoolboys, the women who love and torment the brothers.

Pro and Contra

At the centre of the book, in a tavern, Ivan tells Alyosha why he returns his ticket to God’s world: no future harmony, he says, could be worth the tortured children he has read about. Then he tells his poem of the Grand Inquisitor, in which Christ returns to Seville and an old cardinal explains why the Church has corrected his work, giving men bread, miracle, mystery and authority in exchange for their freedom. Some of the cruelty Ivan describes came from real trials that Dostoevsky had discussed in his journal, A Writer’s Diary.

Dostoevsky called this book of the novel, Pro and Contra, its culminating point in letters to his editor, Nikolai Lyubimov. The next book, The Russian Monk, gives Father Zossima’s life and teaching, and Dostoevsky told the statesman Konstantin Pobedonostsev, who was worried by the force of Ivan’s atheism, that it was meant as the answer: an indirect one, he said, given as an artistic picture and not point by point.

The elder and the monastery

On 16 May 1878 the Dostoevskys’ small son Alyosha died, and for a long time his father could not work. That June, at his wife’s urging, Dostoevsky travelled with the philosopher Vladimir Solovyov to the monastery of Optina Pustyn and met its elder, Amvrosy. According to Anna Dostoevskaya, some of Zossima’s turns of phrase came from the elder’s conversation, and the grieving mother who comes to Zossima in Book II, mourning a little son called Alexey who died before he was three, carries the family’s own loss.

Dostoevsky began the novel in July 1878. The monastery near the town, with its elders, its jealous ascetics and its pilgrims, draws on what he saw at Optina and on his reading, and the town, called Skotoprigonyevsk only late in the book, on Staraya Russa, where the family often stayed.

A trial by jury

The plot has a source in Dostoevsky’s own prison years. At Omsk he knew Dmitri Ilyinsky, a nobleman convicted of killing his father, and in The House of the Dead he first described him as a cheerful, dissolute man he could never believe guilty, then reported the news from Siberia that the real murderers had confessed after Ilyinsky had served ten years.

Its action is set in the mid-1860s, soon after the court statutes of 1864 brought Russia public trials, professional advocates and juries. Dostoevsky took advice on the investigation and the trial from the jurist Anatoly Koni, and Book XII follows the proceedings closely, from the doctors’ disagreement about Dmitri’s sanity to the rival speeches of the prosecutor and the celebrated counsel from Petersburg. The book’s title for the verdict is A Judicial Error.

Publication

The Brothers Karamazov appeared in the Russian Messenger from January 1879 to November 1880. On 8 November 1880 Dostoevsky wrote to Lyubimov that the Epilogue had been sent, after three years’ work and two in print, and the book edition came out in two volumes in December, dated 1881. In a short preface Dostoevsky presented the novel as the first of two about Alyosha. The second was never written: Dostoevsky died in St Petersburg in February 1881, months after his celebrated speech in Moscow at the unveiling of the Pushkin monument.

Constance Garnett’s translation

Constance Garnett’s translation, published in London by Heinemann in 1912, was the first in English; her preface points out that, unlike the French and American versions, it is complete and unabridged. It made the novel widely read in England, and changed an older view of the novel as chaotic and formless. Virginia Woolf, Katherine Mansfield and E. M. Forster welcomed it, while Henry James, Joseph Conrad and D. H. Lawrence were among its critics. In America, Thomas Wolfe and Scott Fitzgerald counted it among their favourite books, and William Faulkner said he reread Dostoevsky almost every year.

The text and this edition

Garnett’s translation leaves out Dostoevsky’s short preface, From the Author, but gives the four parts, twelve books and epilogue in full. The book edition of December 1880 followed the serial text of 1879 and 1880 with some corrections.

This Astor edition prints the complete Constance Garnett translation, with chapter summaries and explanatory footnotes, historical context, themes and character studies, critical perspectives, a glossary and original context essays. It adds extensive original material on Dostoevsky, Russian law and religion, the novel’s real-life sources, money and social rank, the Grand Inquisitor, publication history and critical reception.
