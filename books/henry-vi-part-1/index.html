At home, the red and white roses give faction a theatrical image that will outlive everyone who first chooses a side.

Henry VI, Part 1, William Shakespeare and collaborators

The funeral of Henry V opens a political vacuum. The young Henry VI cannot replace his father’s authority, English commanders quarrel, and Joan’s arrival gives the French cause a charismatic military centre. Talbot wins honour but cannot turn honour into a functioning state.

At the Temple Garden, nobles select red and white roses and convert private disagreement into the visual language of civil faction. By the end, Talbot and his son are dead, Joan has been captured and the proposed marriage between Henry and Margaret points directly into the conflicts of Part 2.

An origin story written after the crisis began

The modern title suggests the first chapter, but the play may have been written after material now called Parts 2 and 3.

  • Only Folio text No quarto of Part 1 is known; the 1623 First Folio is the earliest printed text.
  • Early stage record Philip Henslowe’s diary records a play called Harey Vi at the Rose on 3 March 1592, though identification is not absolutely certain.
  • Nashe’s Talbot Thomas Nashe praised spectators seeing brave Talbot triumph again on stage, often connected with this play.
  • Possible prequel Many scholars think the play was created to extend the successful Henry VI material backwards.
  • Collaborative writing Style and structure have led editors to propose several hands, with Shakespeare contributing parts rather than necessarily writing the whole alone.
  • One king, two wars The conflict in France and faction at home are shown as connected failures of leadership.
  • Temple Garden The red and white rose selection is Shakespearean invention, not a documented beginning of the Wars of the Roses.
  • Talbot and son The double death is a powerful theatrical invention, though the two historical deaths did not occur exactly as staged.

    • How the action moves

    Battlefield reversals and political quarrels make national loss feel both sudden and self-inflicted.

    Act 1

    Henry V dead, France rising

    English nobles gather for Henry V’s funeral and immediately quarrel. News from France reports losses, Talbot’s capture and the appearance of Joan, who convinces the Dauphin of her power.

    Act 2

    Talbot and the Countess

    Talbot escapes and wins military fame. The Countess of Auvergne tries to trap him, but his soldiers answer the plot. The episode turns reputation, body and military force into a compact theatrical joke.

    Act 3

    Roses chosen

    Plantagenet and Somerset’s quarrel in the Temple Garden divides their supporters around white and red roses. In France, Joan and the French regain momentum while English unity becomes increasingly fragile.

    Act 4

    Talbot abandoned

    York and Somerset blame each other and fail to send effective support. Talbot refuses rescue if it means leaving his son; father and son die together as faction defeats military courage.

    Act 5

    Joan captured, Margaret introduced

    Joan’s trial is written through hostile English legend rather than modern historical understanding. Suffolk captures Margaret of Anjou and promotes her marriage to Henry, creating the relationship that drives the next play.

    Characters and competing histories

    The drama turns historical figures into sharply opposed ways of imagining nation, faith and authority.

  • Henry VI A child king whose title cannot provide the military and political command associated with his father.
  • Joan la Pucelle Charismatic French leader and English enemy; the play’s hostile representation reflects wartime legend and Tudor theatrical culture, not a neutral biography of Joan of Arc.
  • Lord Talbot England’s heroic soldier, celebrated by allies and enemies but destroyed by the division of those who should support him.
  • Charles, the Dauphin The French royal figure who accepts Joan’s aid and helps reverse English conquest.
  • Richard Plantagenet / York A claimant restored to title whose white rose becomes a sign of future dynastic conflict.
  • Somerset York’s rival in the Temple Garden and in the failed reinforcement of Talbot.
  • Gloucester and Winchester Protectors of competing interests whose public quarrels expose instability at the centre of government.
  • Margaret of Anjou Introduced near the end as a marriage prize, but already positioned to become a central political actor in Parts 2 and 3.

    • Sources, authorship and historical distance

    The page distinguishes chronicle material, theatrical invention and modern scholarship.

    Edward Hall

    Dynastic history

    Hall’s chronicle provided an influential Tudor account of national division and the houses of Lancaster and York. The play inherits that retrospective shape while making present action look confused and contingent.

    Raphael Holinshed

    Chronicle sequence

    Holinshed supplied events and names from the French wars and English politics. The dramatists compress, rearrange and invent so that military loss and faction can be grasped in immediate scenes.

    Authorship

    A play made in company

    Modern attribution work uses vocabulary, metre, scene construction and comparison with other drama. The results vary in detail, but a collaborative account is now common in major scholarly discussion.

    Joan

    Enemy image and historical person

    The play’s Joan shifts from inspired leader to witch-like enemy because it stages an English wartime tradition. Responsible reading keeps that dramatic construction separate from the historical Joan and her later rehabilitation.

    Performance and the history cycle

    Part 1 is most often staged in a sequence, where its links to later civil war become visible.

    1592

    The Rose and early popularity

    The possible Henslowe record and Nashe’s reference to Talbot suggest that Henry VI material could command a large early audience. Military spectacle and national memory were commercial theatre.

    1963

    RSC, The Wars of the Roses

    John Barton and Peter Hall reshaped the Henry VI plays with Richard III into an influential cycle. The adaptation helped establish the early histories as forceful modern theatre.

    1983

    BBC Television Shakespeare

    Jane Howell’s production preserved the sequence in a single evolving theatrical space. Its visible doubling and stage-like design keep the plays’ repertory origins in view.

    2016

    The Hollow Crown

    The television cycle condensed the Henry VI plays as The Wars of the Roses. Screen adaptation brings the political sequence to a broad audience but necessarily reshapes the collaborative play’s structure.
