William Shakespeare

Henry V

A young king crosses the Channel to enforce a disputed claim and discovers that victory has a human cost. Around Henry’s great public speeches, the play keeps other voices audible: soldiers who question responsibility, civilians threatened by invasion and a Chorus who asks the audience to turn a bare stage into France.

History

Henry V, William Shakespeare

Henry V begins in the council chamber, where churchmen turn a difficult genealogy into a case for war, and ends with a diplomatic marriage meant to bind England and France together. Between those scenes Shakespeare places conspiracy, siege, exhaustion, argument and the startling English victory at Agincourt.

Henry can inspire a frightened army and speak privately with uncommon directness, yet the play never lets eloquence settle the moral questions around him. His threat at Harfleur, the execution of former friends, the killing of prisoners and his night-time debate with ordinary soldiers all complicate the heroic story told by the Chorus.

The shape of the play

How the action moves.

Five changes that hold the plot together. Each one alters who possesses power, knowledge or the right to tell the story.

01 · Claim

A war is made to sound lawful

Canterbury’s account of Salic law gives Henry the answer he seeks, while the Church’s threatened wealth sits quietly behind the discussion. The Dauphin’s gift of tennis balls then lets the proposed invasion be presented as an answer to personal insult as well as dynastic right.

02 · Departure

The crown severs old loyalties

Henry exposes Cambridge, Scroop and Grey before they can assassinate him, and sends all three to death. In Eastcheap, Falstaff dies offstage and Pistol, Nym and Bardolph leave for France, carrying the remains of Prince Hal’s earlier world into the king’s war.

03 · Harfleur

Eloquence turns from fellowship to terror

‘Once more unto the breach’ creates a shared martial body out of tired soldiers. When Harfleur still resists, Henry’s next speech threatens rape, infanticide and indiscriminate destruction, forcing the audience to hear what conquest may require.

04 · Agincourt

The king walks among those who must fight

Disguised on the eve of battle, Henry hears Bates and Williams question how much of a soldier’s death belongs on a king’s conscience. By morning he has answered fear with the Saint Crispin’s Day speech; the victory that follows is accompanied by the order to kill French prisoners.

05 · Settlement

A marriage closes the campaign, not the history

The Treaty of Troyes recognises Henry as heir to France and makes Katherine part of the settlement. The Epilogue immediately breaks the triumphal frame: their son will lose France and England will descend into the conflicts dramatised in the Henry VI plays.

Read closely

Three ways into the writing.

These are not themes to collect. They are patterns to follow through particular scenes, voices and choices.

The Chorus

Who makes the heroic picture?

The Chorus repeatedly admits that the theatre cannot show what it names. A few actors must become armies; a wooden stage must hold two kingdoms; hours must contain years. The apology is also an invitation. Spectators are asked to supply horses, ships and fields with their own ‘imaginary forces’, making them active partners in the history being staged.

That partnership matters because the Chorus’s Henry is unusually polished: a ‘mirror of all Christian kings’ moving through a clean national epic. The scenes that follow are more resistant. They return us to self-interested bishops, hungry soldiers, threatened townspeople and disputed responsibility. A production can make the Chorus trustworthy, propagandist, anxious or knowingly theatrical; each choice changes the pressure exerted by the rest of the play.

Kingship

Public certainty, private argument

Henry’s authority depends upon an extraordinary ability to change register. He can speak legal precision to ambassadors, violent prophecy to Harfleur, intimacy to his troops and plain awkwardness to Katherine. These are not ornamental speeches added to a fixed character. They are the means by which the king produces consent and turns a precarious claim into collective purpose.

At night before Agincourt, disguise removes the automatic obedience attached to his crown. Bates and Williams insist that ordinary men must answer for their own souls but leave Henry with a harder question about the cause in which they die. His long reply separates royal duty from private guilt; his following prayer reveals that the legitimacy of his father’s crown still troubles him. The scene makes leadership look less secure than it sounds in daylight.

Language

A nation heard in several accents

The army called ‘English’ includes the Welsh Fluellen, the Irish Macmorris and the Scottish Jamy. Their accents can be used for cheap caricature, but the scene also exposes the labour required to imagine Britain as a single military nation. Fluellen’s persistent attention to ‘the disciplines of the wars’ offers a professional standard against which Pistol’s empty swagger can be measured.

French is not merely the language of the defeated. Katherine’s lesson with Alice lets her test English words before she becomes part of an English treaty, and the wooing scene never grants Henry complete linguistic control. Jokes, mistranslations and pauses keep negotiation visible. What looks in political terms like possession can therefore remain, in performance, an encounter between people who do not fully share a language.

Book, theatre, history

The worlds around the play.

Dating, source and performance matter most when they return us to something the text is doing.

1599

A play written beside a live campaign

The fifth Chorus imagines a victorious English general returning from Ireland, almost certainly Robert Devereux, Earl of Essex. Essex departed in March 1599 and returned in disgrace that September, so the allusion catches a brief moment of public expectation. A play about an earlier French campaign was speaking to an audience watching another war unfold.

Text

The quarto and Folio do not tell precisely the same play

The 1600 quarto is much shorter than the 1623 Folio, omitting the Choruses, the night-time debate and much else. Editors disagree over how the shorter text came into being, but modern editions normally take the Folio as their principal basis. Comparing the versions shows how strongly the Chorus and the common soldiers shape the play’s moral range.

Performance

Patriotism has never been the only way to stage it

Henry V has repeatedly been recruited in wartime, most famously in Laurence Olivier’s 1944 film. Later productions and Kenneth Branagh’s 1989 film made mud, fatigue and grief harder to overlook. The text sustains both traditions because its ceremonies of national unity are repeatedly interrupted by the damage required to create them.

Keep in view

People carrying the argument.

Henry V

A newly established king whose command of language can unite an army, intimidate a town and conceal unresolved questions about legitimacy and responsibility.

The Chorus

Guide, admirer and theatrical impresario, asking the audience to imagine the scale the stage cannot supply while offering an elevated account of Henry’s campaign.

Katherine

Princess of France and eventual queen of England. Her language lessons and careful answers prevent the final courtship from becoming a simple display of Henry’s control.

Fluellen

A Welsh captain devoted to military discipline and historical analogy; comic in manner but often more exact and honourable than the men who mock him.

Michael Williams

An ordinary soldier who refuses easy reassurance on the eve of Agincourt and makes the king answer, however imperfectly, for the justice of his cause.

Pistol

A theatrical braggart who treats war as a chance for profit. His inflated speech and shabby conduct form a dark parody of heroic rhetoric.

Carry through the text

Questions that stay open.

Use them to test a scene, a performance choice or an argument of your own.

01

Does the play celebrate Henry’s war or put it on trial?

Set the Chorus’s praise beside Canterbury’s motives, Harfleur, the soldiers’ debate and the order to kill prisoners. The force of the play lies in the fact that neither account simply cancels the other.

02

What changes when Henry is not recognised as king?

Follow the night before Agincourt closely. Disguise gives Henry access to honest speech, but it also lets him withdraw from the consequences of revealing who has been listening.

03

Is the final courtship private, political or both?

Katherine is being asked to marry the conqueror named in a peace treaty. Attend to who translates, who jokes, who gives consent and what the Epilogue says about the marriage’s promised future.
