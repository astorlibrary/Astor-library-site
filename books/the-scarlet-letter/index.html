The edition

The novel remains at the centre

Summaries and notes are there when they help. The longer material gives readers a route into form, history and interpretation without replacing the work itself.

American romance · 1850

The Scarlet Letter, Nathaniel Hawthorne

The book begins twice. “The Custom-House” claims an embroidered letter and old papers as the source of the romance; the main story opens in Puritan Boston as Hester emerges from prison. Archive and invention are placed beside each other before the reader can treat any sign as self-explanatory.

This Astor Library edition presents the complete novel with chapter summaries and contextual notes. Longer material follows the Custom House, Puritan Boston, romance, the three scaffold scenes, the unstable A, symbolism, narration, character and reception.

Hawthorne’s ambiguity is kept open rather than converted into a key where each object has one meaning. The aim is to help readers distinguish evidence from interpretation and see why public certainty becomes one of the book’s most dangerous forms of power.

The shape of the work

Three public scenes and seven years of concealment

The scaffold returns each time the relation between secrecy and public knowledge changes.

01 · Exposure

Prison door and first scaffold

Hester emerges with Pearl and the A. She refuses to name the father and recognises her absent husband in the crowd.

02 · Edge

A life beyond the town

Hester supports herself through needlework. Chillingworth assumes a new name and binds her to secrecy while seeking Pearl’s father.

03 · Surveillance

Physician and minister

Chillingworth moves into Dimmesdale’s household. A midnight scaffold vigil imitates confession without accepting daylight.

04 · Forest

Escape is imagined

Hester reveals Chillingworth’s identity to Dimmesdale and plans Europe. Pearl will not cross the brook until the A is restored.

05 · Confession

The last scaffold

After the Election Sermon, Dimmesdale acknowledges Hester and Pearl, confesses and dies. Hester later returns and resumes the letter.

Read closely

A sign, a stage and a child

Each route begins with particular language and follows what changes around it.

The A

A letter that will not keep one meaning

The authorities intend the A as a fixed public sentence. Hester’s elaborate embroidery changes it before she reaches the scaffold. Later neighbours read “Able”, while an A-shaped light is interpreted as “Angel” and Dimmesdale sees judgement.

The letter still governs where Hester lives and how strangers see her. Meaning is not limitless; it is disputed. The book shows communities and individuals forcing stories onto marks, bodies and events.

The scaffold

Daylight, midnight, daylight

The first scaffold makes Hester visible, protects Dimmesdale behind his office and lets Chillingworth watch without disclosure. The midnight return gives Dimmesdale the form of confession without its public cost.

The final scene occurs in daylight, yet witnesses later disagree about what appeared on Dimmesdale’s chest and some deny confession. Revelation ends his secrecy but cannot control the accounts made from it.

Pearl

A symbol who is also a child

Adults repeatedly make Pearl carry meanings she did not choose. She is called the living scarlet letter, treated as evidence of sin and examined to see whether Hester may keep her. Her questions expose what the adults will not say.

The ending partly releases her from that work. Dimmesdale’s recognition, Chillingworth’s inheritance and a life only inferred elsewhere allow the child once treated as a symbol to possess a future beyond the narrator’s interpretation.

Book, history, form

Puritan Boston inside an American romance

Context matters here because it changes what we can notice in the writing.

History

The seventeenth century from 1850

Historical figures appear, but the romance is not a documentary reconstruction. Hawthorne uses Puritan Boston to examine punishment, authority and inherited guilt from nineteenth-century America.

Form

Romance and uncertainty

The Custom-House invents archival origins. Within the story, the narrator offers natural and supernatural explanations without deciding between them, making readers experience the desire for verdicts.

Inheritance

Ancestry and office

Hawthorne’s ancestors included a Salem witch-trial judge. His own Custom House employment adds another history of institutions, patronage and judgement to the frame.

Keep in view

The people made to carry meanings

Hester Prynne

A skilled needleworker, mother and object of discipline who develops an independent life without escaping the letter’s history.

Pearl

Hester’s observant daughter, repeatedly made to serve as a symbol while asking the clearest questions.

Arthur Dimmesdale

A revered minister whose public eloquence grows as secrecy damages his body and freedom.

Roger Chillingworth

Hester’s estranged husband, turning a search for truth into a chosen vocation of revenge.

The Custom-House narrator

A version of Hawthorne whose irony and uncertainty make evidence and authorship part of the story.

Puritan Boston

A collective judging voice whose verdict on Hester changes without ever becoming harmless.
