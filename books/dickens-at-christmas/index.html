About the work

The Christmas books of 1843–48.

Dickens published five short books for the Christmas market in these years; the account below follows the four reprinted here, from Scrooge’s night of memory to Redlaw’s bargain to forget sorrow.

The work

What happens.

Between 1843 and 1848, Dickens published five short books for the Christmas market. This collection brings together four of them as complete, unabridged texts.

A Christmas Carol follows Scrooge through memory, present responsibility and a possible future; The Chimes shows Trotty Veck the consequences of treating poverty as moral failure.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

The Cricket on the Hearth turns on marriage, trust, misunderstanding and reconciliation around the Peerybingle household.

The Haunted Man and the Ghost’s Bargain gives Redlaw the power to forget sorrow, only for the loss of painful memory to destroy sympathy for other people.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

Four volumes from a five-book publishing sequence

The four works collected here first appeared as separate short books: A Christmas Carol in 1843, The Chimes in 1844, The Cricket on the Hearth in 1845, and The Haunted Man and the Ghost’s Bargain in 1848. The intervening Christmas book, The Battle of Life, appeared in 1846 and is not part of this volume. The collection therefore brings together four specified works rather than claiming to reproduce the complete five-book sequence.

The books’ original seasonal publication does not mean that all their actions take place on Christmas Day. The Chimes concerns the passage from one year to the next, and The Cricket on the Hearth describes domestic events through the terms of a fairy tale. Reading them together shows the range of stories Dickens published for the Christmas market during these years.

Staves, quarters, chirps and chapters

Each work makes its divisions part of its presentation. A Christmas Carol has five staves, using a musical term to sustain the title’s suggestion of a song. The Chimes is divided into four quarters, corresponding to the striking of a clock. The Cricket on the Hearth has three chirps, while the three chapters of The Haunted Man trace the gift bestowed, diffused and reversed.

These short sequences make useful reading sessions, but their effects differ. Scrooge receives successive visits; Trotty Veck is shown consequences extending beyond the immediate present; John Peerybingle has to reconsider what he thinks he has witnessed; and Redlaw discovers that his bargain affects people beyond himself. The supernatural machinery changes from book to book rather than repeating the Carol’s arrangement.

Households, work and the terms of assistance

The characters’ occupations give the stories concrete social settings. Scrooge employs Bob Cratchit; Trotty works as a porter; John Peerybingle is a carrier; Caleb Plummer makes toys; and Redlaw teaches chemistry. Wages, employment, debt and the maintenance of households remain present even when ghosts, goblins or domestic spirits enter the action. The stories do not take place in a festive interval from material concerns.

They also distinguish different kinds of help. Fezziwig’s treatment of his employees, the wealthy speakers’ advice to Trotty, Caleb’s attempts to protect Bertha, and Milly Swidger’s care for others can be compared through what each person actually does. The collected format makes those comparisons possible without reducing four different narratives to a single general message about Christmas generosity.
