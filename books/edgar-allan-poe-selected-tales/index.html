Edgar Allan Poe: Selected Tales

Poe revised his stories again and again, so every edition has to choose a version. This one prints ten tales, among them ‘The Fall of the House of Usher’, ‘The Tell-Tale Heart’, ‘The Black Cat’ and two of the Dupin detective stories, and says for each which printing it follows.

The ten tales

The contents are The Tell-Tale Heart; The Black Cat; The Cask of Amontillado; The Fall of the House of Usher; The Oval Portrait; The Masque of the Red Death; The Pit and the Pendulum; Hop-Frog; The Murders in the Rue Morgue; and The Purloined Letter. Each story is printed complete. This is an Astor selection, rather than a reproduction of a collection that Poe issued under this title.

The selection includes accounts of murder and concealment, an artist’s fatal absorption in a portrait, Prince Prospero’s attempt to exclude plague, a prisoner’s efforts to understand his confinement, and the revenge of a court entertainer. The two Dupin stories bring a different kind of narrative: an unnamed companion records investigations that distinguish an explanation of the evidence from the assumptions made by the police.

Publication in magazines and gift-books

These ten tales first appeared between 1839 and 1849 in magazines, newspapers and annual gift-books. The distinction matters: The Pit and the Pendulum and The Purloined Letter appeared in The Gift, an annual volume, rather than first appearing in a collection assembled by Poe. The edition records the original publication setting of each story.

Poe worked as a magazine editor and reviewer as well as a writer. Stories were revised, reprinted and moved between periodicals and books during his lifetime. The supporting material explains that publishing environment and follows particular transactions, including the appearance of The Tell-Tale Heart in the first number of James Russell Lowell’s The Pioneer in 1843, for which Poe received ten dollars.

Which versions are printed

The textual note preserves distinctions between first publication and the version selected for this edition. The Purloined Letter follows Tales of 1845, while acknowledging later manuscript alterations in Poe’s copy owned by J. Lorimer Graham. Those later changes are not silently substituted for the chosen printed text.

The Oval Portrait and The Masque of the Red Death use Poe’s revised forms. The earlier version of The Oval Portrait was titled Life in Death and included an opening subsequently removed. By stating these choices, the edition allows readers to understand why a familiar story can differ from its earliest magazine appearance without treating every reprint as an identical text.

Narrators and investigations

Several stories give the account of events to someone directly implicated in them. The Tell-Tale Heart begins with a defence of the speaker’s sanity; The Black Cat offers a condemned man’s explanation; The Cask of Amontillado recounts a calculated act of revenge. Their narrators’ claims can be compared with what they describe doing, rather than accepted as an independent record.

In the Dupin stories, the narrator instead watches another person analyse a problem. The Murders in the Rue Morgue concerns apparently impossible violence in a Paris room, while The Purloined Letter concerns a stolen document and an unsuccessful official search. These stories are important early examples of detective fiction; the edition places them in the history of investigation without implying that their procedures describe actual police practice.

Historical references and Poe’s reputation

The contextual material supplies information about magazine culture, Gothic conventions and the historical references behind the tales. It distinguishes the story-world of The Pit and the Pendulum from the documented history of the Spanish Inquisition. Readers can therefore recognise an invented setting or device without assuming that every incident in the fiction has a direct historical equivalent.

Biographical material also examines the evidence behind Poe’s reputation. Accounts associated with Rufus Wilmot Griswold, whose hostile portrayal helped shape later ideas about the author, require checking against letters and other records. The edition’s account treats those sources as historical documents to be assessed, rather than using sensational anecdotes to explain the fiction.

Using the edition

Story summaries provide a record of events, while the historical sections explain references and circumstances surrounding publication. These are separate tasks: a summary establishes what happens, and a contextual account identifies information outside the story. The complete tales remain available for close reading alongside that supporting material.

Readers interested in the supernatural can compare Usher, the portrait and Prospero’s sealed rooms; readers interested in narration can compare the three accounts of criminal acts; and readers approaching detective fiction can read the Dupin stories together. The selection’s contents and textual note make the limits of the volume explicit: ten specified stories, with documented support, rather than Poe’s complete fiction.
