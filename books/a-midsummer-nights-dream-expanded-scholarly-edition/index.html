About the work

The lovers, the wood and the play within the play.

Hermia’s forced choice, Puck’s misapplied flower and the mechanicals’ Pyramus and Thisbe are followed here alongside the quarto and Folio evidence the text records.

The work

What happens.

Hermia is ordered to marry Demetrius or face death or permanent chastity. Her escape with Lysander draws Demetrius and Helena into a wood already disturbed by Oberon and Titania’s dispute over a changeling child.

Puck misapplies the flower that causes love at first sight, while Bottom receives an ass’s head and becomes the enchanted Titania’s beloved. The confusion joins desire, coercion, theatre and metamorphosis before dawn rearranges the lovers.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

Back in Athens, the mechanicals perform Pyramus and Thisbe. Their play within the play tests courtly spectatorship and makes amateur theatre central to Shakespeare’s comedy.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

The 1600 quarto and the later false date

Thomas Fisher registered A Midsummer Night’s Dream on 8 October 1600. The first quarto was printed that year by Richard Braddock for Fisher. It is the earliest printed witness and the principal basis identified for this Astor edition. Its date should be distinguished from the probable date of composition in the middle of the 1590s.

The second quarto also bears a 1600 date, but bibliographical research assigns its printing to 1619. It was prepared from the first quarto with some alterations, and an annotated copy of that reprint subsequently supplied copy for the 1623 Folio. The three texts therefore form a history of reprinting and intervention; they are not three independent surviving manuscripts by Shakespeare.

A change in the cast of the final act

The Folio’s substitution of Egeus for Philostrate in Act 5 changes which character discusses the available entertainments with Theseus. Egeus is Hermia’s father, whose demand concerning her marriage begins the play’s central conflict. Philostrate is the official responsible for arranging the court’s festivities. Their dramatic functions are different even when substantially the same lines are assigned to them.

The Astor edition retains Philostrate, as stated in its textual description. The variant remains useful for readers and performers because it shows that a speech heading can carry as much dramatic consequence as a changed word within a speech. Deciding who remains present at a wedding celebration also changes the visible group receiving the mechanicals’ performance.

The mechanicals make a play

The preparation of Pyramus and Thisbe records several practical stages of amateur production: allocating parts, arranging a rehearsal, deciding how to represent the wall and moonlight, and worrying about the effect of a lion on the audience. Quince directs a company whose members continue to explain their devices even while performing them.

At court, the spectators interrupt and comment on the entertainment, while the players attempt to continue. The scene therefore supplies a precise vocabulary for discussing the play’s interest in theatrical representation: actor and part, object and imagined setting, spoken explanation and visible action. Read the rehearsal beside the final performance to see which problems the company anticipated, which solutions it adopted and how the court receives them. The contrast is available in the scene sequence itself, without requiring a claim that the mechanicals represent a single identifiable real company.
