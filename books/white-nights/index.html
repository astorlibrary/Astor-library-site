Fyodor Dostoevsky

White Nights

Over four nights on a Petersburg canal embankment, a lonely young dreamer falls in love with Nastenka, who is waiting for another man. Dostoevsky published this short sentimental novel in 1848, four months before his arrest, and revised it in 1860; Constance Garnett’s translation follows the revised text.

Four nights and a morning

The narrator is twenty-six, a minor official who has lived in St Petersburg for eight years and has hardly an acquaintance. He knows the faces he passes every day and greets the houses as friends, and when the whole city leaves for its summer villas he feels deserted. Walking home late along a canal one night, he finds a girl crying at the railing, rescues her from a drunken gentleman and sees her home. Her name is Nastenka, she is seventeen, and she agrees to meet him the next night on condition that he does not fall in love with her.

On the second night he tells her what a dreamer is, and she tells him about the young lodger who promised a year ago to come back for her. The lodger has been in Petersburg for three days and has not come. The narrator takes her letter to be forwarded, waits with her through the third night, and on the fourth tells her that he loves her. She accepts him, and minutes later the lodger walks past and calls her name. The next morning a letter arrives from Nastenka, asking his forgiveness and announcing her wedding.

The dreamer

Dostoevsky had already described the Petersburg dreamer in a newspaper column, the Petersburg Chronicle, in 1847: a man who longs for real life, is too weak to reach it, and becomes a strange creature of the neuter gender. In White Nights the dreamer describes himself. On the second night he speaks of himself in the third person, because he is ashamed, and gives an account of his condition that is funny and exact: the corner he hides in like a snail, the friend who calls once and never again, the romances he lives in his head, and the anniversaries he keeps for feelings that never happened.

Dostoevsky kept returning to the type for the rest of his writing life, and the narrator of Notes from Underground, which Garnett printed in the same volume, is one of its descendants. The early dreamer has a tenderness the later ones lose. He wants nothing from Nastenka that she cannot give, and when she chooses the other man he blesses her.

Publication and revision

White Nights appeared in the December 1848 issue of the journal Otechestvennye zapiski, subtitled a sentimental novel from the reminiscences of a dreamer and dedicated to Dostoevsky’s friend the poet Aleksei Pleshcheyev. Four months later both men were arrested with other members of the Petrashevsky circle. Dostoevsky was sentenced to death, reprieved at the place of execution in December 1849, and spent the next ten years in prison and exile in Siberia.

The first reviewers liked it. In January 1849 Aleksandr Druzhinin placed it above several of Dostoevsky’s earlier stories, though he wished the dreamer had a clearer place, time and occupation. When Dostoevsky collected his early work in 1860 he revised the style and added the long list of the dreamer’s fantasies, from Hoffmann and Walter Scott to the taking of Kazan. Constance Garnett translated the revised text.

Petersburg in the white nights

The white nights are the weeks around midsummer when St Petersburg, at almost sixty degrees north, never gets properly dark. The story happens in that season, when anyone who could afford it had left for a villa on the islands or in the country, and the empty city feels to the narrator like a wilderness. Most of the action takes place on a canal embankment, which Russian commentators identify as the Catherine Canal, now the Griboyedov Canal, and on a bench near Nastenka’s side street.

Dostoevsky’s city is full of literature. Pushkin’s poems about Petersburg lie behind its canals and small wooden houses, Rossini’s Barber of Seville gives the story its love letter, and the dreamer’s reveries come from Scott, Hoffmann and the Romantic poets. Nastenka’s dry remark, that he talks as though he were reading it out of a book, is the story’s own verdict on how much of this Petersburg life is lived at second hand.

Readers and film-makers

Radical critics of the 1860s were less kind than the first reviewers. Nikolai Dobrolyubov, writing in 1861, saw the dreamer as a forerunner of the narrator of The Insulted and Injured and had no patience with lovers who run errands for their rivals. In the same year the novelist Evgenia Tur called it one of the most poetic works in Russian literature. Its brevity and warmth have made it, for many readers since, the way into Dostoevsky.

Film-makers have taken it far from Petersburg. Luchino Visconti’s Le notti bianche (1957), with Marcello Mastroianni and Maria Schell, moved it to a modern Italian port town recreated at Cinecittà and won the Silver Lion at Venice, and Robert Bresson’s Four Nights of a Dreamer (1971) set it on the banks of the Seine.

Constance Garnett’s translation and this edition

Constance Garnett (1861–1946) translated Dostoevsky’s novels and stories for Heinemann in twelve volumes between 1912 and 1920, and her versions shaped how English readers met him for most of the twentieth century. White Nights and Other Stories, published in 1918, was the tenth, and it also contains Notes from Underground. Her English is clear and a little formal, and she keeps the story’s exclamations, broken-off sentences and repeated Nastenkas.

This Astor edition prints Garnett’s translation complete, with chapter summaries and explanatory footnotes. The supporting material covers Petersburg in 1848, the phenomenon of the white nights, Dostoevsky’s literary sources, the story’s language, characters, publication and afterlife, with character studies, critical material and a glossary.
