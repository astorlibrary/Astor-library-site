William Shakespeare and a collaborator

Edward III

Edward III was printed without an author’s name in 1596 and left out of collected Shakespeare until the 1990s. The scenes in which the king tries to seduce the Countess of Salisbury are now generally given to Shakespeare. Who wrote the rest is still argued over.

About the work

The Countess episode and the French campaigns.

The account below follows both movements of the play: Edward’s pursuit of the Countess of Salisbury, and the campaigns in France that test the Black Prince.

The work

What happens.

Edward asserts a claim to the French crown, but a Scottish invasion first takes him north to relieve the Countess of Salisbury at Roxborough Castle. After driving away the Scots, he becomes consumed by desire for the Countess, commissions poetry in her praise and even orders her father to support his suit.

The Countess appeals to marriage, conscience and kingship, forcing Edward to confront whether a ruler capable of commanding a country can command himself. The later French campaigns test discipline in public warfare as the Black Prince faces battle and the expectations passed from father to son.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

The romantic and military halves are sharply different, yet both ask what mastery means. Private appetite and public command place different pressures on the king’s ability to govern himself.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

The first quarto and the Elizabethan history play

Cuthbert Burby entered the play in the Stationers’ Register on 1 December 1595 and published it in 1596. The register’s description emphasises Edward, the Black Prince and their war with the French king. Burby also issued Shakespeare plays during the 1590s, including Love’s Labour’s Lost and the second quarto of Romeo and Juliet. Edward III belongs to that commercial world of printed drama, although its first title page does not name an author.

The publication date establishes when the play was in print, not an exact date of composition or first performance. Its later exclusion from the First Folio and the modern arguments for shared authorship are further stages in the text’s history. They should be distinguished from the historical fourteenth-century events represented on stage.

A king commissions a love poem

In the Countess episode, Edward employs Lodowick to put his desire into verse. Their exchange brings the making of poetry directly onto the stage: the king demands praise adequate to the woman he wants, finds conventional descriptions insufficient, and attempts to direct another person’s words. Literary composition becomes one of the services royal power expects to command.

The Countess’s resistance makes the limits of that expectation explicit. She is already married, as Edward is, and her replies force him to acknowledge obligations his pursuit attempts to disregard. The episode is therefore not a detachable account of a royal courtship. It shows the king issuing instructions and encountering an answer that he cannot honourably compel his subject to change.

Battle, surrender and the treatment of civilians

The campaign scenes use messengers, challenges, reports and changes of location to represent warfare across a large area. The Black Prince’s experience is central, but the action also includes French rulers, soldiers and inhabitants of besieged Calais. Keeping track of who is speaking and where a report originates helps separate an announced expectation from an event actually shown to have occurred.

Calais introduces questions that victory alone cannot settle. The play shows poor inhabitants expelled from the town because they cannot contribute to its defence and must still be fed. Later, the six citizens who offer themselves to save the town face Edward’s anger, while Philippa argues for mercy. These scenes place the use of power over surrendered or defenceless people beside the account of military achievement.
