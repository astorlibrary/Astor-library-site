The Overcoat

Akaki Akakievitch, a poor copying clerk in St Petersburg, goes without supper for months to pay for a new overcoat, wears it for one day and has it stolen on the way home. Gogol’s story of 1842, in Claud Field’s translation, started a whole line of Russian fiction about poor clerks.

The clerk and his cloak

Akaki Akakievitch Bashmatchkin has copied documents in a St Petersburg government office for as long as anyone can remember. He is in his fifties, a titular councillor on four hundred roubles a year, and the younger clerks drop scraps of paper on his head and tell him it is snowing. He hardly notices. Copying is the one thing he loves, and he takes documents home to copy in the evenings for pleasure.

When the winter wind finds the holes in his cloak, the tailor Petrovitch refuses to patch it again and names a price for a new one. Akaki goes without tea and supper for months, and the thought of the new cloak becomes, in the narrator’s words, a companion on his life journey. He wears it for a single day. On the way home from a party it is torn off him in an empty square, and the important official he asks for help shouts at him until he has to be dragged out of the room. Within days he is dead, and soon afterwards a ghost starts pulling the cloaks off the backs of the city’s officials.

How Gogol wrote it

Gogol knew this world from the inside. After moving to St Petersburg from Ukraine he worked as a junior clerk in two government departments between 1829 and 1831. His friend Pavel Annenkov remembered him hearing an office anecdote, probably in the mid-1830s, about a poor clerk who saved for months to buy a fine gun and lost it from his boat on his first day’s shooting. That clerk fell ill with grief, and his colleagues clubbed together to buy him another. Gogol kept the saving and the loss and left out the happy ending.

In July 1839, at the spa town of Marienbad, Gogol dictated a first draft to the historian Mikhail Pogodin under the working title A Tale of a Clerk Who Steals Overcoats. He went on revising it for the next two years, and it went into the third volume of his collected Works. That volume is dated 1842 and was printed at the end of the year, but the censor held it up and it did not go on sale until January 1843, some months after the first part of Dead Souls.

Rank and money

Peter the Great’s Table of Ranks of 1722 divided the civil service into fourteen grades, and in Gogol’s Russia an official’s grade came before his name, as the narrator says it must. A titular councillor stood in the ninth. Akaki will never rise from it, and the story measures his life in the same exact terms: four hundred roubles a year, a kopeck put aside from every rouble, a Christmas bonus of sixty-five roubles when fifty would have been good luck, and a first price from the tailor of a hundred and fifty, more than a third of his salary.

The city is part of the reckoning. The northern cold, the narrator says, is the enemy of everyone on four hundred roubles a year, and the walk to his superior’s party crosses St Petersburg’s social map from ill-lit streets to bright ones full of lacquered sleighs and bearskins. The ghost is reported near the Katinka Bridge, Field’s name for the Kalinkin Bridge over the Fontanka, and the last shadow in the story strides off towards the Obukhov Bridge further along the same river.

The narrator

Much of the story’s effect comes from the man telling it. He will not name the department in case officials take offence, forgets where the party was held, and admits that he has never inquired who inherited Akaki’s goose-quills. For most of the story he laughs at Akaki along with the office. Then, without warning, he turns solemn: a young clerk, stopped in the middle of a practical joke, hears in Akaki’s voice the words Am I not your brother? and cannot forget them for the rest of his life.

Readers have divided over that paragraph ever since. For Russian critics of the 1840s it was the heart of the story, a plea on behalf of the downtrodden clerk. In 1919 Boris Eikhenbaum argued the opposite in his essay How Gogol’s Overcoat Is Made: that the whole story is a spoken performance of jokes, digressions and changes of tone, and that the famous humane passage is one more of those changes. Both readings start from the same pages, and the ghost at the end can be made to fit either.

Reception

At first the story attracted little comment, and even Vissarion Belinsky, Gogol’s great champion among the critics, mentioned it only in passing. Its influence showed in what came next. Within a few years Russian journals were full of stories about poor officials, and in Dostoevsky’s first novel, Poor Folk (1846), the clerk Makar Devushkin reads The Overcoat and is wounded by it because he sees himself in Akaki. In 1886 the French critic Eugène-Melchior de Vogüé wrote in Le Roman russe that a Russian man of letters had told him, We have all come out of Gogol’s Overcoat. Vogüé did not name him, and the remark is often credited to Dostoevsky without evidence.

The story has been adapted many times for stage and screen. The silent film The Overcoat of 1926, made in Leningrad by Grigori Kozintsev and Leonid Trauberg from a script by Yury Tynyanov, combines it with Gogol’s Nevsky Prospect, and the animator Yuri Norstein began an animated version in 1981 that has occupied him for decades.

Claud Field’s translation and this edition

The translation is Claud Field’s, from The Mantle and Other Stories, published in London by T. Werner Laurie in 1916 with Prosper Mérimée’s essay on Gogol as a preface. Field (1863–1941) also translated al-Ghazali and Meister Eckhart, and his English is brisk and plain. He calls the garment a cloak and sometimes a mantle, gives the Kalinkin Bridge as the Katinka Bridge, and uses the title Superintendent both for the police officer Akaki visits first and for the important personage he visits second. Where Gogol’s Russian says I am your brother, Field’s young clerk hears a question.
