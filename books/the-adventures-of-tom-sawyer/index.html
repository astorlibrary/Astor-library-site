Mark Twain

The Adventures of Tom Sawyer

Tom Sawyer tricks the boys of St. Petersburg, Missouri, into whitewashing his aunt’s fence for him, runs off to be a pirate on Jackson’s Island, and with Huck Finn sees a murder in the graveyard at midnight. Twain drew the town from Hannibal, where he grew up in the 1840s. The book appeared in 1876, in London before New York.

Tom’s life in St Petersburg

Tom Sawyer lives with Aunt Polly in St Petersburg, a fictional Missouri town on the Mississippi. His efforts to evade work and school include the whitewashing scheme, while his friendships and rivalries involve Huckleberry Finn, Joe Harper and Becky Thatcher. Play, punishment and the attraction of independence are recurring parts of his life in the town.

The adventures acquire a different urgency when Tom and Huck witness a murder in the graveyard. Tom must decide what to do with knowledge that puts another man at risk and threatens his own safety. The later search for treasure and the cave episode connect the boys’ imagined adventures with events that have consequences beyond a game.

The unusual route into print

The first edition appeared in London through Chatto & Windus on 9 June 1876, ahead of the authorised American edition. Belford Brothers issued an unauthorised Canadian edition in Toronto on 29 July. The American Publishing Company’s edition followed in December 1876. The Astor publication history records all three appearances rather than assuming that this American novel was first published in the United States.

The delay allowed Canadian copies and widely reprinted newspaper extracts to reach readers before the authorised American book was available. Twain’s suspicions about stolen proofs are distinguished from what can be established about the piracy. The edition explains these events as part of the work’s publishing history and its disappointing initial commercial performance.

The text and the early illustrations

The Astor volume preserves the complete novel while adding chapter summaries, historical context and material on textual and publication history. It keeps the story’s progression intact, from the early domestic episodes through the murder, the cave and the concluding arrangements for Tom and Huck.

True W. Williams supplied the great majority of the original American illustrations, with some work by others. Those pictures and their role in marketing the book are discussed in the editorial material, but they are not reproduced in this edition. This distinction matters for readers choosing a copy: illustration history is part of the commentary, while the printed reading text is accompanied by modern contextual material.

Hannibal and the historical town

St Petersburg draws on Hannibal, where Samuel Langhorne Clemens grew up before adopting the name Mark Twain. The Mississippi, the nearby cave, local schooling, churches and the social life of the town provide identifiable contexts for the fiction. The edition gives factual information about those settings while distinguishing them from the novel’s rearrangement of people and events.

Slavery is part of that history and of the world represented in the book. The historical sections do not describe the town only through the freedom available to Tom and his friends. They also attend to the people whose work and legal position made that childhood society possible, placing the novel’s incidents beside the record of Missouri life before the Civil War.

Childhood, language and belief

Schoolroom exchanges, Sunday-school prizes, punishments, courtship rituals and the boys’ plans for piracy make up much of the book’s social detail. The accompanying material explains the customs and institutions behind these episodes. It also considers the superstitions associated with the graveyard visit and the methods the boys propose for solving practical problems.

Twain’s language is retained as part of the novel rather than rewritten into contemporary speech. Contextual material helps identify historical vocabulary, social assumptions and the difference between the narrator’s account and the boys’ explanations. The edition follows the events closely enough for readers to see when a game, a belief or a private promise becomes relevant to a later decision.

Twain’s reputation and later accounts

The reception material examines contemporary responses and the book’s subsequent standing, together with the circumstances of its first sales. It also checks later anecdotes. Twain’s recollection that Tom Sawyer was his typewritten literary manuscript is treated as an unreliable memory; the discussion identifies Life on the Mississippi as the more likely work involved.

The volume brings these histories together with the complete novel: the real Hannibal, the fictional St Petersburg, the British first edition, Canadian piracy and the American illustrated book. Chapter summaries and critical background provide ways to study the narrative, while the publication record shows how the form and availability of the book differed for its first readers.
