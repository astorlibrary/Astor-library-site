Carnacki, the Ghost-Finder

Carnacki, a Chelsea bachelor with a camera and an ‘electric pentacle’, invites four friends to dinner and then tells them about his latest case. William Hope Hodgson published six of these stories in magazines in 1910 and 1912, and this edition prints them in that order, from the magazine texts.

Six original cases

The stories appear in this order: ‘The Gateway of the Monster’, ‘The House Among the Laurels’, ‘The Whistling Room’, ‘The Horse of the Invisible’, ‘The Searcher of the End House’, and ‘The Thing Invisible’. Five were published in The Idler in 1910; the sixth appeared in The New Magazine in January 1912.

These are the six Carnacki stories published during William Hope Hodgson’s lifetime. Three further stories were collected posthumously in the expanded 1947 volume. The Astor edition discusses those later additions in its editorial matter, but its primary-text selection is the original six-story sequence. It is not advertised as the later nine-story collection.

Carnacki and his investigations

Thomas Carnacki investigates reported hauntings and then describes his cases to four friends gathered at his home in Cheyne Walk, Chelsea. His equipment includes a plate camera, sealing wax, microphones, a revolver and the Electric Pentacle. Practical tests of a building’s physical conditions accompany his use of occult texts and protective rituals.

The cases do not all reach the same kind of conclusion. Some involve human deception; others retain supernatural or unresolved elements. The introductory section ‘Six Cases, Six Verdicts’ examines those differences, while material on the Cheyne Walk frame explains how the investigations are presented as recollections told to a recurring group of listeners.

Magazine order and the 1913 book

Eveleigh Nash collected the six stories in London in March 1913. That book put ‘The Thing Invisible’ first, changing the order in which magazine readers had encountered Carnacki. This edition instead begins with ‘The Gateway of the Monster’ and ends with ‘The Thing Invisible’, restoring the sequence of first publication.

The source text also preserves readings associated with the magazine printings. The textual note records examples, including the butler’s name in ‘The Gateway of the Monster’ and a sentence added to the collected version of ‘The House Among the Laurels’. Readers comparing this volume with an edition descended from the Nash book will therefore find deliberate textual differences.

Textual decisions and notes

The copy-text is the Project Gutenberg transcription of the magazine versions. Two emendations are documented: ‘gallows-iron’ replaces an inconsistent ‘galley-iron’, and the name Garder is restored where the same authority is otherwise called Carder. British spellings are restored from a list printed in the edition; other American forms are left alone.

Irish dialect spellings and defensible period usages remain. Other doubtful readings are left as printed and discussed in the notes. Paragraphing, scene breaks and italics follow the copy-text. The stories have no authorial footnotes: the footnotes in this volume are editorial, and each story’s original magazine number is given with its title.

Hodgson and psychical research

Hodgson was born in 1877 and spent years at sea before working as a physical-culture instructor and writer. Photography was one of his practical interests. His earlier novels included The Boats of the ‘Glen Carrig’, The House on the Borderland and The Ghost Pirates; the Carnacki series followed in the magazine market.

The supporting essays place the stories beside the activities of the Society for Psychical Research, founded in 1882, and earlier fictional investigators such as Le Fanu’s Hesselius, Flaxman Low and Blackwood’s John Silence. Separate sections examine Carnacki’s apparatus and Hodgson’s invented terminology, keeping fictional occult authorities distinct from actual organisations and technologies.

The reading apparatus

A summary and contextual panel follows each story. The notes explain historical references, unusual vocabulary and practical details of the investigations. The introductory material covers Hodgson’s life, a chronology, the making and reception of the stories and their later history.

A glossary and bibliography support further reading. The emphasis is on making the original magazine series understandable on its own terms: how the cases were ordered, which version of the text is printed, what equipment existed at the time, and where Hodgson’s invented procedures depart from the period’s documented attempts to investigate supernatural claims.
