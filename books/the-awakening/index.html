Kate Chopin

The Awakening

Edna Pontellier, twenty-eight, married to a New Orleans businessman and the mother of two boys, spends a summer at Grand Isle and learns to swim. By the autumn she has moved out of her husband’s house. Reviewers in 1899 disliked the book so much that Kate Chopin published very little afterwards. It is printed here in the first-edition text, without footnotes.

Edna Pontellier and the course of the novel

Edna Pontellier, her husband Léonce and their children spend the summer at Grand Isle, off the Louisiana coast. There Edna forms a close attachment to Robert Lebrun and begins to question the domestic arrangements of her married life. Swimming, conversation, painting and music have particular places in that summer, and each returns in the later narrative.

Back in New Orleans, Edna increasingly refuses the social obligations that Léonce regards as part of their position. She pursues painting, moves into a smaller house and conducts relationships on terms that differ from the life expected of her. Chopin follows these decisions through Edna’s encounters with Robert, Alcée Arobin, Adèle Ratignolle and the pianist Mademoiselle Reisz. The final chapter returns to Grand Isle.

Composition and first publication

Chopin began the novel in 1897 and completed it on 21 January 1898. Its original title was A Solitary Soul. Herbert S. Stone & Company published The Awakening in Chicago on 22 April 1899. These dates distinguish composition from publication.

The Astor text follows the first edition of 1899. It presents the complete narrative rather than extracts arranged around selected themes. Publication history accompanies the text, allowing readers to place the novel beside Chopin’s earlier writing and the contemporary publishing circumstances in which it appeared.

The Louisiana setting

Grand Isle, Chênière Caminada and New Orleans are identifiable places, and the edition treats them as such. The coastal resort and the city household offer different social settings; journeys between them are part of the novel’s construction. Historical material explains the resort culture, local geography and the hurricane of 1893, which devastated the coastal communities before Chopin wrote the novel.

The discussion of Creole society distinguishes that particular historical setting from Edna’s own Kentucky background. Material on marriage and property provides information about the legal and economic arrangements surrounding the Pontellier household. The account also attends to servants and racial divisions, so that the household’s relative leisure is not described as though it existed independently of other people’s work.

Music, work and domestic expectations

Edna’s painting and her encounters with music are specific activities in the narrative. Mademoiselle Reisz performs for listeners whose responses differ, while Adèle’s music belongs to another domestic setting. The supporting material identifies these episodes and their historical references, giving readers information with which to examine them directly.

The novel also repeatedly records money, visiting, childcare and the organisation of a home. Edna’s proposed independence therefore has practical as well as personal consequences. Contextual sections on marriage, property and social convention explain the conditions under which she acts; they do not replace the narrative with a single approved account of her decisions.

Chopin and the record of reception

Kate Chopin was born in St Louis in 1850 and spent important years of her married life in Louisiana. Her fiction includes both novels and short stories. The biographical and literary material situates The Awakening within that career, including its regional settings and the writers whose work she read.

The reception history distinguishes documented hostility from later repetition. No evidence has been found for the often-repeated claim that St Louis libraries banned the novel; the removal by Evanston Public Library in 1902 is a separate documented event. Similarly, the cancellation of the projected collection A Vocation and a Voice is recorded without inventing a reason for the publisher’s decision.

The Astor commentary

Chapter summaries and contextual material are placed at chapter ends, with no new footnotes introduced into Chopin’s narrative. Readers can consult the summaries to check the sequence of events and then turn to material on Louisiana life, music, race, language and literary influences. The layout preserves a distinction between the novel and its modern apparatus.

Critical perspectives and reception history give accounts of different scholarly responses rather than presenting a single interpretation as fact. Alongside the publication and textual histories, these sections make it possible to study both the 1899 book and the later discussion that changed its standing. The edition describes the historical evidence behind its claims and keeps that evidence distinct from the characters’ words and the narrator’s account.
