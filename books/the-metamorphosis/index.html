About the work

Gregor Samsa’s transformation and the job he cannot miss.

The account below follows the Samsa household from the first missed train to the arrival of the lodgers, and records the writing of Die Verwandlung in twenty-one days of 1912.

The work

Story, argument and structure.

Gregor Samsa wakes from uneasy dreams and finds that he has been transformed into what Ian Johnston translates as a “monstrous verminous bug”. His immediate concern is missing work as a commercial traveller.

Gregor supports his parents and younger sister through a job he despises because the family is indebted to his employer. When he misses his train, a senior representative of the firm comes to the flat to investigate.

Reading the work

What the complete text brings into view.

As Gregor is confined to his room, the household’s economic structure changes. His father returns to work, his mother takes in sewing, Grete becomes a shop assistant, servants leave and three paying lodgers arrive.

Written in twenty-one days in 1912 and first published in 1915, Die Verwandlung is the longest work of fiction Kafka completed and published during his lifetime.

Text and context

Johnston’s translation and the German behind it.

Ian Johnston’s public-domain translation stands, sixteen corrections aside, and the notes reach from the Prokurist’s authority and compulsory sickness insurance to the translation history of ungeheueres Ungeziefer.

Text

Editorial method

Ian Johnston’s translation, prepared for students at Malaspina University-College and released into the public domain in January 1999, is retained apart from sixteen evident typographical errors checked against Kafka’s German. Notes explain Gregor’s position in the commercial world, the authority of the Prokurist, Austro-Hungarian money, domestic servants, compulsory sickness insurance, trains and trams, and the German vocabulary behind Johnston’s English.

Supporting material

Context and interpretation

Supporting essays examine German-speaking Prague, work and bureaucracy, the Samsa household, illness and disability, publication, Kafka’s instruction that Gregor’s transformed body must not appear on the original cover, and the translation history of ungeheueres Ungeziefer.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

The three parts and the flat’s doors

Kafka divides the novella into three parts. The first concentrates on the morning Gregor cannot leave for work and the effort to open his locked bedroom door. The second follows the family’s accommodation to his condition, including the disputed removal of furniture. The third introduces the lodgers and develops the household’s final decision about his continued presence.

The flat’s arrangement gives these changes a visible structure. Gregor hears conversations through doors, hides before other people enter and attempts to judge events from his restricted position. The room that initially contains his work and personal possessions gradually becomes a place where unwanted objects are stored. Changes to this small space record decisions about whether he is still to be treated as a member of the family.

What Gregor can understand and communicate

The narrative remains close to Gregor’s thoughts even after his speech becomes unintelligible to the people outside his room. He considers train times, excuses to his employer and his family’s needs while his listeners respond to the altered sound of his voice. Readers therefore retain access to intentions that the other characters increasingly refuse or fail to recognise.

His preferences also change in ways he struggles to assess. Fresh food that he once liked becomes unappealing, while scraps offered by Grete suit him better. Crawling on the walls gives him new activity, yet removing the furniture threatens his connection with his former life. Kafka records these incompatible responses without supplying an explanation for the original transformation. The change is established at the start and never resolved by a medical diagnosis or an account of how it occurred.

Grete’s music and the family’s calculations

Before the transformation, Gregor intends to pay for Grete to attend the conservatory. Her violin playing later draws him out towards the lodgers, whose initial interest gives way to dissatisfaction. Gregor’s response to the music recalls an ambition for his sister that she has not fully known. The episode places his continuing attachment beside the household’s increasingly commercial use of its rooms.

The family finances contain information Gregor had not understood while he was earning. His father has retained some money, and the other members gradually obtain employment. This does not make their altered circumstances comfortable, but it complicates Gregor’s conviction that all survival depends on him. The closing movement follows the parents and Grete outside the flat, after the narrative has spent most of its time within or immediately around Gregor’s room.
