About the work

How the nineteenth century remade Christmas.

The history begins with customs already fading before 1837 — kissing boughs, Twelfth Night, wassailing — and follows the festival’s reshaping through cheaper postage, railways, mass publishing and consumer culture.

The work

What happens.

People often talk about the British Christmas as a set of ancient traditions. This book traces where they came from, and shows that many began with nineteenth-century changes in transport, print, shopping and family life.

The history begins before Victoria’s accession with customs already becoming less common: kissing boughs, Twelfth Night celebrations, New Year gift-giving, wassailing and seasonal charity. It then follows the rapid Victorian reshaping of the festival through cheaper postage, railway travel, mass publishing and a growing consumer culture.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

Individual chapters examine the Christmas tree, the first commercial card, Tom Smith and the cracker, goose clubs and dinner, familiar carols, urban and rural celebration, servants below stairs and Christmas inside the workhouse. Christmas charity is set beside the New Poor Law and the workhouse.

Literature is part of this social history. The book reproduces three Dickens pieces and discusses A Christmas Carol, The Chimes, Trollope’s Christmas at Thompson Hall and Gabriel Grub in The Pickwick Papers, as well as the Victorian Christmas number and ghost-story tradition.

Editorial method

The edition brings material culture, legislation, labour and literature into one chronology. Familiar objects are treated as historical developments whose meanings changed as they moved through royal example, commercial manufacture and domestic use.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

A card with an identifiable maker and date

One unusually well-documented object is the Christmas card commissioned by Henry Cole and designed by John Callcott Horsley in 1843. Cole’s diary records receiving Horsley’s design on 17 December. The card combines a central family toast with smaller scenes of charitable giving. It joins two subjects that recur throughout Victorian Christmas writing: celebration within the household and responsibility towards people outside it.

A thousand copies were printed, with space for a personal greeting, and the design was offered for sale at a shilling. The V&A’s account records that the venture initially failed commercially. So the surviving card records one experiment in Christmas printing; it does not show that everyone took up the custom straight away. Price, production and the later expansion of the market remain part of its history.

The royal tree before and after 1848

The Royal Collection’s account traces royal Christmas trees to Queen Charlotte before Victoria’s reign. Albert helped popularise a practice that already had a history in Britain. This earlier evidence matters because an account beginning only with Victoria and Albert can mistake the spread of a custom for its first appearance.

The familiar illustration of the royal family around a decorated tree appeared in the Illustrated London News in 1848. It gave readers a reproducible image of a royal domestic celebration. Trees, presents and the grouping of adults and children could then be encountered on the printed page as well as in an actual household. The relationship between private observance and public illustration is a useful subject when reading the book’s chapters on publishing and material culture.

Dickens in a weekly journal

A Christmas Tree appeared in Household Words on 21 December 1850, in issue 39 of the journal’s second volume. This is a different publication setting from the separate gift-book format of A Christmas Carol in 1843. The dated journal record allows the piece to be placed within the seasonal publishing trade discussed in this cultural history.

The three complete Dickens pieces reproduced in the Astor volume give readers primary literary texts alongside the historical chapters. When following one custom through the book, keep the kind of evidence in view: a surviving card shows a manufactured object; an illustration shows a published representation; a literary piece shows a writer arranging material for readers. Comparing these forms makes it possible to ask precise questions about what Christmas looked like, who could afford its goods and how its familiar images circulated.
