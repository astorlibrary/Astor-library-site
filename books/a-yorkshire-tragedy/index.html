About the work

Ten scenes from the Calverley case.

The play strips Walter Calverley’s 1605 attack on his family to Husband, Wife, children, debt and inheritance; what follows covers both the action and the 1608 quarto that carries it.

The work

What happens.

An unnamed gentleman has squandered land and money through gambling. His wife conceals his cruelty, protects the household and secures him a court position, but he reads help as humiliation and control.

He murders his eldest son, attacks another child and wounds his wife before riding towards the third. A fall from his horse leads to capture and a return past the house where he finally confronts the bodies and the logic of his violence collapses.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

The play’s speed comes from its compressed form and proximity to the real case. The absence of personal names turns one notorious crime into a stark domestic structure of Husband, Wife, children, debt and inheritance.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

Two titles within the 1608 quarto

The book’s main title page calls the work A Yorkshire Tragedy, but an internal heading introduces All’s One and describes it as one of four plays in one. That second heading is evidence for a larger entertainment of which the surviving short tragedy may have been a part. It does not identify the missing companion pieces or establish their authorship.

The first edition was printed by Richard Braddock for Thomas Pavier. Its explicit attribution to Shakespeare, combined with its reference to the King’s Men and the Globe, is unusually substantial contemporary print evidence. Modern scholarship nevertheless generally accepts Middleton’s authorship. The discrepancy is worth preserving: an early publisher’s attribution and the conclusions drawn from later study of the text are separate forms of evidence.

An opening conversation that precedes the violence

The first scene is a conversation among servants, with news brought from London, jokes about fashions and reports of the young master’s behaviour. Its comparatively comic manner differs from the scenes that follow. The audience hears of extravagant expenditure, debts and abuse before meeting the family at the centre of those reports. The scene makes the household’s condition a matter of knowledge among servants as well as among their employers.

Reports about a brother at university extend the consequences beyond the immediate family. He has stood security for the Husband and is now endangered by debts contracted on his behalf. When the Master of a College arrives, the prospect of lost education and imprisonment supplies another example of obligations the Husband has allowed other people to bear.

What the Husband says, and what the play shows

The Husband describes the killing of his children as a way of preventing the poverty he has brought upon them. The surrounding action exposes the contradiction in that claim. His wife is seeking help, other people intervene, and the children become the objects of violence rather than beneficiaries of protection. Keeping his speeches distinct from the account supplied by the action is essential to reading the tragedy accurately.

After his capture, the return to the house forces him to look at the results of his conduct. The play ends with the movement towards legal custody and does not stage the historical Calverley’s eventual punishment. Its final concentration is on recognition within the damaged household. That ending is one reason to distinguish the dramatic sequence from a complete documentary account of the 1605 case.
