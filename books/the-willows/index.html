Algernon Blackwood

The Willows

Two men canoeing down the Danube in flood tie up for the night on a sandy island among the willows below Pressburg, and cannot leave. Algernon Blackwood had made the same journey by canoe himself, a few years earlier. This is the original British text of 1907, printed without footnotes.

Two canoeists on the Danube

An unnamed narrator and his Swedish companion are travelling down the Danube when they camp on an island below Pressburg. The river is in flood. Sandbanks, channels and low willows surround a campsite exposed to the current and the wind, and the men must attend to ordinary practical matters such as shelter, food, fuel and the condition of their canoe.

The narrator begins to report sights and sounds that he cannot explain in the same terms. His companion’s apparent confidence gives way to a different account of the danger they face. The two men compare what they have noticed and try to decide whether they can leave, and no narrator steps in to explain what is happening.

The 1907 collection

The Willows first appeared as the third story in The Listener and Other Stories in 1907. In that volume it occupied pages 127–203. Its later publication as a separate story can obscure the fact that it originally formed part of a collection; the Astor publication history restores that setting and follows its subsequent reputation.

This edition presents the complete story in its original British text. The textual history and note on the apparatus distinguish Blackwood’s narrative from modern explanations added around it. There are no footnotes in the story itself; the notes at the end of each section give facts and do not interpret the supernatural events.

The journey behind the fiction

Blackwood made a real canoe journey on the Danube. The edition examines the accounts of that trip, including his own recollections, and records the uncertainty about its date. Accounts give 1900 or 1901, and the edition reports the disagreement.

The documentary material provides information about travel, camping and the river route that can be compared with the story. The real journey and the story are treated separately. A resemblance between a remembered landscape and a description establishes a useful context, but does not establish that the narrator’s whole experience happened to the author.

The Danube today

The names and places in the narrative belong to the historical Danube. Pressburg is the city now called Bratislava, and the channels below it form the geographical setting for the canoeists’ difficulty. The edition supplies historical geography so that readers can locate this stretch of river without expecting present-day maps to reproduce the landscape as described.

Material on later river engineering explains why that comparison has limits. Major works and the diversion of water into the Gabčíkovo power canal in 1992 substantially altered the area. These changes are discussed as part of the site’s history, not as evidence for the story’s supernatural claims. The geographical account also explains the practical significance of flooding, unstable banks and changing channels.

Blackwood and the history of the weird tale

Algernon Blackwood lived from 1869 to 1951. His career included fiction, journalism and later radio and television appearances. The biographical material places the story within his travelling and writing life and considers the supernatural and occult interests that form part of its historical background.

The reception account treats H. P. Lovecraft’s praise with attention to the actual wording of Supernatural Horror in Literature. The sentence often quoted as calling The Willows the finest supernatural tale in English literature paraphrases his high opinion; it is not a quotation. The edition explains that distinction and places his response alongside the broader history of the story’s circulation.

What the notes are for

Section summaries help a reader keep track of the two men’s observations, their changing assessment of their situation and their attempts to manage the camp. The factual notes explain geography, canoe travel, unfamiliar language and documented background. They are designed for reference while leaving the story’s account of its events available to the reader.

The longer material brings together the author’s life, the evidence for the real voyage, publication history and the changing physical landscape. It is particularly useful when a modern retelling has encouraged assumptions about the story’s setting or when a familiar statement about its reputation proves to be a paraphrase. The volume identifies what is documented and where the record remains uncertain, alongside the complete work of fiction.
