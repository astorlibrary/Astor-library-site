The seventeen-story collection

William Heinemann published The Open Boat and Other Stories in London in 1898. Its seventeen stories make it a different collection from the eight-story American volume, The Open Boat and Other Tales of Adventure. This Astor edition follows the fuller London contents and their original arrangement.

Part I, ‘Minor Conflicts’, contains eight stories, including the title story, Western and Mexican narratives and ‘Death and the Child’. Part II, ‘Midnight Sketches’, contains nine shorter pieces concerned largely with city life. The two headings are preserved so that readers can see the collection’s structure rather than encounter the works as an undifferentiated selection.

Part I: Minor Conflicts

The first part contains ‘The Open Boat’, ‘A Man and Some Others’, ‘The Bride Comes to Yellow Sky’, ‘The Wise Men’, ‘The Five White Mice’, ‘Flanagan and His Short Filibustering Adventure’, ‘Horses’ and ‘Death and the Child’. The stories range across sea travel, gunfights, gambling, armed expeditions and wartime experience.

The title story draws on Crane’s survival of the wreck of the Commodore off Florida in January 1897. Four men occupy a small boat as they attempt to reach shore. The volume retains the London dedication to William Higgins, Captain Edward Murphy and steward C. B. Montgomery, connecting the published collection with the people involved in that experience.

Part II: Midnight Sketches

The second part comprises ‘An Experiment in Misery’, ‘The Men in the Storm’, ‘The Duel That Was Not Fought’, ‘An Ominous Baby’, ‘A Great Mistake’, ‘An Eloquence of Grief’, ‘The Auction’, ‘The Pace of Youth’ and ‘A Detail’. Several are brief studies of poverty, crowds, work or encounters in New York.

Their inclusion gives the book a wider range than a selection restricted to the shipwreck and Western stories. The editorial material considers how Crane’s reporting and observation supplied material for fiction, while the story apparatus identifies relevant circumstances of publication and setting for each individual piece.

The London copy-text

The copy-text is the Heinemann London edition of 1898. Its mixture of British and American forms is retained, rather than regularised into a single modern spelling system. Section numerals also remain: six stories are divided into numbered sections, while eleven run without those internal numbers.

The textual note records differences between magazine and book versions. It also explains a correction to the contents listing: the first edition prints ‘Dual’ there, although the story’s own heading correctly reads ‘The Duel That Was Not Fought’. This edition follows the story heading. Centred rules replace two rows of asterisks used for additional breaks in the original setting.

Crane’s journalism and travels

Stephen Crane was born in Newark, New Jersey, in 1871. His writing developed through newspaper work, observation of New York’s poorer districts and fiction including Maggie: A Girl of the Streets and The Red Badge of Courage. The success of the latter brought him international attention while still in his twenties.

His travels included the American West and Mexico, the attempted voyage towards Cuba aboard the Commodore, and reporting on the Greco-Turkish War. He later worked as a correspondent during the Spanish-American War and lived in England. The edition connects these documented journeys with particular stories without assuming that every fictional incident directly reproduces a biographical event.

Sources, context and commentary

The introductory matter includes a biography, publication details, chronology, sources and influences, narrative framework and discussion of language and style. A section on filibustering in 1895–1898 explains the armed expeditions relevant to the Commodore voyage and other material in the collection. ‘Against the Record’ considers documentary evidence, while ‘In the Magazines’ follows the stories’ periodical appearances.

Story summaries, historical context and explanatory notes accompany the complete texts. Critical perspectives, reception history, glossary and bibliography provide additional reference material. The edition therefore identifies both the contents of this particular 1898 volume and the different reporting, magazine and book contexts from which its seventeen stories emerged.
