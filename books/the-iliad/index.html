About the work

Achilles’ anger in Butler’s prose.

The account below follows the poem from the quarrel with Agamemnon to the funeral of Hector, and shows how Butler’s 1898 prose and the notes open the poem to readers without ancient Greek.

The work

What happens.

The poem begins with the quarrel between Achilles and Agamemnon. When Achilles withdraws from the battle, the Greeks start losing, and the poem shows what a code of honour costs when a man’s whole identity depends on public recognition.

Hector’s defence of Troy, Patroclus’s decision to enter battle and Achilles’ return after his friend’s death move the poem from anger towards catastrophic revenge. The ending does not celebrate the city’s fall; it pauses over Priam’s appeal and the funeral of Hector.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

Butler’s prose prioritises clarity and narrative movement. The notes explain unfamiliar language, the social and religious world of the poem and the terminology of Homeric warfare without requiring readers to know ancient Greek.

Every book is accompanied by a summary and context box. Longer essays introduce Homer, Butler, oral tradition, the Homeric question, the possible history behind Troy and the transmission of the poem.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

A route through the twenty-four books

After the opening quarrel, Books II–VII establish the opposing forces and several leading fighters. They include the catalogue of ships, the proposed duel between Menelaus and Paris, Diomed’s fighting and Hector’s return to Troy. Book IX sends an embassy to Achilles with an offer of gifts, but persuasion fails to bring him back into battle. The fighting subsequently moves towards the Greek ships.

Patroclus enters combat in Book XVI. His death leads to the struggle over his body, Achilles’ decision to return and the making of new armour. Hector dies in Book XXII, but two substantial books remain: the funeral games for Patroclus and Priam’s visit to recover his son. The poem’s structure gives negotiation, burial and the organisation of remembrance space after the principal duel.

Names in Butler’s translation

Butler’s naming conventions can initially obscure connections with other translations. Jove is Zeus, Juno is Hera, Minerva is Athena, Neptune is Poseidon, Mars is Ares, Venus is Aphrodite and Vulcan is Hephaestus. Ulysses corresponds to Odysseus, while Butler commonly writes Diomed for Diomedes and Alexandrus for Paris. Keeping these equivalents in view avoids mistaking alternative names for additional characters.

The Greeks themselves are collectively called Achaeans, Argives and Danaans at different points. All three names usually mean the same army. References to a man as the son of Atreus or the son of Peleus likewise require attention to family: Agamemnon and Menelaus are both sons of Atreus, while Achilles is the son of Peleus. Context establishes which person is meant.

Encounters beyond single combat

In Book VI, Glaucus and Diomed discover a bond of hospitality between their families and exchange armour instead of fighting. Later in the same book, Hector meets Andromache and their child inside Troy. These scenes introduce obligations and relationships that extend beyond a warrior’s immediate performance on the battlefield.

The shield made for Achilles in Book XVIII contains cities, a dispute before elders, agricultural work, a vineyard, herds and dancing. It places scenes of civilian activity on an object intended for combat. Book XXIII similarly broadens the action through athletic contests, prizes, disagreements and settlements at Patroclus’s funeral. Reading these episodes alongside the battle books makes clear how much of the epic concerns speech, exchange, work and social order. Butler renders all of these passages in prose, so readers can follow their narrative sequence while using the notes for names and unfamiliar practices.
