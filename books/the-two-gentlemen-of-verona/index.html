The Two Gentlemen of Verona, William Shakespeare

This Astor Library Edition presents the complete play with scene summaries, line numbering and explanatory notes. The support follows the movements between Verona, Milan and the forest, clarifies the traffic in letters, rings and disguises, and gives the servants’ prose comedy its place beside the unsettled story of their masters.

The introductory material considers the play as an early experiment in patterns Shakespeare would revisit: friends divided by desire, a woman travelling in male disguise, lovers banished into green space and recognition delayed until the final scene. Its debt to the story of Felismena in Jorge de Montemayor’s Diana is explained without treating the play as a mere rehearsal for later comedies.

Close-reading sections concentrate on constancy, male friendship, Julia and Silvia’s acts of resistance, and the troubling speed of the final reconciliation. The ending is addressed plainly, including Proteus’s threat of sexual violence and Valentine’s apparent offer of Silvia, so that readers can distinguish a conventional comic finish from the difficult actions the text asks a company to stage.

The shape of the play

How the action moves

Five changes that hold the plot together. Each one alters who possesses power, knowledge or the right to tell the story.

01 · Movement one

The friends divide

Valentine leaves Verona to gain experience at the court in Milan, while Proteus remains because he loves Julia. Julia pretends indifference to Proteus’s letter, tears it and then tries to recover its pieces. Proteus’s father decides that his son must follow Valentine, forcing the lovers to part after exchanging vows and rings.

02 · Movement two

A new love and an old betrayal

At Milan, Valentine has fallen for Silvia, the Duke’s daughter, and plans to elope with her rather than yield to the favoured suitor Turio. Proteus arrives and immediately desires Silvia. In a private argument with himself, he chooses the new pursuit over both Julia and Valentine, then informs the Duke of the planned escape.

03 · Movement three

Banishment and disguise

The Duke exposes the rope ladder hidden beneath Valentine’s cloak and banishes him. Proteus pretends sympathy while offering to manage Valentine’s messages to Silvia. In Verona, Julia decides to follow Proteus and adopts the identity of a male page named Sebastian, accepting the risks of travel in order to learn the truth for herself.

04 · Movement four

The forest and the false courtship

Valentine encounters a group of outlaws who compel him to become their leader. Proteus claims to assist Turio but secretly presses his own suit, which Silvia rejects. Julia, still disguised, must carry Proteus’s gifts and even the ring she once gave him. Launce’s account of taking punishment for his dog Crab supplies a comic image of loyalty absent from Proteus’s conduct.

05 · Movement five

A reconciliation that will not sit quietly

Silvia flees Milan to find Valentine but is captured by the outlaws. Proteus intervenes, then threatens to force her when she still refuses him. Valentine stops the assault. Proteus repents, yet Valentine appears to offer him his claim in Silvia; Julia faints and reveals herself. Proteus returns to Julia, the Duke accepts Valentine, and two marriages are proposed with striking speed.

Read closely

Three ways into the writing

These are not themes to collect. They are patterns to follow through particular scenes, voices and choices.

Close reading

Proteus and the failure of constancy

Proteus carries the name of a shape-changing sea god, and his language repeatedly remakes betrayal as necessity. He knows that pursuing Silvia requires him to be false to Julia, Valentine and his own former self. The soliloquies do not hide this knowledge; they show intelligence being used to license appetite.

Valentine, whose name suggests steadfast devotion, appears more constant but is also capable of treating Silvia as part of an exchange between men. The title’s ‘gentlemen’ therefore invites examination rather than simple respect. Birth, education and courtly speech do not guarantee the ethical conduct the word is supposed to describe.

Close reading

Julia and Silvia refuse the parts written for them

Julia’s disguise lets her cross the distance that Proteus expects to keep under his control. As Sebastian, she hears his new declarations and is ordered to deliver her own ring to another woman. Her private comparisons with Silvia reveal pain and insecurity, but the disguise also makes her the best-informed person in the plot.

Silvia refuses Proteus throughout. She recognises the ring, defends Julia and rejects the idea that persistence can convert treachery into love. Her response to the final settlement is not supplied in a large explanatory speech. A production must decide whether her silence signals shock, resistance, conventional acceptance or a refusal the men have failed to hear.

Close reading

Why Crab matters

Launce’s scenes with Crab can look detached from the main plot, yet they turn loyalty into something physical and costly. Launce takes the blame for the dog’s behaviour and describes a devotion that survives embarrassment, punishment and the complete absence of gratitude.

That comic fidelity exposes Proteus more sharply than another moral lecture would. It also gives performance an unpredictable living presence: Crab may ignore cues, steal attention or make Launce’s claims about him visibly doubtful. The play’s most famous joke therefore depends on the gap between an idealised story of loyalty and the creature actually standing on stage.

Book, theatre, history

The play in context

Dating, source and performance matter most when they return us to something the text is doing.

Career

An early comedy finding its forms

The play contains structures that Shakespeare later developed with greater complexity: rivalry between male friends, letters that travel badly, a woman disguised as a page and a forest beyond civic authority. Its unevenness is part of its interest. Readers can watch a playwright testing how romantic convention, clowning and moral danger might occupy the same stage.

Source

Felismena’s journey

The closest narrative source is the Felismena episode in Montemayor’s Diana. An English translation by Bartholomew Yong was printed in 1598, though the story may have circulated earlier in manuscript or reached Shakespeare by another route. Shakespeare transforms its devoted disguised heroine into Julia and places her journey within a play about friendship and gentlemanly education.

Performance

The problem at the end

The final forest scene has become central to modern performance because its language resists a comfortable rush towards marriage. Directors must stage Proteus’s threat, Valentine’s intervention, Silvia’s silence and Julia’s disclosure in immediate succession. Cuts, pauses and physical reactions can either conceal the rupture or make the promised comic settlement answer to it.

Characters

Principal characters

Valentine

A young Veronese gentleman who leaves home for Milan, falls in love with Silvia and is banished after Proteus betrays his escape plan. His loyalty is tested again by his response to Proteus in the final scene.

Proteus

Valentine’s friend and Julia’s pledged lover. Brilliant at turning change into argument, he abandons both relationships to pursue Silvia and becomes the play’s most direct test of what gentlemanly language can conceal.

Julia

Proteus’s first love, who travels to Milan disguised as the page Sebastian. Her journey exposes his betrayal while demanding that she perform service to the man whose conduct has injured her.

Silvia

The Duke of Milan’s daughter and Valentine’s beloved. She resists her father’s preferred marriage, rejects Proteus’s advances and remains morally clear even when the men around her treat her future as theirs to arrange.

Launce

Proteus’s servant and Crab’s devoted owner. His patient, wandering stories slow the romantic plot and reveal forms of affection, sacrifice and common sense that his socially superior master lacks.

Speed

Valentine’s quick-witted servant. He reads gestures and love letters more accurately than his master, using wordplay to expose the gap between romantic self-image and what an observer can plainly see.

Carry through the text

Questions that stay open

Use them to test a scene, a performance choice or an argument of your own.

01

What makes a gentleman?

Gather the qualities attached to education, birth, friendship and conduct across the play. Which characters behave according to those standards, and how far does the title become ironic by the final scene?

02

What does disguise give Julia?

Compare what Julia can say, see and do as herself with what becomes possible as Sebastian. Does the disguise grant agency, intensify her suffering, or make both effects inseparable?

03

Can the ending be a reconciliation?

Study Proteus’s repentance, Valentine’s response and Silvia’s silence without assuming that the promised marriages settle them. What choices of pause, movement and tone would make the ending comic, critical or deliberately unresolved?
