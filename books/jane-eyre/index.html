Charlotte Brontë

Jane Eyre

Jane Eyre is an orphan at Gateshead, shut in the red room by her aunt, and then a pupil at Lowood, a charity school where the girls go hungry. At eighteen she becomes governess at Thornfield Hall, where Mr Rochester lives and where she hears laughter from the third storey. Charlotte Brontë published the novel in 1847 as Currer Bell.

About the work

Jane’s story, from Gateshead to Ferndean.

The account below follows Jane through Gateshead, Lowood, Thornfield and Moor House, and the question of how much of herself she should surrender for love, duty, respectability or safety.

The work

What happens.

Jane begins as an orphan dependent on the Reed family at Gateshead. Punished for resisting her bullying cousin and treated as an unwelcome burden, she is sent to Lowood School, where deprivation, disease and harsh discipline shape the principles that govern her adult life.

As a governess at Thornfield Hall, Jane meets Edward Fairfax Rochester. Their attachment offers a home and equality she has never possessed, but a concealed truth about Rochester’s existing marriage makes their wedding impossible. Jane leaves Thornfield with almost nothing, because staying would mean living with Rochester as his mistress.

Reading the work

Reading it whole.

Her later life with the Rivers family, an unexpected inheritance and St John Rivers’s proposal present different forms of dependence. Again and again Jane has to decide how much of herself to give up for love, duty, respectability or safety.

The supporting material follows the Gothic inheritance of Thornfield, Victorian class and the governess, marriage and property law, madness and confinement, Jamaica and empire, religion, gender and the novel’s direct address to its reader.

A closer look

Publication, text and historical context.

The preface and the note

Brontë’s Preface to the second edition is dated 21 December 1847 and signed Currer Bell. It thanks readers and reviewers, answers objections about the novel’s religious tendency and dedicates the new edition to William Makepeace Thackeray. These are responses to the reception of an already published book; the first edition had no corresponding Preface.

The Note to the Third Edition, dated 13 April 1848, has a more specific purpose. Currer Bell states that the claim to be a novelist rests on Jane Eyre alone and corrects the attribution of other works of fiction to the same author. Beside the Brontës’ pseudonyms, the document shows a practical problem about who the author was; it changes nothing in Jane’s story.

Teaching as paid work

Jane spends eight years at Lowood, six as a pupil and two as a teacher. She obtains the Thornfield post by placing an advertisement and corresponding about a position teaching a child under fourteen. Mrs Fairfax offers a salary of thirty pounds a year. The employment is negotiated before Jane knows Rochester or understands the household into which she is entering.

Her later work at the village school near Moor House provides a different teaching situation. She begins with limited expectations of the pupils but comes to recognise their individual abilities and progress. Comparing the two posts keeps Jane’s independence connected to a skill she can exchange for income. An inheritance changes her financial position later; it does not erase the years in which she supports herself by teaching.

Reading, drawing and the adult narrator

The first chapter introduces Jane as a reader looking at Bewick’s History of British Birds behind a window curtain. Books recur as possessions, sources of images and occasions for judgement. Her drawings also become evidence of an inner life that others do not immediately understand: Rochester examines her portfolio and questions her about its unusual subjects.

The story is presented as the autobiography of an adult recounting her earlier life. Jane can reproduce a child’s fear while also explaining circumstances that the child could not then assess. Her direct addresses to the reader make the act of telling visible. The final chapter explicitly locates the account ten years into her marriage, giving readers a point from which to understand the distance between narrated experience and retrospective statement.

Religion

Brocklehurst, Helen Burns, Miss Temple and St John Rivers do not represent a single religious position. Brocklehurst enforces deprivation on the pupils while his own family enjoys luxury; Helen advises endurance and forgiveness; Miss Temple investigates Jane’s treatment and offers practical care. St John later asks Jane to adopt missionary work as a vocation under his direction. The differences are established through conduct and conversation, allowing the novel’s religious arguments to be examined through particular relationships.
