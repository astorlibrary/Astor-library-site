Biography

Melville’s life.

Herman Melville was born in New York City in 1819; both his grandfathers were heroes of the Revolution. His father’s import business failed, and he died in debt in 1832, when Herman was twelve. After that the boy’s schooling came and went. He worked as a clerk and schoolteacher, studied surveying and, in 1839, crossed the Atlantic as a crew member on the merchant ship St Lawrence. Two years later he signed the articles of the Acushnet, a whaler bound for the Pacific. He left the ship in the Marquesas, travelled through Tahiti and Hawaii, and eventually returned to the United States in 1844 aboard the naval frigate United States.

The voyages gave him his material and his first reputation, as the man who had lived among cannibals. Typee (1846) and Omoo (1847) mix remembered travel with invention, ethnography, satire and adventure, and they sold well. Mardi (1849) puzzled the readers those books had won; Redburn (1849) and White-Jacket (1850) went back to the sea he had known. In 1847 he married Elizabeth Shaw, daughter of the Chief Justice of Massachusetts. In 1850 they bought Arrowhead, a farmhouse near Pittsfield in the Berkshires, and there he finished Moby-Dick.

In August 1850 he met Nathaniel Hawthorne on a picnic on Monument Mountain, and found in him the reader he wanted for the darker book he was writing; Moby-Dick is dedicated to Hawthorne. The British edition, The Whale, came out first, in three volumes in October 1851; the American Moby-Dick; or, The Whale followed in one volume in November. The two differ in wording and arrangement, and the British edition left out the Epilogue that tells how Ishmael survived. Every modern copy rests on an editor’s choices between them.

Moby-Dick sold poorly, and one New York paper greeted Pierre (1852) with the headline “Herman Melville Crazy”. He kept experimenting, in the magazine stories collected as The Piazza Tales (1856), which include “Bartleby” and “Benito Cereno”, in Israel Potter (1855) and in The Confidence-Man (1857), while his readers and his income fell away. From 1866 to 1885 he worked as a customs inspector on the New York docks and wrote mostly poetry, including the long poem Clarel (1876). He died in 1891, leaving Billy Budd unfinished in manuscript; it was first published in 1924, during the revival that made him famous again.

How the writing works

Reading Melville closely.

01

Ishmael

Ishmael starts with his own restlessness (“a damp, drizzly November in my soul”), becomes an observer of New Bedford, and then at times disappears into a voice that seems to know the whole ship, the whale and the history of whaling. He is one survivor building a world big enough to hold an event that was too big for him. Mark each change from “I” to “we”, from memory to lecture, and from what he saw to what he imagines.

02

Form

Adventure gives way to sermon, stage play, cetology, anatomy, legal argument, comic dialogue and metaphysics. A chapter may move the chase on or stop to look at a rope, a picture or the colour white. The changes keep Ahab’s plot from taking over the whole book. Instead of asking which chapters you can skip, ask what each form lets Ishmael know, and what stays unknown after he has classified it.

03

Work and meaning

Ahab makes the whale stand for his injury and demands that a crew hired for profit accept his private reading of it. Around him Melville keeps showing bodies, tools, oil, risk and shared labour: the Pequod is a symbol and a working ship. Follow who handles the ropes, who lowers in the boats and who shares the danger. The physical detail carries the book’s largest questions, and it also stands against a captain who treats people as tools.

A route through the voyage

Reading the forms used in Moby-Dick.

Start before the famous first sentence. “Etymology” and “Extracts”, supplied by a pale usher and a sub-sub-librarian, gather authorities, fragments and mistakes about the whale, and none of them gets hold of the creature. Keep that unfinished archive in mind when later chapters classify species or measure skeletons: the book loves knowledge and never supposes that piling it up will give final possession.

Follow Ishmael and Queequeg through New Bedford and Nantucket before Ahab takes over. The shared bed at the Spouter-Inn, Father Mapple’s sermon in the Whaleman’s Chapel, the signing on and the owners’ haggling over Ishmael’s lay set out hospitality, belief and commercial risk. Note what Ishmael gets wrong about Queequeg at first and what living alongside him corrects. Their friendship is another way of living with someone, long before Queequeg’s coffin saves Ishmael’s life.

Once the Pequod sails, keep two records side by side. In one, trace how Ahab turns the voyage into a hunt: the gold doubloon nailed to the mast, the oath on the quarter-deck, his control of information, his meetings with other captains. In the other, trace the ship’s actual work: lookouts, boats, cutting-in, boiling the oil and mending the gear. Starbuck sees a moral wrong and a broken contract: “I came here to hunt whales, not my commander’s vengeance.” The ship was sent out for oil, and its captain keeps spending men and equipment on a purpose they did not choose.

Give the whale-science chapters and the chapters set out as a play your full attention. The classifications show thought reaching for a subject that will not keep still; the stage form gives us soliloquies Ishmael could not have overheard. Then compare the final chase with the Epilogue, and if you can, compare the British and American first editions in the Melville Electronic Library. Without the Epilogue the British text seemed to leave nobody alive to tell the story, and some early reviewers said so.
