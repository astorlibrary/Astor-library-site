Biography

Mary Shelley’s life.

Mary Wollstonecraft Godwin was born in London in 1797. Her mother, Mary Wollstonecraft, the author of A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, died eleven days later, and her father, the philosopher William Godwin, brought her up in a house full of books, political argument and visitors. In 1814, at sixteen, she ran away to the Continent with the poet Percy Bysshe Shelley, who was married; her stepsister Claire Clairmont went with them. Her father refused to see her, money was always short, and of their four children only one, Percy Florence, lived to grow up. They married in December 1816, a few weeks after Shelley’s first wife drowned herself.

In June 1816, by Lake Geneva, Lord Byron suggested that each of his circle write a ghost story. Mary’s became Frankenstein, published anonymously in January 1818. She drew on the Gothic, travel writing, natural philosophy, Paradise Lost and arguments about education and political responsibility. The surviving notebooks show the work: Mary drafted and fair-copied the novel, Percy made changes and suggestions, and the design and the authorship are hers.

Percy Shelley drowned off the Italian coast in 1822, and Mary, a widow at twenty-four, came back to London to support herself and her son by writing. Her novels include Valperga (1823), The Last Man (1826), a story of a plague that leaves one survivor, Lodore (1835) and Falkner (1837). She wrote travel books and biographical essays, and edited Percy’s poems with notes that are still read. She revised Frankenstein for a new edition in 1831, and died in London in 1851.

How the writing works

Reading Shelley closely.

01

Narrators

Walton writes to his sister; Victor tells Walton his history; the creature gives Victor another life story inside it. Each speaker asks to be believed, and each makes the listener a judge. Shelley never lets the reader forget who is telling each part of the story, or why.

02

Creation and conduct

The moment of creation takes a paragraph. Most of the novel is about what Victor does afterwards: he runs away, keeps silent, gives chase, and again and again refuses the relationship he made possible. The book keeps asking what a maker owes to what he has made.

03

Landscape

Alpine valleys, Arctic ice and violent storms give private anguish a world big enough to move through. The glacier below Montanvert steadies Victor for a moment, and it is there that the creature finds him. Sublime scenery can lift a mind; it cannot settle what Victor owes.

A route through the novel

Reading the narrators in Frankenstein.

Begin by marking the listener in every account. Walton addresses Margaret Saville, Victor tries to govern Walton’s judgement, and the creature must persuade the person who already fears him. Ask what each narrator wants from the act of telling: warning, sympathy, vindication, companionship or action.

Next, separate the creation from what follows it. Victor often talks as if his fate was sealed long before (“Destiny was too potent”). The plot keeps bringing him back to later choices: whether to stay, speak, protect, promise or confess, as when he lets Justine hang rather than tell what he knows. Fate does not excuse what he does.

Finally, compare the first edition of 1818 with Shelley’s revision of 1831, which is the text Astor follows. In 1831 she changed wording and relationships (Elizabeth becomes an orphan taken in by the family, where in 1818 she was Victor’s cousin), gave destiny more weight, and added the introduction that tells how the novel began. Know which text you are reading.
