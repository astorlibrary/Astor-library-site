Biography

Hardy’s life.

Thomas Hardy was born in a cottage at Higher Bockhampton, near Dorchester, to Thomas Hardy, a builder and stonemason, and Jemima Hand Hardy. He grew up with fiddle music (his father and grandfather had played in the Stinsford church band), churchgoing, local stories and the trades of a country parish. At sixteen he was apprenticed to the Dorchester architect John Hicks. In 1862 he moved to London to work for the architect Arthur Blomfield; one of his jobs there was to oversee the removal of graves from Old St Pancras churchyard for the new railway, and in the evenings he wrote poems that the magazines turned down.

He came back to Dorset in 1867 and began to write fiction. His first novel, The Poor Man and the Lady, was turned down and never published; Desperate Remedies (1871) and Under the Greenwood Tree (1872) appeared anonymously. Restoring a church took him to St Juliot in Cornwall in 1870, where he met Emma Gifford, the rector's sister-in-law. They married in 1874, the year Far from the Madding Crowd, serialised in the Cornhill, made his name. From then on his novels came out first as magazine serials, cut to suit editors, and were revised for the books.

In 1885 the Hardys moved into Max Gate, a house outside Dorchester that he designed himself and his family's firm built. The architect never left him: the fiction notices thresholds, materials, sightlines and the older ground beneath a building. While the foundations were being dug, the workmen found Romano-British burials, and Hardy wrote a paper about them for the Dorset Natural History and Antiquarian Field Club.

How the novel works

The subtitle, the land and the revisions.

01

The subtitle

Hardy added the subtitle, A Pure Woman Faithfully Presented, for the book, and reviewers fought over it. It challenges the moral words applied to Tess. Whenever a character sums up her history as a verdict, compare that confidence with the narrator’s distance, irony and moments of closeness to her. The novel questions the standards before it questions any one judgement.

02

Work and landscape

Marlott, Trantridge, Talbothays and Flintcomb-Ash are places of work. Note who owns the land, who travels, what work each season needs and how the threshing machine sets the pace for human bodies. Beauty and exploitation can share a field.

03

Revisions

For the 1891 serial in The Graphic Hardy cut and altered the story for a family readership: Tess is tricked by a mock marriage, the baby and its baptism disappear, and Angel wheels the milkmaids through the flooded lane in a wheelbarrow instead of carrying them in his arms. The book put most of it back, and Hardy went on revising later editions.

Later work

Wessex, Tess and the poems.

Hardy borrowed the name of the old Saxon kingdom of Wessex for Dorset and the counties round it, renaming, combining and moving real places: Dorchester becomes Casterbridge, Salisbury Melchester, Oxford Christminster. Readers soon came looking for the places, and Hardy, though he drew a map of Wessex for his collected edition, called it in a preface of 1895 ‘a partly real, partly dream-country’. It is exact about roads, soils, distances and kinds of work, and arranged for the story.

In Tess of the d'Urbervilles country custom is neither a comforting museum nor mere ignorance. The novel records the women's club-walking in May, dialect, dairying and field work beside railways, tourists, threshing machines and changing beliefs, and Tess, who has been to the village school, speaks dialect at home and ordinary English elsewhere. Her troubles do not come from fate alone. Poverty, a double standard for men and women, lack of information and the power of employers, clergymen and men in general all make an accident harder for her to survive.

The manuscript, now in the British Library, carries the marks of this. Two magazines turned the novel down before The Graphic took it, and it ran there from 4 July to 26 December 1891. Hardy published the two cut episodes separately, the baptism as ‘The Midnight Baptism’ and the night in the Chase as ‘Saturday Night in Arcady’, before the three-volume book restored them. Its working title had been A Daughter of the d'Urbervilles.

Jude the Obscure (1895) caused an even fiercer row; a bishop announced that he had burned his copy. Hardy wrote no more novels. He had been writing poems since his twenties, and now he gave the rest of his life to them: Wessex Poems (1898) was followed by seven more collections, about memory, war, belief, grief, animals, marriage and change, and by The Dynasts (1904–08), a vast drama of the Napoleonic Wars. The second career lasted thirty years.

The marriage to Emma had long been unhappy. When she died in 1912 Hardy went back to Cornwall and wrote the ‘Poems of 1912–13’ about her, their courtship and the years of estrangement. He married Florence Dugdale in 1914 and died at Max Gate on 11 January 1928. After an argument about where he should lie, his ashes were buried in Poets' Corner, Westminster Abbey, and his heart at Stinsford, with his family.

A route through the novel

Reading Tess’s choices and constraints.

Read the first two phases as a chain of money. Parson Tringham's chance remark gives John Durbeyfield a fantasy of noble ancestry; the death of the horse, Prince, leaves Tess guilty and the family without its living. Her journey to the d'Urbervilles follows from both. At Trantridge, keep in view the differences of money, freedom of movement, age and knowledge between Tess and Alec when you read his pursuit and the night in the Chase.

At Talbothays, enjoy the pastoral interlude without mistaking it for an escape from society. Tess works, makes friends and imagines a future. Angel's advanced opinions sit alongside a gaze that idealises her. The two confessions on the wedding night are the test: Angel owns to “eight-and-forty hours’ dissipation with a stranger” in London and expects forgiveness, then cannot forgive Tess.

In the final phases, follow work and letters. At Flintcomb-Ash the weather and the threshing machine wear down the women's bodies. Letters go astray, like the confession Tess pushed under Angel's door before the wedding, which went under the carpet as well and was never read; rescue depends on knowledge and money Tess does not have. At Stonehenge the scale changes for a moment from Victorian judgement to ancient ground, but dawn brings the police. Then read the last image carefully: the black flag over the prison, and Angel and ’Liza-Lu, who watch it and go on.
