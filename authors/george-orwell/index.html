Biography

Orwell’s life.

Eric Arthur Blair was born in Motihari, where his father worked in the Opium Department of the colonial civil service, and grew up chiefly in England. A scholarship took him to Eton. Instead of going to university, he joined the Indian Imperial Police in Burma in 1922, and for five years he was part of the machinery of empire. He resigned in 1927. Opposition to imperial rule stayed with him, and essays such as “A Hanging” and “Shooting an Elephant” look hard at his own part in it.

Back in Europe he set out to learn about poverty at first hand, washing dishes in a Paris hotel kitchen and sleeping in London lodging houses and casual wards. He could always leave, as the people around him could not, and he said later that everything in the book he made of it had happened, though not always in the order he gave. Down and Out in Paris and London (1933) was the first book published under the name George Orwell. Burmese Days (1934) went back to colonial Burma; The Road to Wigan Pier (1937) reported on housing, mining and unemployment in the north of England and then argued with English socialists.

In December 1936 Orwell went to Spain, joined the militia of the POUM, a small anti-Stalinist party, and fought on the Republican side. In May 1937 a sniper’s bullet went through his throat. A few weeks later the Republican authorities, under Communist pressure, suppressed the POUM, and Orwell and his wife Eileen escaped to France. In Homage to Catalonia (1938) he described events he had seen for himself being rewritten in the party press. He opposed Stalin from the left: “Every line of serious work that I have written since 1936,” he wrote in 1946, “has been written, directly or indirectly, against totalitarianism and for democratic socialism, as I understand it.” That matters when you read Animal Farm.

During the Second World War, Orwell worked for the BBC’s Eastern Service and then became literary editor of Tribune. He wrote Animal Farm in 1943–44, when the Soviet Union was Britain’s wartime ally. Several publishers turned it down, T. S. Eliot at Faber among them, and it finally appeared from Secker & Warburg in August 1945. Eileen had died during an operation that March. Orwell went on reporting, reviewing and writing essays as tuberculosis took hold, and finished Nineteen Eighty-Four in a remote farmhouse on the island of Jura. It was published in June 1949, and he died in a London hospital in January 1950. Both late books describe things Orwell had already seen happen: power gathered into a few hands, records altered, memory coerced and language cut down until some thoughts cannot be put into words.

How the writing works

Reading Orwell closely.

01

Fable and allegory

The main figures and events match the Russian Revolution and Stalin’s rule: Napoleon resembles Stalin, Snowball Trotsky. That key is only a start. Boxer is a horse before he stands for anything, and the farm runs on bodies that need feeding. Ask what the animal story lets you see: work, hunger, fear, and who ends up with what the animals produce.

02

Power and language

Power on the farm works through words as well as through the dogs. A commandment gains two words; a speech offers figures nobody can check; a question becomes disloyalty. Set each new wording beside the event it was made to excuse.

03

Repetition

Slogans, meetings, harvests and anniversaries come round again, and each time they do a different job. Early on, “Beasts of England” and “Four legs good, two legs bad” hold the animals together; later the sheep bleat the slogan to drown out argument. The narrator seldom comments. He sets the official claim beside the hunger, the exhaustion or the animal who has disappeared, and leaves it there. Keep a note of who works, who eats, who remembers and who can write.

A route through the fable

Reading political language in Animal Farm.

Begin with Old Major’s speech and separate what he diagnoses from what he predicts. He blames Man for the animals’ hunger and overwork, then imagines a future in which all animals are comrades. Mark which parts become Animalism and which the pigs later drop or reinterpret. That gives you a measure from inside the book: you can judge the rebellion by history, and also by the animals’ own promises.

Track the Seven Commandments as writing on the barn wall. Note which animals read well, which know only a few letters and which depend on someone reading aloud. Each time a commandment seems to have changed (about beds, about alcohol, about killing), look at what has just happened. The new wording follows an advantage the pigs have already taken, and makes it harder to challenge afterwards.

Judge Snowball and Napoleon by what they do as well as by whom they resemble. Compare their plans, their use of education (Napoleon takes the puppies away to bring up himself) and their use of force. Then follow Squealer: any question about present conditions becomes a warning that Jones will come back, and he reads out figures nobody can check. Ask why it works. Tiredness, poor memory, lack of information and the dogs beside him do more than his rhetoric.

Give particular attention to Boxer, Clover, Benjamin and the hens. They show four ways of living under the pigs: Boxer works, Clover stays loyal, Benjamin doubts and the hens rebel. Boxer’s two maxims, “I will work harder” and “Napoleon is always right”, stop him thinking while his strength keeps the farm going; Benjamin sees what is happening and does almost nothing about it. In the last chapter, compare the farm’s name, its commandments, its owners and its trade with the hopes of the first. The final sentence, where the animals can no longer tell pig from man, works because Orwell has recorded every small, deniable step on the way.
