Biography

Milton’s life.

Milton was born in Bread Street, London, in 1608. His father was a scrivener, who drew up legal documents and arranged loans, and a composer good enough to be published, and he paid for an unusually wide education. At St Paul’s School and with private tutors Milton learned Latin, Greek, Hebrew, French and Italian. He went up to Christ’s College, Cambridge, in 1625, where he was nicknamed ‘the Lady of Christ’s’, and stayed until his MA in 1632. Then he spent nearly six years reading at his father’s houses in Hammersmith and Horton, working through classical literature, scripture, history and modern European writing in preparation for the great poem he meant to write.

The early poems already have the range the epic would gather up. L’Allegro and Il Penseroso set a cheerful and a thoughtful temperament side by side; Comus, a masque performed at Ludlow Castle in 1634, puts a moral argument on stage; Lycidas (1638), an elegy for a drowned Cambridge contemporary, turns aside to attack the corrupt clergy. In 1638–39 Milton travelled through France and Italy, where he met Hugo Grotius in Paris and visited Galileo, old and a prisoner of the Inquisition, near Florence. He came home as the quarrel between Charles I and Parliament grew, and for twenty years wrote mostly prose.

In 1642 he married Mary Powell, who went home to her royalist family within weeks; the next year he published the first of his tracts arguing for divorce on grounds of incompatibility. He also wrote on church government, education and censorship. Areopagitica (1644) attacked Parliament’s licensing of books before publication, though Milton left Catholic writing out of the toleration he argued for. After the execution of Charles I in 1649 he defended the people’s right to depose a tyrant, and the new Commonwealth made him Secretary for Foreign Tongues, writing its Latin letters and answering its critics abroad. The habits of argument and rebuttal he learned there are heard all through his verse.

Milton was completely blind by 1652, the year Mary died, leaving him with three young daughters. He went on working by dictation to whoever was at hand: relatives, paid assistants, friends. His early biographers say that he composed Paradise Lost in his head, often at night, and dictated it in the morning in batches of lines. It was made to be heard, and its sentences are carried by breath, rhythm and memory before they settle on the page. A poem so concerned with freedom and service was written by a man who depended on other people every day.

The Restoration of Charles II in 1660 ended the republic Milton had served. His books were burned by the hangman, he went into hiding, and he was arrested and briefly imprisoned before friends secured his release. Paradise Lost appeared in ten books in 1667 and in twelve in 1674; Paradise Regained and Samson Agonistes were published together in 1671. He died in London in November 1674. The late poems were written after political defeat and private loss, and they ask what freedom, responsibility and hope are left when the public cause has failed.

How the writing works

Reading Milton closely.

01

Syntax

A line may end long before its sentence does. The poem’s first sentence runs sixteen lines and reaches its main verb, “Sing”, only in the sixth. Milton delays verbs, puts qualifications between subject and action and lets a sentence change direction across many lines. Find the main clause first, then put the other parts back one at a time, and notice the order in which facts and judgements arrive.

02

Rhetoric

Satan can make defeat sound like courage and grievance like liberty: “Better to reign in Hell, than serve in Heaven.” His speeches persuade, which is why they matter, and the poem does not have to agree with them. Compare what a speaker claims with what the narrator says, what happens next and the images round the voice. A long simile can make a figure huge and then small. Satan’s shield hangs on his shoulders like the moon seen through Galileo’s telescope; by the end of the same book his followers have shrunk smaller than dwarfs to crowd into Pandemonium. Watch for the moment when eloquence becomes a way of hiding from oneself.

03

Conversation

The council in Hell and the war in Heaven give the poem its huge scale, but the Fall turns on a conversation between two people about work, trust, being apart and knowledge. Adam and Eve have their parts in a theological scheme, and their language changes as they listen, persuade, hide things and blame each other. Set the public words, liberty and obedience, beside these exchanges at home: the poem thinks through its politics and religion in relationships as well as in declarations.

A route through the poem

Reading the syntax of Paradise Lost.

Before each book, read its prose Argument: knowing what happens frees your attention for the language. When a sentence gets dense, don’t start again at every line. Mark the subject, the main verb and the object, underline the main clause lightly, then attach each qualification. Reading a short passage aloud often lets you hear the structure before you can see it.

In Books I and II, keep two records for Satan: what he says has happened, and what the narrator and the plot show. He keeps renaming loss, necessity and defeat so that he can go on seeing himself as unconquered (“The mind is its own place”). In the debate in Pandemonium, Moloch, Belial and Mammon propose war, waiting and building, but none of them questions the revolt itself, and the plan that Beelzebub carries was Satan’s all along.

Books III to VIII widen the view: Heaven, Satan’s arrival in Eden, Raphael’s account of the war in Heaven and the Creation, and Adam’s memory of waking into life. They are part of the story, not a delay before it. They set out competing accounts of knowledge and rank, and in Adam and Eve giving an angel a meal in their garden they offer hospitality, teaching and conversation in place of the solitary intelligence Satan admires.

Slow down further in Book IX. Adam and Eve’s disagreement about working apart sets the terms Satan will use. Track who changes another person’s words, and who mistakes love, independence or knowledge for a whole argument. After the fruit is eaten, watch the grammar and the pronouns: the talk moves quickly from “we” to concealment, accusation and each trying to make the other carry the choice.

In the last two books the archangel Michael shows Adam the future: murder, war, the Flood, the law and the promise of redemption. Adam learns from it, and it belongs to the poem as much as the Fall does. The ending gives the couple knowledge, grief, responsibility and a future outside the garden. Read the last lines beside the opening: the poem that began by announcing the loss of Eden ends with two people walking out into the world, hand in hand.

For further reading

College, cottage and the early editions.

Christ’s College, Cambridge, has a detailed account of Milton’s life, from his student years to the late poems. Dartmouth’s Milton Reading Room has an annotated text of the poems. The New York Public Library shows a copy of the 1667 first edition, and Milton’s Cottage in Chalfont St Giles, where he went in 1665 to escape the plague, is where he finished the poem.
