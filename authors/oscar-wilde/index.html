Biography

Wilde’s life.

Oscar Fingal O'Flahertie Wills Wilde was born in Dublin in 1854 to two well-known parents. Sir William Wilde was an eye and ear surgeon and an antiquarian; Jane Wilde wrote nationalist verse as ‘Speranza’ and kept a literary salon. Oscar went to Portora Royal School in Enniskillen, read classics at Trinity College Dublin from 1871 to 1874, winning the Berkeley Gold Medal for Greek, and then went to Magdalen College, Oxford, where he took a double first and won the Newdigate Prize for poetry. At Oxford he heard Ruskin lecture and read Walter Pater, and the arguments about art, beauty and criticism he met there run through everything he wrote.

In London he became famous before he had written much, for his clothes, his talk and his championing of the aesthetic movement, which Punch mocked and Gilbert and Sullivan satirised in Patience (1881). In 1882 he spent a year lecturing across the United States and Canada, on a tour arranged partly to publicise Patience. The famous sayings hide how hard he worked: poems, reviews, two years editing The Woman's World (1887–89), fairy tales, critical dialogues, a novel and, between 1892 and 1895, four successful comedies.

Wilde married Constance Lloyd in 1884, and their sons Cyril and Vyvyan were born in 1885 and 1886. From about 1886 he had relationships with men, first with Robert Ross and later with Lord Alfred Douglas, at a time when the law punished sex between men with prison. The comedies are not about his own household, but double lives run through them: Jack is “Ernest in town and Jack in the country”. Wilde makes concealment funny, up to the point where it stops being funny.

How the novel works

Epigrams, looking and two texts.

01

Epigrams

Lord Henry speaks in polished reversals that sound finished before anyone has tested them: “The only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it.” Hear the rhythm, then set the sentence beside the lives around him. A line can be seductive, funny and morally hollow at once, and Wilde does not let one quality cancel the others.

02

Looking

Basil paints, Henry watches, Dorian learns to study himself, and the portrait records what his face hides. Looking gives some people power over others. Track when a person becomes a work of art, an audience or an experiment, and ask whose reading decides what can be seen.

03

Two texts

The story first appeared in Lippincott's Monthly Magazine in July 1890, already cut by the magazine’s editor. After hostile reviews Wilde expanded it from thirteen chapters to twenty for the 1891 book, added the Preface and toned down passages about Basil’s feelings for Dorian. The book is a record of revision and self-censorship as well as a novel.

Other writing

Wilde’s other work.

The Picture of Dorian Gray is Wilde's only novel, and it is best read beside his criticism. In the essays and dialogues collected in Intentions (1891), Wilde treats criticism as a creative art and doubts that a work of art simply copies reality. The novel puts those ideas into characters. Basil believes the portrait gives away too much of the painter. Henry treats influence as an elegant game. Dorian tries to keep what he looks like apart from what he does, and the portrait records everything he does.

The fairy tales in The Happy Prince (1888) and A House of Pomegranates (1891) show another Wilde: tender, satirical, alert to suffering and suspicious of people who admire beauty and put up with cruelty. In the comedies, secrets drive the plot. Names, engagements, a handbag, letters and double lives get laughs because social identity rests on performances everyone agrees to accept. The Importance of Being Earnest opened on 14 February 1895, with An Ideal Husband still running across town. Seven weeks later Wilde was in court.

Lord Alfred Douglas’s father, the Marquess of Queensberry, had left a card at Wilde’s club accusing him of posing as a sodomite. Wilde sued for libel and lost, and the evidence from that case led to his arrest. He was convicted of gross indecency on 25 May 1895 and given the maximum sentence, two years’ imprisonment with hard labour. The story is often told as one man’s recklessness, but the law that sent him to prison is the larger part of it.

In Reading Gaol he wrote a long letter to Douglas, later published as De Profundis. Released in 1897, bankrupt and cut off from his sons, whose surname Constance had changed to Holland, he lived mainly in France under the name Sebastian Melmoth and published The Ballad of Reading Gaol in 1898. He died in a Paris hotel on 30 November 1900, aged forty-six.

A route through the novel

Reading ideas and evidence in Dorian Gray.

Begin in Basil's studio by separating three positions. Basil fears what he has placed of himself in the portrait. Henry enjoys influence as talk and performance. Dorian has not yet learned to repeat Henry's language. Mark how quickly a theory about beauty becomes a wish about time, and how the wish moves the consequences from Dorian’s face to the hidden picture.

Through Sibyl Vane's story, test the aesthetic claims against another person's life. Dorian values her first as art and rejects her when experience changes her acting. Henry treats her death as material for a clever explanation; Basil tries to protest. Ask which man speaks for Wilde, and also what each way of speaking lets its owner notice, evade or make into entertainment.

In the later chapters, follow objects and absences: the locked schoolroom, the covered portrait, the yellow French book Henry sends him, the eighteen years that pass in a single chapter and the friends whose lives he has damaged. Dorian's face remains readable to society only because the evidence is elsewhere. On reaching the final scene, return to the Preface. Its claims about art will now sound different. The plot supplies no easy moral, but you have watched someone try to keep beauty, responsibility and interpretation in separate rooms, and fail.
