Edinburgh, illness and experiment

A writer trained to notice structures

Robert Lewis Balfour Stevenson was born into an Edinburgh family whose engineering work shaped the Scottish coast. His father Thomas belonged to the celebrated Stevenson lighthouse engineers; his mother was Margaret Isabella Balfour. The son was expected to enter that profession and began engineering studies at the University of Edinburgh. He later changed to law, qualified as an advocate in 1875 and did not build a legal practice. Both abandoned professions remained useful to the writer. Engineering taught attention to constructed spaces and material limits; law supplied wills, statements, testimony and the careful management of what a document can prove.

Stevenson lived with recurrent poor health from childhood. Its exact diagnosis has been debated, and the older habit of explaining every journey as a search for a cure simplifies a life in which travel was also chosen, observed and made into art. France, the Cévennes, California, Switzerland, the United States and the Pacific became places of encounter rather than picturesque backgrounds. An Inland Voyage and Travels with a Donkey in the Cévennes established the travelling essayist before the adventure novels made him widely famous.

He met Fanny Van de Grift Osbourne in France and travelled to California in 1879; they married in San Francisco the following year. Their mobile household included Fanny’s children and later moved through Europe, Britain, the United States and the Pacific. Stevenson wrote across forms and audiences: essays on style, children’s verse, short stories, historical fiction, adventure, fable and literary criticism. The apparent ease of the storyteller rests on exact decisions about pace, point of view and withheld information.

How the novella works

Three ways into Jekyll and Hyde

01

Keep Utterson inside his limits

The lawyer is steady, loyal and often wrong. He builds explanations from blackmail, inheritance and professional discretion because those are the forms he knows. Do not treat his restraint as blank neutrality: every delayed question and protected reputation helps determine how long the mystery remains unsolved.

02

Map the divided house

The respectable square and the damaged door are not two unrelated settings. They belong to one building approached from different social worlds. Follow keys, thresholds, windows, streets and rooms. The architecture makes division possible while leaving material evidence that the parts were never truly separate.

03

Read the confession as testimony

Jekyll’s final statement explains the mechanism, but it is also a self-account written at the edge of disappearance. Attend to his changes of pronoun, his distribution of blame and the point at which Hyde becomes grammatically separate. Explanation does not free the speaker from responsibility for the story he tells.

Romance, doubles and the Pacific

The famous split belongs to a varied career

Treasure Island began with a map made during a wet summer at Braemar and appeared in Young Folks before its 1883 book publication. It helped establish forms of pirate adventure that later culture now makes seem inevitable. Kidnapped, published in 1886, joins rapid movement to a contested Scottish history. Stevenson called such works romances, but romance did not mean carelessness about the world. It allowed intense selection: a landscape organised around pursuit, danger and moral choice.

The doubled self recurs before and around Jekyll and Hyde. Stevenson had collaborated on a play about the Edinburgh criminal Deacon Brodie, a respectable tradesman who maintained a secret criminal life. Short fiction such as Markheim also tests the attempt to separate action from moral identity. The 1886 novella gives that problem an unforgettable physical form, but its deepest disturbance is not the claim that every person contains one good and one evil person. Jekyll’s own account describes mixed motives, appetites, shame and self-deception that never divide so cleanly.

Stevenson later remembered that scenes of transformation came in a dream. The detailed chronology records work begun at the end of September 1885, completed in October and published by Longmans, Green on 9 January 1886. Stories of sudden inspiration can hide revision, household discussion and the formal control required to make the disclosure arrive only after Utterson’s investigation has reached a locked room.

After leaving Britain in 1887, Stevenson travelled through the Pacific and settled at Vailima in Samoa. He did not merely use the islands as exotic scenery. His later writing engaged with colonial power and Samoan political conflict, most directly in A Footnote to History. That involvement was committed but still conducted from the complicated position of a European settler and celebrated author. He died suddenly at Vailima on 3 December 1894, probably from a cerebral haemorrhage, and was buried on Mount Vaea. The unfinished Weir of Hermiston shows a career still changing.

A route through the novella

Preserve the order of discovery

For a first reading, resist solving the case in advance. Begin by recording what Utterson actually knows: Enfield’s story, the will, Hyde’s appearance and Jekyll’s assurances. Notice how often gentlemen agree not to ask. Their discretion looks humane, but it also protects a social arrangement in which appearances carry more weight than open enquiry.

From the Carew murder to the incident at the window, track handwriting, chemical orders, servants’ reports and changes in Jekyll’s sociability. Hyde is described through the effects he has on witnesses who cannot identify a precise deformity. That repeated failure is part of the method. It makes interpretation visible and prevents the reader from reducing evil to a stable bodily sign.

Read Lanyon’s narrative and Jekyll’s statement as two documents with different stakes. Lanyon records a witnessed transformation that destroys his intellectual security. Jekyll offers origin, motive and sequence, yet continues to speak as a man defending an account of himself. Then return to the first walk and the divided building. The revelation changes the evidence, but it does not make every judgement simple.
