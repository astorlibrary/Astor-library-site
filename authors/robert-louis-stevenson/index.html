Biography

Stevenson’s life.

Robert Lewis Balfour Stevenson was born in Edinburgh in 1850; at eighteen he changed the spelling of Lewis to Louis. His father, Thomas, was one of the Stevensons who built the lighthouses round the Scottish coast, and his mother was Margaret Isabella Balfour. Their only child was expected to join the firm. He began engineering at the University of Edinburgh, switched to law, was called to the Scottish bar in 1875 and never practised. Both abandoned professions stayed useful. Engineering taught him to look at how spaces are built; law gave him wills, statements and testimony, and a sense of exactly what a document can prove.

He was ill, with his chest, for most of his life, and nobody is sure what the illness was. Much of his travelling was for his health, though not all of it, and he made books out of it: a canoe trip through Belgium and France became An Inland Voyage (1878), and twelve days walking with a donkey called Modestine became Travels with a Donkey in the Cévennes (1879). He was known as an essayist and travel writer before the adventure novels made him famous.

In 1876, at an artists' colony at Grez-sur-Loing in France, he met Fanny Van de Grift Osbourne, an American ten years older than he was and separated from her husband. In 1879 he followed her to California, crossing the Atlantic and then America on an emigrant train, and they married in San Francisco in 1880. With Fanny's son Lloyd they lived in Switzerland, the south of France and then Bournemouth, where he wrote Jekyll and Hyde and Kidnapped. He wrote in almost every form: essays on style, the children's poems of A Child's Garden of Verses (1885), short stories, historical novels, adventure and fable.

How the novella works

Utterson, the house and the confession.

01

Utterson

The lawyer is steady, loyal and often wrong. He explains what he sees in terms of blackmail, inheritance and professional discretion because those are the forms he knows. His restraint is not neutral: every question he puts off and every reputation he protects keeps the mystery unsolved a little longer.

02

The house

The handsome house on the square and the blistered door in the back street turn out to be one building, reached from two different social worlds. Follow keys, thresholds, windows, streets and rooms: the architecture makes the division possible and also leaves the evidence that the two parts were never really separate.

03

Jekyll’s confession

“Henry Jekyll's Full Statement of the Case” explains how it was done, and it is also a man's account of himself, written as he is about to disappear. Watch his pronouns, how he shares out the blame, and the point where he refuses to call Hyde “I”: “He, I say—I cannot say, I.” Explaining himself does not free him from responsibility for the story he tells.

Other writing

Stevenson’s other work.

Treasure Island began in 1881 with a map of an island that Stevenson drew with his stepson during a wet summer at Braemar. It ran as a serial in the boys' paper Young Folks before the book came out in 1883, and much of what we now think of as pirate lore, from the treasure map to the parrot on Long John Silver's shoulder, comes from it. Kidnapped (1886) sends David Balfour across the Highlands after the Appin murder of 1752. Stevenson called these books romances, by which he meant selective, not careless: every landscape is arranged around pursuit, danger and moral choice.

The double life turns up before Jekyll and Hyde as well. With W. E. Henley, Stevenson wrote a play about Deacon Brodie, an Edinburgh cabinet-maker and town councillor who was a burglar by night and was hanged in 1788, and his story “Markheim” (1885) has a murderer argue with a visitor who may be his own conscience. In the 1886 novella the problem takes a body. But Jekyll's own account is not of one good man and one evil one: he describes mixed motives, appetites, shame and self-deception that never divide so cleanly.

Stevenson said that the scene at the window and a scene of transformation came to him in a dream. The rest was work. He wrote the story in Bournemouth in the autumn of 1885, burned the first draft after Fanny criticised it and wrote it again, and timed the revelation so that it comes only after Utterson's investigation has reached a locked room. Longmans, Green published it in January 1886 as a shilling paperback, and it sold forty thousand copies in Britain in six months.

After his father died in 1887 Stevenson left Britain for good. He spent a winter at Saranac Lake in New York State, sailed the Pacific in the chartered yacht Casco, and in 1890 settled at Vailima on Upolu, in Samoa, where the Samoans called him Tusitala, the teller of tales. He wrote about colonial power and the Samoan civil war, most directly in A Footnote to History (1892). He backed the chief Mata’afa against the officials whom Germany, Britain and the United States had installed to run Samoa, though he did so as a European landowner and a famous writer. He died suddenly at Vailima on 3 December 1894, probably from a cerebral haemorrhage, and was buried on Mount Vaea. He left unfinished Weir of Hermiston, a novel about a hanging judge and his son.

A route through the novella

Reading the novella’s order of discovery.

On a first reading, don't solve the case in advance. Record what Utterson actually knows: Enfield's story, the will, Hyde's appearance and Jekyll's assurances. Notice how often gentlemen agree not to ask (“the more it looks like Queer Street, the less I ask,” says Enfield). Their discretion looks kind, and it also protects a world where appearances count for more than honest questions.

From the Carew murder to the incident at the window, track handwriting, orders to the chemists, servants' reports and Jekyll's comings and goings. Hyde is described through his effect on witnesses, who all feel a deformity they cannot name. Because nobody can say what is wrong with him, evil never becomes a simple mark on the body.

Read Lanyon's narrative and Jekyll's statement as two documents with different stakes. Lanyon describes a transformation he saw with his own eyes, and the shock kills him. Jekyll gives origin, motive and sequence, and still speaks as a man defending himself. Then go back to the first walk and the divided building: the revelation changes the evidence without making every judgement simple.
