Biography

Doyle’s life.

Arthur Conan Doyle was born in Edinburgh in 1859 and studied medicine at the university there, breaking off in 1880 to sail as surgeon on an Arctic whaler. After qualifying he went as ship’s doctor on a voyage to West Africa, and in 1882 he set up in practice at Southsea, near Portsmouth, where he stayed for eight years. Patients were slow to come. In the quiet hours he wrote, and in 1887 Sherlock Holmes appeared in A Study in Scarlet. Holmes’s method owes something to Joseph Bell, the Edinburgh surgeon Doyle had worked for as a student, who could tell a patient’s occupation and recent history at a glance.

The stories are not clinical reports, though. Doyle had a gift for pace, atmosphere and the striking entrance: a client on the doorstep, a locked room, a clue that seems to mean nothing. Watson’s narrative then decides when we learn each fact. Holmes solves the case; Watson makes us want to follow it.

How the writing works

How a Holmes story works.

01

Evidence

Holmes’s deductions seem miraculous because the decisive detail was in plain view and nobody read it. As he tells Watson in “A Scandal in Bohemia”, “You see, but you do not observe.” On a second reading the solution is still surprising, but you can see that it was prepared: the clue was there all along.

02

Watson

Watson is no fool, and he is not a plain window either. He chooses the scenes, sets the atmosphere and reacts as an intelligent reader would. Because he does not see what Holmes sees, the mystery survives; because he is loyal, Holmes stays human. Every case reaches us through the friendship.

03

London

Cabs, railways, newspapers and telegrams connect late-Victorian London, and they also let people disappear into it. A stranger can arrive at Baker Street with a problem from another street, class or country. The same networks that carry Holmes to the solution made the crime possible in the first place.

A route across the shelf

Reading the case and Watson’s narration.

On the first reading, keep close to Watson. Record what seems strange and resist jumping ahead. On the second, mark exactly when each fact arrives. Doyle plays fair: not every clue is obvious, but the final explanation answers the story we have actually read.

Watch Holmes when he fails, pauses or changes his mind, as he does when Irene Adler outwits him. His method is strongest when it stays a method and does not become magic, and his corrections show more of how he thinks than the famous flashes of brilliance do.
