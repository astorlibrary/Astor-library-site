Biography

Douglass’s life.

Douglass was born Frederick Augustus Washington Bailey on Maryland’s Eastern Shore, probably in February 1818. He saw his mother, Harriet Bailey, only on a few night visits, and never knew for certain who his father was. Sent to Baltimore as a boy, he learned the alphabet from Sophia Auld until her husband stopped the lessons. He went on teaching himself, giving bread to hungry white boys in the street in return for lessons and reading The Columbian Orator, a schoolbook of speeches, whenever he could. Hugh Auld’s anger showed him that keeping enslaved people from reading was a way of controlling them.

After a failed earlier attempt, he escaped in September 1838, travelling north dressed as a sailor and carrying borrowed protection papers. Anna Murray, a free Black woman in Baltimore, helped pay for the journey and joined him in New York, where they married; they settled in New Bedford, Massachusetts. There he took the surname Douglass, suggested by his host Nathan Johnson, who had been reading Walter Scott’s The Lady of the Lake. He worked as a labourer, went to abolitionist meetings and in 1841 spoke at an antislavery convention on Nantucket, which led to his hiring as a lecturer.

The lectures raised a problem the book set out to settle. Some white listeners doubted that so eloquent a speaker could ever have been enslaved, and some of his own abolitionist allies wanted him to give the facts and leave the philosophy to them. In the Narrative (1845) Douglass named the people and places, which proved his story and told the Aulds where to find him. He sailed for Britain and Ireland that summer, and British friends bought his freedom in 1846.

He lived for fifty more years. He published My Bondage and My Freedom in 1855 and Life and Times of Frederick Douglass in 1881, revised in 1892. From 1847 he edited his own paper, The North Star, in Rochester, New York. He spoke for women’s suffrage at Seneca Falls in 1848, recruited Black soldiers during the Civil War, two of his sons among them, and later served as Marshal of the District of Columbia and as the United States’ minister to Haiti. He died in Washington in 1895. The Narrative is the first of his three accounts of his life, written when he was in his twenties and still in danger.

How the writing works

Reading Douglass closely.

01

Scenes

Douglass seldom leaves an idea abstract. The separation from his mother, Sophia Auld’s lessons and their sudden end, the songs of the enslaved and the fight with Edward Covey each show the system at work in a particular place, on particular people. The scenes are his evidence.

02

Irony and religion

Enslavers talk of Christianity, providence and moral discipline while they whip and sell people. Thomas Auld grows crueller after his conversion at a Methodist camp meeting. In the Appendix Douglass separates “the Christianity of this land” from “the Christianity of Christ”, so that his attack on hypocrisy cannot be read as an attack on faith.

03

Looking back

The man writing in 1845 understands what the boy in these scenes could not yet name. He can show a child’s question turning into political knowledge, and a few alphabet lessons becoming a road to freedom, without making the boy any less exposed.

A route through the book

Reading knowledge and resistance in the Narrative.

Begin with what Douglass was not allowed to know. He does not know his age or who his father was; children are taken from their mothers; reading is forbidden. Each of these gaps was made by people who profited from it. His precision with names, places and dates is a reply to that enforced ignorance, though he never pretends that everything lost can be recovered.

Next, mark the difference between the boy living through an event and the man writing about it. The older Douglass can explain why Hugh Auld feared his learning to read, and why the songs sung on the way to the Great House Farm, which northerners took for signs of contentment, were full of grief. As a boy, he writes, “I was myself within the circle; so that I neither saw nor heard as those without might see and hear.”

Finally, read what surrounds the story: William Lloyd Garrison’s preface and Wendell Phillips’s letter at the front, the Appendix at the back. Garrison and Phillips vouch for Douglass to a mostly white antislavery readership, and his own chapters then show an authority that needs no one’s guarantee. Ask why he chose to end the book with religion.

For further reading

Papers, newspapers and the longer public life.

The Library of Congress has digitised a large collection of Douglass’s papers and newspapers. The National Park Service looks after Cedar Hill, his house in Washington, and gives a clear account of his life. The Smithsonian’s collections include some of the many photographs he sat for: he was among the most photographed Americans of the nineteenth century, and lectured on what photographs could do.
