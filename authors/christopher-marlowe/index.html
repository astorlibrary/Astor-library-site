Biography

Marlowe’s life.

Marlowe was the son of a Canterbury shoemaker and went to the King’s School before a scholarship took him to Corpus Christi College, Cambridge. His schooling shows in everything he wrote. Grammar school and university gave him Latin rhetoric, classical history and the habit of argument, and he translated Ovid’s Amores and the first book of Lucan’s Pharsalia into English. When his characters summon ancient gods, dispute theology or imagine empires, they are drawing on that learning.

Too much has been built on the Cambridge record. Marlowe took his BA in 1584. When the university hesitated over his MA three years later, the Privy Council wrote that he had done Her Majesty good service “in matters touching the benefit of his country”. The letter shows that he had powerful protectors and had done some government service; it does not say what. Later writers have built a whole career as a secret agent on it, but the evidence goes no further than his absences from college, a rumour that he meant to go to the Catholic seminary at Rheims, and the Council’s support.

After Cambridge, Marlowe wrote for the London playhouses, and his career there lasted only about six years. Tamburlaine, the rise of a Scythian shepherd to conqueror of half the world, made his name, carried by Edward Alleyn’s performance and the drive of unrhymed verse. The Jew of Malta, Edward II, The Massacre at Paris, Dido, Queen of Carthage and Doctor Faustus range across different histories and genres, and each follows someone who wants more than an ordinary life allows. He did not always write alone: the title page of Dido names Thomas Nashe as his collaborator.

Marlowe died at Deptford on 30 May 1593. The coroner’s inquest found that Ingram Frizer stabbed him in self-defence during a quarrel over the bill, but the two other men in the room, Robert Poley and Nicholas Skeres, had worked for the government, and theories have followed ever since. Most of the plays reached print only after he died. Doctor Faustus survives in two different early texts, printed in 1604 and 1616, and Hero and Leander, left unfinished, was completed by George Chapman. Much of what we read as Marlowe has passed through the hands of actors, printers and editors.

How the writing works

Reading Marlowe closely.

01

Appetite

Marlowe’s blank verse makes a speaker sound grand, and it drives him on. Clauses pile up, place names widen the map and the verbs reach for possession. Read a speech aloud and mark where the breath has to run on past the line ending: the momentum can make an impossible wish sound practical for a moment. Then look for the places where repetition becomes compulsion, or a pause shows what the speaker has left out.

02

Argument

Faustus is learned, and he reads badly. He quotes half a verse of scripture (“The reward of sin is death”) and stops before the promise that follows it, dismisses each discipline by limits he has set himself, and treats Mephistopheles’s account of hell as a bargaining position. Marlowe gives dangerous ideas intelligence and force, so the reader has to check each claim against what the scene shows, and remember that eloquence is not authority.

03

Scale

The plays move between world-changing ambition and the limits of a stage. Faustus imagines command of the elements and of nations, and spends much of his twenty-four years on conjuring tricks for an emperor, a pope and a duke. Marlowe means the shrinking: the comedy shows how little the power amounts to, and in the last scene the clock brings twenty-four years down to a single hour.

A route through the play

Reading Doctor Faustus.

Begin with the opening survey of learning. Make two columns: what each discipline actually offers and what Faustus demands from it. He judges medicine, law and theology by whether they can give him absolute security or command. The habit lasts the whole play: he has plenty of information, and he treats it as permission for the choice he has already made.

Next, follow every warning. Mephistopheles describes the loss of heaven from inside it. Faustus’s blood congeals as he signs the contract, and “Homo, fuge” appears on his arm. The Good and Evil Angels argue over him, and an Old Man later begs him to repent. Mark each time Faustus considers repentance, and what distracts him straight afterwards: he keeps himself busy so that he does not have to think.

Keep a separate record of what the twenty-four years produce. The early speeches promise travel round the world, secret knowledge and political command; the later scenes are tricks, conjured shows and service to great men. Productions treat the comedy very differently. Rushed through, the middle of the play can look like a string of episodes; played for its diminishing returns, it shows a life growing smaller while Faustus goes on speaking the language of greatness.

Finally, compare the early texts and do not silently combine them. The 1604 A-text is shorter and moves fast. The 1616 B-text adds material, including more spectacle and comic action, and reflects stage revisions made after Marlowe’s death. Ask what each version makes the play value: inward argument, public display, theological terror or entertainment. In the last soliloquy, listen again: the clock, the broken syntax and the changing requests make time dramatic before any devil enters.
