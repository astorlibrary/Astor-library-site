Biography

Dickens’s life.

Charles Dickens was born in Portsmouth in 1812, the son of a clerk in the Navy Pay Office, and grew up in Chatham and London. In February 1824, just after his twelfth birthday, he was sent to work at Warren’s Blacking Warehouse at Hungerford Stairs, covering and labelling pots of boot-blacking. That same month his father was arrested for debt and taken to the Marshalsea prison. The work lasted about a year. Dickens hardly spoke of it afterwards, and the story became public only in John Forster’s biography, published after his death. Frightened children, unstable homes, debt, prisons and offices where a person can vanish inside a system run through all his fiction.

He left school at fifteen to work as a solicitor’s clerk, taught himself shorthand and became a parliamentary reporter. His sketches of London life were collected as Sketches by Boz in 1836, and The Pickwick Papers, published in monthly parts in 1836–37, made him famous. He married Catherine Hogarth in 1836; they had ten children and separated in 1858. He edited the weekly magazines Household Words and All the Year Round, walked the London streets for miles at night, and in his last twelve years gave public readings from his books in Britain and America, playing every voice himself. He died at Gad’s Hill Place in Kent in 1870 and was buried in Westminster Abbey.

How the writing works

How Dickens writes.

01

Serial publication

Most of Dickens’s novels first came out in monthly or weekly parts, and he usually wrote only a little ahead of the printer. Each part had to satisfy readers and leave them wanting the next: characters return, secrets are planted early and an instalment often ends on a turn. Great Expectations ran weekly in All the Year Round from December 1860 to August 1861, and the weekly beat is still there in a single volume. The Christmas books were different: each came out complete, in time for the holiday.

02

Voice

Dickens seldom makes a general statement about society. He gives a system a room, a smell, a catchphrase and a person who speaks for it: Scrooge asking “Are there no prisons?”, Pumblechook taking the credit for Pip’s fortune. The names are jokes, and the jokes make what lies under them sharper.

03

Change

His plots keep asking whether people can change the stories they tell about themselves. Scrooge is shown his own life from outside. Pip finds out how badly he has mistaken gentility for worth. John Peerybingle, convinced that his wife is deceiving him, has to decide what kind of man he will be.

A route across the shelf

Reading Dickens’s prose.

Try reading the first page of each book aloud. Dickens wrote for the ear, and later read his work to paying audiences. Notice who is telling the story, where a repeated word turns comic and when an object (Marley’s chain, the convict’s file, the cricket) starts to carry more than its literal weight.

Then watch where private feeling meets public life. Pip’s shame looks personal until it turns out to be about class. Scrooge’s loneliness looks private until the story sets it beside Bob Cratchit’s fifteen shillings a week, the gentlemen collecting for charity and the Poor Law. Dickens is at his sharpest when a room and a system share a sentence.
