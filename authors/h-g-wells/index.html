Biography

Wells’s life.

Herbert George Wells was born in Bromley, Kent, in 1866. His father kept a struggling china shop and played cricket for Kent; his mother went back into service as housekeeper at Uppark, a country house in Sussex, where the boy read his way through the library. Apprenticed to a draper at fourteen, he hated it, got himself taken on as a pupil-teacher, and in 1884 won a scholarship to the Normal School of Science in South Kensington, where he studied biology under T. H. Huxley.

Evolution, anatomy and the long scale of geological time stayed with him as ways of cutting humanity down to size. He taught science, wrote a biology textbook and journalism, and in 1895 published The Time Machine. The Island of Doctor Moreau (1896), The Invisible Man (1897) and The War of the Worlds (1898) followed. He began The War of the Worlds while living in Woking, and told a friend that he was wrecking the town and killing his neighbours in painful and eccentric ways. He knew enough science to make an invention persuasive, but his stories are about how people behave once the old explanation of the world has stopped working.

Wells wrote far beyond what we now call science fiction. Kipps (1905) and The History of Mr Polly (1910) are comic novels about shop assistants like the young man he had been; Ann Veronica (1909) caused a scandal; A Modern Utopia (1905) and The Outline of History (1920) laid out plans for a world state. He quarrelled with the Fabians, with Henry James and with a great many others, and died in London in 1946.

How the writing works

Reading Wells closely.

01

The familiar

The first Martian cylinder falls on Horsell Common, near Woking, and people walk out to look. A journalist gathers news; soldiers arrive expecting their organisation and their guns to work. Roads, commons, houses, railway lines and newspaper offices make the impossible measurable: Wells lets you recognise the route before he destroys it.

02

The balance of power

The Martians outmatch Britain as completely as the British outmatched the peoples they colonised, and the narrator says so, setting the invasion beside the destruction of the Tasmanians by European settlers. Once the balance changes, command, property, empire and expertise have to show what they are worth.

03

Delayed knowledge

The narrator watches carefully and still misunderstands what he sees. Explanations arrive in pieces, after the terror. The delay keeps the book from turning into a lecture, and shows that knowing the scientific words is not the same as being in control.

A route through the novel

Reading The War of the Worlds.

At Horsell Common, separate seeing from knowing. The crowd watches the cylinder, the newspapers report it as news, and the authorities assume that whatever has arrived can be contained. The Heat-Ray destroys those assumptions before anyone has a useful name for the weapon.

As the invasion spreads, follow the systems: news, roads, railways, the army. News travels unevenly; roads and railways jam; military organisation meets a force from another technological age. Wells shows a modern society connected enough to spread panic, but not informed enough to act together.

In the ruined house with the curate, and at the artilleryman’s half-dug tunnel on Putney Hill, listen to the competing explanations. The curate’s religion, the artilleryman’s plans for a race living in the drains and the narrator’s own observation each promise some order, and each can become a hiding place from what is happening. In the end the Martians are killed by bacteria, and no human plan has anything to do with it.

For further reading

Books, papers and the Woking landscape.

The University of Illinois holds the main collection of Wells’s papers, including the manuscript of The War of the Worlds, and its online exhibition shows the changes he made for the first book edition. Ohio University has a short guide to the range of his writing, and Wells in Woking follows the invasion through the roads and commons where it begins.
