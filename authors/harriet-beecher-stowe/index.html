Biography

Stowe’s life.

Harriet Elisabeth Beecher was born in Litchfield, Connecticut, in 1811, one of the many children of Lyman Beecher, a famous Congregational minister. Religion, education and public argument were the family’s business. Her sister Catharine founded the Hartford Female Seminary, where Harriet studied and then taught, and her brother Henry Ward Beecher became the best-known preacher in America. In 1832 the family moved to Cincinnati, where her father ran Lane Theological Seminary. Ohio was a free state; across the river, Kentucky was a slave state, and abolition, race and religious duty were argued over at the dinner table.

In 1836 she married Calvin Ellis Stowe, a biblical scholar who taught at Lane. They had seven children, money was short, and she wrote sketches, stories and magazine pieces to help keep the household going. In 1849 their eighteen-month-old son, Samuel Charles, died in Cincinnati’s cholera epidemic. Stowe wrote later that at his dying bed and at his grave she learned what an enslaved mother feels when her child is torn away from her.

In 1850 the family moved to Brunswick, Maine, where Calvin had a post at Bowdoin College. That year the Fugitive Slave Act required people in the free states to help return those who had escaped. Stowe’s answer was Uncle Tom’s Cabin, which began in the Washington antislavery weekly National Era on 5 June 1851. She expected it to run for a few weeks; it ran for ten months, until April 1852. The book came out in March 1852, before the serial had finished, and sold some three hundred thousand copies in the United States in its first year.

When defenders of slavery called the book a lie, Stowe answered with A Key to Uncle Tom’s Cabin (1853), setting out the laws, court cases and testimony she had drawn on. Meanwhile stage versions she had not authorised, the ‘Tom shows’, played the story in blackface with crude comedy and turned Tom’s name into an insult that fits the Tom of the novel badly. The novel has its own paternalism and racial assumptions as well. Stowe went on writing for another forty years, including Dred (1856) and novels of New England life such as The Minister’s Wooing (1859) and Oldtown Folks (1869), and died in Hartford in 1896.

How the writing works

Reading Stowe closely.

01

Two journeys

Eliza flees north with Harry while Tom is carried south. One journey ends in legal safety in Canada; the other passes through harsher and harsher owners. Stowe cuts between them so that the escape never lets you forget the sale. Track the rivers, roads, boats and borders, and ask who can cross openly, who has to hide, and whose movement is controlled by papers, traders or force.

02

Feeling and politics

Stowe keeps showing marriage, parenthood, grief and home where slave law gives them no protection, and her narrator turns to speak to the reader directly, to mothers above all. Rather than skipping the sentimental scenes, look at how they work on you: whose feelings are described, what response is asked for, and whether sympathy leads anywhere. In her concluding remarks she tells readers that the one thing every individual can do is to “see to it that they feel right”.

03

Christian language

Characters quote scripture for opposite purposes. Some use it to defend obedience or excuse themselves; others find in it grounds for resistance and judgement. Tom’s refusal to betray Cassy and Emmeline, knowing what Legree will do to him, is a choice he makes. Compare each character’s religious claim with what it lets them do, and ask what belief requires when the law protects injustice.

A route through the novel

Reading Uncle Tom’s Cabin.

Begin with the Shelby household and note everything that seems to make slavery humane there. Mr Shelby thinks of himself as a decent man, Mrs Shelby opposes the sale, and Tom has earned everyone’s trust. None of it stops Mr Shelby’s debts from turning Tom and Harry into assets to be sold. Follow the words for price, credit, papers and ownership: from the first chapter Stowe shows that a kind owner cannot cancel the law and the market the household rests on.

Read Eliza’s flight and Tom’s journey side by side, even when many chapters separate them. Eliza crosses the Ohio on the ice with Harry in her arms; Tom goes down the Mississippi on a steamboat as part of a trader’s gang. Notice the people who help Eliza and George Harris, Senator Bird and the Quakers of the Halliday settlement among them, and set them beside the traders and agents moving enslaved people the other way.

Keep a record of what characters say about Christianity and what they then do. Senator Bird has just voted for a law forbidding people to help fugitives when Eliza comes to his door, and he drives her to safety himself that night. St Clare sees exactly what is wrong with slavery and keeps putting off freeing Tom; he dies before the papers are signed, and his widow Marie sells Tom. Ask whether belief changes anything about property, risk and conduct, and what happens when good intentions depend on one owner staying alive.

Finally, keep the novel apart from what was made of it later. Read what Tom does before bringing in the insult his name became: he protects others, refuses Legree’s order to flog a fellow slave, and keeps his moral authority with no legal or physical power at all. Then look at how Stowe writes Black speech, beauty, education and independence, and where racial hierarchy survives inside her abolitionism. Read for both: what the book made people see in 1852, and what it passed on.
