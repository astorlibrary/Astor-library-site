Biography

Hawthorne’s life.

Nathaniel Hawthorne was born in Salem, Massachusetts, on 4 July 1804. His father, a sea captain, died of yellow fever in Surinam in 1808. His great-great-grandfather John Hathorne had been one of the magistrates at the Salem witch trials of 1692, and Hawthorne, who added the ‘w’ to the family name as a young man, kept going back to Puritan New England as a place where private fear could take on the authority of law. He did not reconstruct that past as a historian would. He used it to explore guilt, secrecy, inheritance and the stories later generations tell in order to live with what came before them.

At Bowdoin College in Maine he made friends who mattered all his life, among them the future president Franklin Pierce and Horatio Bridge; Henry Wadsworth Longfellow was in his class. After graduating in 1825 he spent twelve years in Salem, reading and writing in his mother’s house. He published his first novel, Fanshawe, anonymously and at his own expense in 1828, and later tried to destroy the copies. The stories collected in Twice-Told Tales (1837) brought him a reputation but not a living. In 1841 he spent some months at Brook Farm, an experimental community, and left unconvinced. The next year he married the painter Sophia Peabody and moved into the Old Manse in Concord, with Emerson and Thoreau for neighbours.

Political jobs paid where fiction did not. Hawthorne, a Democrat, became Surveyor of the Port of Salem in 1846 and was dismissed in 1849 when the Whigs came to power. He got his own back in “The Custom-House”, the long introduction to The Scarlet Letter, which made fun of his former colleagues and gave offence in Salem. In it a narrator very like Hawthorne claims to have found a faded scarlet letter and an old manuscript among the papers of an earlier surveyor.

The Scarlet Letter (1850) made him famous. The House of the Seven Gables (1851), about a Salem family living under an old curse, and The Blithedale Romance (1852), drawn from Brook Farm, followed. He wrote a campaign biography for Pierce, who made him United States consul in Liverpool (1853–57). After four years in England and more than a year in Italy, which gave him The Marble Faun (1860), he came home to Concord. He died in 1864 at Plymouth, New Hampshire, on a journey with Pierce.

How the writing works

Reading Hawthorne closely.

01

Symbols

The scarlet A is imposed as if its meaning were fixed, and the novel keeps reporting new meanings for it, made by rumour, memory and Hester’s own conduct. It is a punishment, an ornament (she embroiders it in gold thread) and an accusation, and years later many people say it stands for ‘Able’. Don’t settle on one meaning. Note who reads it, what they know, and who has the power to make their reading stick.

02

Romance

Hawthorne called the book a romance, not a novel. In “The Custom-House” he describes moonlight in a familiar room, “where the Actual and the Imaginary may meet”, as the light he needs. A romance can let coincidence, emblems and psychological possibility matter without turning into fantasy. The narrator often offers several explanations for an event and declines to choose, as with the mark some spectators claim to have seen on Dimmesdale’s breast. Ask what each explanation would make true, and why Hawthorne leaves the question open.

03

Public scenes, private knowledge

The scaffold scenes come three times: Hester alone with her baby before the town; Dimmesdale there at midnight with Hester and Pearl, seen only by Chillingworth; and Dimmesdale’s confession in daylight seven years after the first. Each time the arrangement changes, and you can measure what confession, concealment and time have done. Notice who can see a scene, who understands it and who controls how it is told afterwards.

A route through the romance

Reading The Scarlet Letter.

Read “The Custom-House” as part of the book. Its narrator works among official records and complains that the job has dulled his imagination; then he claims to find the scarlet cloth and the papers of Surveyor Pue, who held his post the century before. Mark the details that imitate historical proof and the moments that admit to invention. By the time the story begins, you know the past is reaching you through someone who is making it.

In the first three chapters, separate what the town knows from how sure it is. The crowd knows Hester’s sentence and can see the child, but not who Pearl’s father is or anything of Hester’s marriage, and the women by the scaffold pass judgement anyway. Keep the difference in mind as Dimmesdale’s reputation grows and Chillingworth’s private suspicion turns into an investigation. The book is interested in secrets, and even more in the wish to own another person’s inner life.

Track Hester’s work and movements through Boston. Her needlework appears at ceremonies she is shut out of, from the Governor’s ruff to the shrouds of the dead, though never on a bride’s veil; her cottage at the edge of the settlement confines her and also leaves her free to think. When the town starts to read the A differently, ask whether Hester has been accepted, made useful or just given another role. Her thoughts about women and society go well beyond the town’s new praise.

Treat Pearl as a character before you treat her as an allegory. Note her questions, her fascination with the letter, and her refusal to go along with what the adults want kept apart: in the forest she will not cross the brook to her mother until Hester fastens the A back on. Then compare the daylight scaffold scenes with the forest meeting and the midnight vigil, and see how light and place change what can be admitted. At the end one tombstone serves for two graves, with a single letter on it; Hawthorne leaves you to decide what it says about them.
