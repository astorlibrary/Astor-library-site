Webster.

John Webster (c.1580–c.1634), a London coachmaker’s son, wrote The Duchess of Malfi, first staged about 1613 and printed in 1623.

Biography

Webster’s life.

Much of Webster’s life is blank. He was probably born in London around 1580, the son of a coach and cart maker who belonged to the Merchant Taylors’ Company, and he may be the John Webster admitted to the Middle Temple in 1598. The plays’ fondness for trials and legal procedure would fit, but the identification is not certain. A licence records his marriage to Sara Peniall in 1606. After that, title pages and theatre records show a working career and almost nothing of a private life.

Because so little is known, Webster is often turned into a character out of his own tragedies, solitary, morbid and interested only in blood. His early work tells a different story. He wrote with other dramatists and for several companies: the additions to John Marston’s The Malcontent (1604), and Westward Ho and Northward Ho (1604–05), quick city comedies written with Thomas Dekker. Later plays were shared with Thomas Middleton, John Ford and Philip Massinger. Plays in his theatre were often joint work, made to deadlines for particular companies and actors.

The White Devil was performed by Queen Anne’s Men at the Red Bull, an open-air playhouse in Clerkenwell, and printed in 1612. Webster complained in print that it had been acted in a dull winter season, in an open theatre, to an audience that did not understand it. The play turns a sensational Italian murder story into a trial run by compromised judges. Vittoria answers her accusers with wit and nerve, against a verdict decided before she speaks.

The Duchess of Malfi was written for the King’s Men, Shakespeare’s company, and performed at the indoor Blackfriars and at the Globe. The 1623 quarto says so on its title page and lists the actors who played the parts, one of the first printed plays to do so. The story is true: Giovanna d’Aragona, the widowed Duchess of Amalfi, secretly married her steward Antonio Bologna around 1510. Webster found it in William Painter’s The Palace of Pleasure, which translated it from Bandello by way of Belleforest. His Duchess has more authority than the sources allow her, and her brothers, who treat her as family property, set a spy on her.

Webster went on writing: The Devil’s Law-Case, a tragicomedy full of lawsuits, was printed in 1623, and other plays survive with disputed or shared attribution. Nobody knows when he died; a poem by Thomas Heywood published in 1635 refers to him in the past tense. What survives is the plays, and they show his mind at work: in who knows what, in what he asks of his actors, and in the moment an object on stage changes its meaning.

How the writing works

Reading Webster closely.

01

Objects

An apricot, a horoscope, a key, a letter or a wax figure is never just a prop. Someone reads it, often under pressure and often for an employer: Bosola offers the Duchess apricots to see whether she is pregnant, and picks up the horoscope Antonio has cast for their son. What an object means depends on who notices it, what they already suspect and who will pay for the reading. On the page, mark every hand that evidence passes through; in performance, watch how long an object stays in view before anyone says what it means.

02

Service

Webster’s courts run on employment. Antonio serves the Duchess as her steward; Bosola serves her brothers as spy and hired hand; officers, a doctor and executioners carry out orders on real bodies. Bosola describes corruption with great accuracy and goes on taking the money. Follow the words for wages, reward, debt and usefulness: they show conscience tangled up with the need to live on someone else’s favour.

03

Tone

The tragedies move through wit, grotesque comedy, ceremony, stillness and sudden violence, and the jolts are deliberate: they keep judgement unsteady. The apricot scene can get laughs while a woman’s body is being inspected without her consent; Ferdinand’s horrors are astonishing as theatre and calculated cruelty at the same time. Keep both in view, the pleasure of the performance and the harm being done.

A route through the play

Reading knowledge and service in The Duchess of Malfi.

On a first reading, keep track of who knows about the marriage. It begins as a private ceremony with Cariola as the only witness; Ferdinand and the Cardinal give orders without knowing the fact they fear; Bosola is paid to turn household gossip into intelligence. Each new person who knows changes the danger. Keeping the record stops the plot from turning into a parade of horrors, and shows secrecy both protecting and exposing the couple.

Go back to the proposal scene and watch rank at work in it. The Duchess outranks Antonio, so she has to do the wooing herself: “The misery of us that are born great! We are forc’d to woo, because none dare woo us.” Antonio’s place as her steward brings love and inequality into the same room. Their secrecy protects them from the brothers and also cuts the family off from any public support. Ask what the scene lets them choose, and what the court has made impossible to choose openly.

Then follow Bosola through his verbs: he watches, interprets, reports, imprisons, stages and kills. Nobody doubts his intelligence, and his discontent with service is real, but the plot keeps asking what insight is worth if he never says no. When he finally turns on the brothers, his revenge brings back none of the people he helped destroy.

Read the imprisonment in Act IV as theatre staged inside the theatre. Darkness, a dead man’s hand, the wax figures behind a curtain, the dance of madmen and Bosola disguised as a tomb-maker are all put on for the Duchess as well as for us. A production has to decide how much the audience sees, when Ferdinand is visible, and whether his show looks controlled or already out of hand. Those choices shift the balance between psychological torture and hints of the supernatural; they do not change who arranged the violence.

At the execution the Duchess is calm and Cariola fights, and Webster does not make either the right way to die: Cariola is as human, and as wronged, as her mistress. The last act is a muddle of revenge. Bosola kills Antonio by mistake, the Cardinal’s real cries for help are ignored because he has told his courtiers to ignore them, and the men who ran the system die by it.
