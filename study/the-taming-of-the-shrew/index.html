William Shakespeare

The Taming of the Shrew

A lord persuades Christopher Sly that he is someone else; suitors in Padua change clothes and identities; Petruchio tries to change Katherina. The comedy keeps asking what performance can make other people believe. Its final wager and speech do not settle that question. They make the audience decide what it has watched: submission, strategy, partnership, coercion, or a troubling mixture of them.

Shakespeare study

The Taming of the Shrew, William Shakespeare

This Astor Study Edition follows the Induction and the Padua action scene by scene. Summaries keep the Katherina–Petruchio plot beside Bianca’s network of disguised suitors, rather than allowing the second plot to disappear behind the title.

Notes clarify wordplay, social terms and the practical business of dowry, household and stage disguise. Focused sections consider the relationship between the 1623 Folio text and the anonymous A Shrew, the missing end of Christopher Sly’s frame, taming and falconry language, food, clothing and the public staging of marriage.

The final speech is approached as drama, not a quotation with one fixed tone. The study material asks what Katherina says, where she says it, who has wagered on her conduct, how the other women respond and what an actor can make visible without changing a word.

The whole play

A frame, two courtships and a wager

Disguise is not confined to the comic subplot. Social identity, obedience and authority are repeatedly presented as roles that require an audience.

Induction · Sly

A man is talked into another identity

A lord finds Christopher Sly asleep and has servants treat him as a nobleman. Clothing, surroundings and repeated address begin to alter what Sly accepts as true. Players arrive to perform the Padua comedy for him.

Act I · Bargain

Marriage begins as a household problem

Baptista refuses Bianca’s suitors until Katherina is married. Lucentio exchanges roles with Tranio to approach Bianca as a tutor; Petruchio announces that he has come to Padua to marry wealthily.

Acts II–III · Courtship

Words and contracts move faster than consent

Petruchio turns Katherina’s resistance into evidence that they have agreed in private. Baptista settles financial terms. The wedding becomes a public display when Petruchio arrives late, dressed absurdly, and removes Katherina before the feast.

Act IV · Taming

Home becomes a controlled stage

Petruchio withholds food and sleep while claiming that each deprivation expresses care. He rejects clothes made for Katherina and demands that she agree with his naming of sun, moon and time before they can return to Padua.

Act V · Wager

Private marriages are tested in public

False identities are exposed and three newly married men bet on their wives’ obedience. Katherina comes when called and delivers the final speech. Petruchio wins the wager, but the words, looks and silences that close a performance decide what that victory means.

Read closely

Three places where performance becomes power

In each scene, watch who controls the names, conditions and audience of the encounter.

Induction

“Am I a lord, and have I such a lady?”

Sly’s new identity is made collectively. Costumes and a grand bed help, but the servants’ agreement is decisive: every person around him repeats the same story. His uncertainty shows that social rank can look theatrical without becoming unreal in its effects.

Because the Folio does not return to Sly at the end, the frame remains open. A production must choose whether he vanishes, continues watching or is restored from the related A Shrew. Each choice changes how the taming plot is judged.

2.1

A contest over names and meanings

Petruchio insists on calling Katherina “Kate” and answers resistance by translating it into its opposite. Anger becomes gentleness; rejection becomes secret agreement. The scene is brilliantly quick, but its speed is also a method of control.

An actor playing Katherina may find pleasure in the verbal contest, growing alarm, strategic calculation or several responses at once. Close reading should preserve what she actually says before deciding what the exchange becomes in performance.

5.2

The final speech is spoken inside a bet

Katherina’s account of wifely duty is often removed from its immediate setting. On stage, it follows a wager made by husbands who turn obedience into a public competition. Bianca and the Widow are present as listeners and counterexamples.

Evidence includes tone, pace and action as well as statement. Does Katherina address the women, the men, Petruchio or the wider audience? Does Petruchio interrupt, accept, recoil or collude? The text fixes the words; performance reveals how many relations those words can hold.

Text, household, stage

The difficulty belongs to the play’s history too

Context should sharpen the evidence on the page, not excuse the harm represented there or pretend every early modern audience agreed.

Folio / quarto

The Shrew and A Shrew are not the same text

The anonymous A Shrew was printed in 1594 and has related plots but different names, wording and structure. Its precise relationship to Shakespeare’s play remains disputed. Modern editions normally base The Taming of the Shrew on the 1623 Folio and discuss A Shrew as a textual problem.

Household

Marriage joins intimacy to property

Baptista negotiates dowries and consent in public, while Petruchio repeatedly speaks of household government. Early modern law and custom gave husbands extensive authority, but women’s lives varied by status, wealth, family and local practice. The play stages a struggle within those structures rather than a simple guide to them.

Metaphor

The language of falconry is a declared method

Petruchio compares Katherina to a hawk and plans to keep her hungry and wakeful until she is trained. Food, sleep and clothing make the metaphor material. A performance can use comic timing, but the text keeps the body affected by that comedy in view.

Ways to study

Three routes that keep the argument exact

Each begins with something observable before moving towards interpretation.

Language route

Follow acts of renaming

Mark every moment when one person assigns another a name, role or intention: Sly as lord, Lucentio as Cambio, Tranio as master, Katherina as “Kate”. Ask when the new identity is shared play, tactical disguise or something imposed.

Stage route

Direct the wager without changing the words

Place Katherina, Bianca, the Widow and Petruchio on a simple plan. Decide eye contact, distance and reaction during the final speech. Then explain which interpretation those choices produce and which details still resist it.

Pattern route

Track food, sleep and clothes

These objects move the play from courtship talk into bodily control. Record who provides or withholds each one, what reason is given and whether the same act looks different to servants, spouses and spectators.

For discussion and essays

Questions with no responsible shortcut

A strong answer distinguishes textual evidence, historical context and a production’s choices.

01

What does the unfinished Induction make us doubt?

Consider social rank, spectatorship and the possibility that the Padua action is another lord’s entertainment.

02

Is Bianca’s courtship an alternative to Katherina’s?

Test the apparent romance against disguise, paternal bargaining, false witnesses and the final wager.

03

Where does comedy depend upon coercion?

Choose one scene and analyse how verbal wit, physical action and audience laughter work together or pull apart.

04

Who controls the meaning of Katherina’s final speech?

Separate what the words assert from what a performer can reveal through address, gesture and response.
