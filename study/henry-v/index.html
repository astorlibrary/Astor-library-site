Shakespeare study

Henry V, William Shakespeare

This Astor Study Edition follows the complete play scene by scene, with summaries that keep the military campaign, the Eastcheap characters and the French court connected. Line numbering and explanatory notes support close quotation without breaking the movement of the drama.

The supporting sections separate the history behind the play from the history Shakespeare makes on stage. They place the 1600 quarto beside the fuller 1623 Folio, trace the work of the Chorus, and examine Henry’s changes of register: legal argument, battlefield exhortation, threat, private prayer and courtship.

Character, theme and performance material leads into passage work, critical debate and essay planning. It does not supply one verdict on Henry. Instead, it gives the reader enough evidence to decide how far the play celebrates victory, exposes the cost of conquest, or makes both readings necessary.

Plot and structure

Five acts, from the legal claim to the French settlement.

The summary follows the campaign and identifies how Henry’s decisions affect the French population, English soldiers and former Eastcheap characters.

Act I · Claim

A war is made to sound lawful

Canterbury turns genealogy into a case for the French crown while a proposed tax on Church wealth sits behind the discussion. The Dauphin’s tennis balls let Henry present invasion as an answer to personal insult as well as dynastic right.

Act II · Severance

The new king cuts away former loyalties

Henry exposes Cambridge, Scroop and Grey by making them argue for the severity later used against them. Falstaff dies offstage, Bardolph, Nym and Pistol depart for France, and the world of Prince Hal is carried into the king’s campaign.

Act III · Siege

Fellowship gives way to terror

The breach speech gathers exhausted soldiers into a heroic company. Henry’s address to Harfleur then threatens rape, infanticide and destruction. Elsewhere Katherine begins learning the language of the nation that will claim victory over hers.

Act IV · Trial

The king walks among those who must fight

Disguised before Agincourt, Henry hears Williams and Bates question royal responsibility. Dawn brings the Saint Crispin’s Day speech, an improbable victory and the troubling order to kill the French prisoners.

Act V · Settlement

Marriage closes the campaign, not the history

The Treaty of Troyes and Henry’s courtship of Katherine promise union. The Epilogue immediately narrows the triumph: Henry will die young, their son will lose France, and England will return to civil conflict.

Critical reading

Analysis of three major passages.

Each section compares Henry’s public account of the war with the experiences of individual characters.

Prologue

“Piece out our imperfections with your thoughts.”

The Chorus admits that the theatre cannot hold two kingdoms, armies or years of history. That apparent weakness becomes a demand placed upon the audience. Spectators must supply horses, ships and battlefields with their own “imaginary forces”.

The request is political as well as theatrical. The audience is asked to help make the heroic image it is watching. Compare the Chorus’s polished account of Henry with the scenes of self-interested bishops, profit-seeking soldiers and civilian fear that follow.

3.1 and 3.3

Two voices at Harfleur

“Once more unto the breach” turns a tired army into sons of England and comrades in a single action. The pronouns gather speaker and soldiers together. When the town still resists, Henry changes the relation: responsibility for the threatened violence is transferred to the governor who will not surrender.

Reading the speeches together prevents either from becoming a detachable quotation. The power to inspire and the power to terrorise belong to the same command of rhetoric.

4.1

A king in borrowed clothes

Disguise removes the obedience automatically attached to Henry’s title. Williams can ask whether soldiers who die in a bad cause are charged to the king who brought them there. Henry answers by distinguishing a ruler’s cause from each man’s private sins.

His reasoning does not end the scene. Left alone, he describes the isolating burden of ceremony and prays over the guilt attached to his father’s crown. The passage tests public certainty against private unease.

Text, theatre, history

The play is not a transcript of Agincourt.

Chronicles, early printings and performance all shape the Henry an audience is invited to see.

Chronicle

History already arranged as a story

Shakespeare drew particularly on Raphael Holinshed and Edward Hall, as well as the earlier play The Famous Victories of Henry the Fifth. He selected, compressed and reordered material. A useful essay treats those choices as part of the play’s argument, not as errors measured against a modern history book.

Quarto / Folio

Two substantially different early texts

The 1600 quarto is far shorter and lacks the Choruses, the night-time debate and other important material. The fuller Folio text appeared in 1623. Comparing what is absent from the quarto reveals how strongly common soldiers and theatrical self-consciousness complicate the campaign.

Performance

The stage decides how triumph sounds

The Chorus may be sincere, propagandist, anxious or divided among several speakers. Mud, visible casualties and the treatment of the prisoners can darken Agincourt; music and direct address can make national fellowship immediate. Performance does not decorate the argument. It produces it.

Study topics

Three topics for detailed analysis.

Each topic can support a lesson, set of notes or essay plan.

Language route

Track who is included in “we”.

Mark Henry’s pronouns in the council chamber, at Harfleur, before Agincourt and with Katherine. Note when he speaks as one man, king, army or nation. Then ask who is required to accept each version of the group and who remains outside it.

Performance route

Put the Chorus beside the scene.

Read each Chorus immediately before the action it introduces. List what is promised, what can actually be staged and what the following scene complicates. Try speaking a Chorus as celebration, salesmanship and doubt; record which words change weight.

Argument route

Build the case from consequences.

Do not begin with “Henry is a hero” or “Henry is a villain”. Begin with one decision: Scroop, Bardolph, Harfleur, the prisoners or the French marriage. Follow how the decision is justified, enacted and remembered.

Discussion and essays

Questions for analysis.

Answers should compare evidence from several scenes and account for relevant performance choices.

01

Does the Chorus explain the play or compete with it?

Compare its descriptions of Henry and war with the voices heard in the scenes that follow.

02

What kind of responsibility can a king refuse?

Begin with the soldiers’ debate in 4.1, then test Henry’s answer against Harfleur and the prisoners.

03

When does eloquence become coercion?

Follow the language used to the army, the Governor of Harfleur, Williams and Katherine.

04

Why does the Epilogue undo the ending?

Ask what happens to the play’s account of providence and national unity when Henry’s gains are immediately declared temporary.
