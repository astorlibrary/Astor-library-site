Novella study

Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde, Robert Louis Stevenson

This Astor Study Edition presents the complete novella with chapter summaries that respect its order of disclosure. The book is built as a case file, and the summaries keep track of what each witness knows, what each document seals away and when the reader is finally allowed to catch up. Explanatory notes clarify legal and medical vocabulary, the money and manners of professional London, and the words witnesses reach for when description fails them.

The supporting sections treat duality as a claim the novella examines. Jekyll’s claim that “man is not truly one, but truly two” is set against the evidence of the earlier chapters, and against his own admission that two marks only the limit of his knowledge. Work on secrecy and setting follows the same division through London itself: one building with a polished front door and a blistered back one, respectable squares within walking distance of Soho.

The material on science and narrative method puts the experiment in its decade without making the book a medical case. Stevenson keeps the chemistry vague and the moral accounting precise, and the study sections ask why. Key passages, from Enfield’s story of the trampled child to the final statement, lead into close reading, discussion and essay planning.

The central question is not whether Jekyll and Hyde are one man; the book answers that. It is why a respectable doctor wanted a second self, what the want cost everyone around him, and how far his last document should be trusted to explain it.

Plot and structure

Ten chapters, from the door in the by-street to Jekyll’s confession.

The summary keeps the novella’s order of knowledge: what each stage shows, who reports it and what stays sealed.

Chapters 1–2 · Testimony

A door, a story and a will

On a Sunday walk, Enfield shows Utterson a door and tells him how a small man “trampled calmly over the child’s body and left her screaming on the ground”, then bought his way out of the crowd. Utterson goes home to a will he has long distrusted, which leaves everything to Edward Hyde in the event of Jekyll’s death or “disappearance or unexplained absence”. Resolving that “I shall be Mr. Seek”, he haunts the by-street until he meets Hyde, and the sight confirms his fears without explaining them.

Chapters 3–5 · Reassurance and murder

Jekyll promises control; Hyde kills

At dinner Jekyll laughs the subject away and extracts a promise that the will’s terms will be honoured. Nearly a year later a maid watches from her window as Hyde, “with ape-like fury”, clubs Sir Danvers Carew to death in the street. The police find half of the broken cane; Hyde vanishes from his Soho rooms. Jekyll produces a letter in which Hyde takes his leave, but Utterson’s clerk notices that Hyde’s hand and Jekyll’s are “in many points identical: only differently sloped”.

Chapters 6–7 · Withdrawal

One friend dies of knowledge; another shuts the window

With Hyde gone, Jekyll briefly returns to society, then locks himself away without explanation. Lanyon, wrecked by something he refuses to discuss, dies within weeks, leaving Utterson a sealed narrative not to be opened before Jekyll’s death or disappearance. The lawyer’s professional discretion holds; the packet goes into his safe. Passing the court with Enfield, Utterson exchanges a few words with Jekyll at his window, until the doctor’s smile gives way to “abject terror and despair” and the sash is thrust down.

Chapter 8 · The last night

The door comes down

Poole fetches Utterson through a wild night because the voice behind the cabinet door has not sounded like his master’s for a week, and whatever is inside cries out “for God’s sake, have mercy”. The axe goes through the red baize door. On the floor lies Hyde’s body in clothes far too large for him, a crushed phial in his hand, and on the table a new will, a note dated that day and a packet addressed to Utterson: the case’s last transfer of paper.

Chapters 9–10 · Explanation

Lanyon’s narrative and Jekyll’s statement

Lanyon’s narrative records what killed him: at his own hearth he watched a drinking figure change until, “like a man restored from death—there stood Henry Jekyll!” Jekyll’s full statement then supplies theory, motive, pleasure and relapse, from the first triumphant transformation to the morning he woke with “the hand of Edward Hyde”. It closes as its writer must: “I bring the life of that unhappy Henry Jekyll to an end.” Explanation arrives only when nobody is left to question it.

Critical reading

Analysis of three major passages.

The passages sit at the beginning, near the end and at the very end of the case, and each turns on the difference between witnessing and explaining.

Chapter 1 · Enfield’s story

“like some damned Juggernaut”

Hyde’s first appearance is second-hand, told across a walk between two men who pride themselves on not gossiping. The horror is carried by an adverb: the man “trampled calmly over the child’s body and left her screaming on the ground”. Enfield concedes that “It sounds nothing to hear, but it was hellish to see”, and when he reaches for a comparison the human category fails him: “It wasn’t like a man; it was like some damned Juggernaut.”

What happens next matters as much as the violence. Hyde is not handed to the police; he is priced. Cornered by a circle of “hateful faces”, he says “Name your figure”, observes that “No gentleman but wishes to avoid a scene”, and settles for a hundred pounds. The first lesson of the case is that this world prefers to hush things up, and Jekyll’s whole experiment depends on it.

Chapter 9 · Lanyon’s narrative

“there stood Henry Jekyll!”

The only eyewitness account of the transformation is withheld until the ninth of ten chapters, and it comes from the book’s confident materialist. The sentence itself makes the reader wait through symptoms before it will surrender the name: “pale and shaken, and half fainting, and groping before him with his hands, like a man restored from death—there stood Henry Jekyll!” The dashes delay recognition exactly as the novella has delayed it, and the exclamation mark records a category breaking.

What kills Lanyon is knowledge. “My life is shaken to its roots; sleep has left me”, he writes, and the insistence of “I saw what I saw, I heard what I heard, and my soul sickened at it” shows a scientific witness reduced to repeating his testimony because explaining it has become unbearable. The professional codes that run the novella (restraint, documents, discretion) are followed to the letter here, and they do not protect him.

Chapter 10 · Jekyll’s statement

“man is not truly one”

Jekyll announces his discovery as everyone’s condition: “man is not truly one, but truly two”. Within two sentences the arithmetic loosens. Two marks only “the state of my own knowledge”, and he guesses that the self will prove “a mere polity of multifarious, incongruous and independent denizens”. The neat pair the adaptations remember is already, in Jekyll’s own vocabulary, a crowd.

The theory is also a defence. If ‘both sides of me were in dead earnest’, then Hyde can be presented as a separate agent, and not as a freedom Jekyll went looking for, a plunge, as he puts it elsewhere, into ‘the sea of liberty’. The annotated page follows the wording phrase by phrase as moral confession becomes scientific speculation and political metaphor, and shows what each shift lets Jekyll avoid saying.

Text, city, science

A short book with a precise history.

Publication, composition and adaptation all bear on how the case reads now.

1886

Publication in January 1886

The novella appeared in January 1886, published in Britain by Longmans, Green and Co., with an American edition the same month. The first London edition ran to just 141 pages, and its title page reads Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde, without an opening The. The missing article is worth an essay sentence: it presents the story as a case, legal, medical, documentary, before the reader has met a single character, and the book delivers on that by arriving as testimony, wills, letters and sealed statements.

Composition

Bournemouth, a dream and a double life

Stevenson wrote the novella in 1885 while living at Skerryvore in Bournemouth, and family accounts connect the story with a dream of transformation. Edinburgh memory stands behind it: William Brodie, the respectable cabinet-maker and civic figure who led a criminal life by night, had already given Stevenson and W. E. Henley the play Deacon Brodie, or The Double Life. Brodie offers a context for double identity and respectability, though he was not the model for Jekyll, and the distinction is a useful discipline in context paragraphs.

Adaptation

The stage and screen reverse the method

Sullivan’s authorised adaptation opened at the Boston Museum in May 1887 with Richard Mansfield playing both men, and reached the London Lyceum in August 1888. John Barrymore filmed the dual role in 1920; Fredric March won an Academy Award for it in Rouben Mamoulian’s 1931 film. Nearly every version shows the transformation early and often, the one thing the book withholds until Lanyon’s narrative. Comparison with an adaptation is therefore most revealing when it starts from the order of knowledge more than from fidelity to plot.

Study topics

Three topics for detailed analysis.

Each topic starts from textual evidence and develops into notes, discussion or an essay plan.

Document route

Follow the paper.

List every document in the case: the will with its provision for “disappearance or unexplained absence”, the letter signed Edward Hyde, Lanyon’s sealed narrative with a sealed enclosure inside it, the papers left on the cabinet table, Jekyll’s statement. For each, record who wrote it, who may read it and when. The suspense is administrative: the truth exists on paper long before anyone is permitted to read it. Finish by asking what it means that explanation arrives only in writing, after everyone who could be cross-examined is dead.

City route

Draw the map the book implies.

Mark the by-street with its “blistered and distained” door, the square where Jekyll’s polished front door opens, Hyde’s rooms in Soho under the fog’s “great chocolate-coloured pall”, Lanyon in Cavendish Square, “that citadel of medicine”. Then note what Utterson only gradually understands: the worst door and the best door belong to one building. The architecture states the thesis before Jekyll does, and an essay on setting can be built entirely from entrances and the routes between them.

Description route

Collect the failures to describe Hyde.

Witnesses agree on disgust and fail at detail. Enfield admits “He is not easy to describe” and settles for “something displeasing, something down-right detestable”; Utterson tries “Something troglodytic, shall we say?” and falls back on “God bless me, the man seems hardly human”. Gather these attempts and ask what they actually describe: a deformity in Hyde’s body, or a gap in the observers’ categories. The question keeps analysis of the novella’s language precise where paraphrase would blur it.

Discussion and essays

Questions for analysis.

Answers should quote exactly and account for the chapter in which each piece of evidence is allowed to arrive.

01

Why does the case belong to Utterson?

Consider what a cautious lawyer makes possible as an observer: discretion, documents, a horror of scandal. Look at how the same habits move the investigation forward and also hold back the truth.

02

What does the novella never show?

No witness can describe Hyde; Jekyll’s pleasures are never named; the transformation is withheld until Lanyon’s narrative. Argue what the withholdings achieve that description could not.

03

Is the final statement a confession or a defence?

Track Jekyll’s pronouns: when Hyde’s acts are “he” and when they are “I”. Set “This, too, was myself” against the passages that treat Hyde as another person entirely.

04

Who is responsible for the death of Sir Danvers Carew?

Weigh Jekyll’s image of the caged animal (“My devil had been long caged, he came out roaring”) against the choices that preceded the murder, including the deliberate return to the draught.

05

What work does respectability do in the book?

Begin with the hundred pounds settled at the door in Chapter 1 and follow the bargains, silences and unasked questions from there. Decide whether the world of gentlemen is Hyde’s opposite or his cover.

06

Where does the science actually sit?

The chemistry is left vague while the moral accounting is exact. Compare Jekyll’s experiment with Lanyon’s materialism and ask why Stevenson gives the mechanism so little and the consequences so much.
