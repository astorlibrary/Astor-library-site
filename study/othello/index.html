Shakespeare study

Othello, William Shakespeare

This Astor Study Edition presents the complete play with scene summaries that keep motive, opportunity and consequence connected as Iago improvises. Line numbering supports accurate reference; explanatory notes clarify Venetian offices and military rank, legal and nautical language, and the early modern vocabulary of Moor, blackness and service that the play’s first audiences heard.

Longer sections place the drama between its source and its early texts. Cinthio’s prose tale supplies the Moor, the Ensign, the wife and the handkerchief; the edition shows what Shakespeare adds, beginning with the public trial of the marriage in Venice. The 1622 Quarto is set beside the 1623 Folio, whose additional lines include the Willow Song, so that readers can see how textual choice affects Desdemona’s voice as well as Othello’s rhetoric.

The study material treats evidence as the play’s central instrument. It follows how Iago converts courtesy into signs and a dropped handkerchief into “proof”, and why the demand for certainty makes Othello easier to deceive, not harder. Race is read historically and theatrically: Othello is indispensable to Venice yet exposed to a suspicion Iago does not have to invent, only to use.

Character work leads into close reading, discussion and essay planning. The guiding question is not whether Othello is jealous. It is how a confident public interpreter is taught, in a single afternoon, to read his marriage through another man’s story.

Plot and structure

Five acts, from the Venetian Senate to the bedchamber.

The summary follows how each act narrows the play’s world and hands more of its interpretation to Iago.

Act I · Venice

A marriage is put on public trial

Iago, passed over for promotion and declaring “I am not what I am”, wakes Brabantio with racialised images of theft. Before the Senate, the charge of witchcraft meets Othello’s account of the courtship, and Desdemona confirms her “divided duty”. Venice needs its general for Cyprus; Iago ends the act promising a “monstrous birth”.

Act II · Cyprus

The storm removes the war and seals the island

The Turkish fleet is wrecked before any battle, leaving a garrison with nothing to fight but itself. During the night’s celebration Iago works Cassio drunk into a brawl and out of his lieutenancy. Cassio’s cry, “Reputation, reputation, reputation!”, gives Iago his remedy: send him to plead through Desdemona.

Act III · Persuasion

One long scene manufactures certainty

In 3.3 Iago moves from hesitation to warning (“O, beware, my lord, of jealousy”), planting what he claims to prevent. Desdemona drops the handkerchief; Emilia passes it to her husband; “Trifles light as air” become confirmations. Othello bids farewell to his occupation, demands “the ocular proof”, and kneels with Iago in a vow of revenge.

Act IV · Spectacle

Othello watches the scene Iago directs

Hidden, Othello watches Cassio laugh about Bianca and hears Desdemona in every gesture; the handkerchief itself arrives on cue. He collapses, then strikes his wife before Venice’s envoy and exits on “Goats and monkeys!”. The act closes in the willow scene, where Desdemona remembers Barbary’s song and Emilia argues that wives fall as husbands teach them.

Act V · Testimony

The murder is performed as justice, then exposed

After the botched night attack on Cassio, Othello enters the bedchamber reasoning “It is the cause” and smothers Desdemona, who dies claiming “Nobody; I myself”. Emilia’s testimony breaks Iago’s story, and he kills her for it; he will explain nothing: ‘Demand me nothing’. Othello asks Venice to report him truly, and executes himself.

Critical reading

Analysis of three major passages.

Each passage shows a different mind at work on the same problem: how words are made to stand for facts.

1.3 · Othello

“Rude am I in my speech”

Accused of witchcraft, Othello promises the Senate “a round unvarnish’d tale” and opens with a disclaimer (“Rude am I in my speech”) that his own controlled verse immediately disproves. The story he tells is of storytelling: Desdemona heard his life recounted, and “She lov’d me for the dangers I had pass’d, / And I lov’d her that she did pity them.” The couplet’s symmetry offers narrative itself as the only witchcraft used.

The scene acquits him because the Senate accepts a narrative where Brabantio offered an accusation, and because Desdemona speaks for her own choice. Yet the verdict rests on trust in a teller. Othello leaves staking “My life upon her faith” and entrusting his wife to “Honest Iago”: the play’s cruelty is that the man saved by a story will be destroyed by one.

3.3 · Iago

“Trifles light as air”

Holding the handkerchief, Iago states his method with the clarity of a craftsman: “Trifles light as air / Are to the jealous confirmations strong / As proofs of holy writ.” The object proves nothing by itself. Suspicion, planted first, will supply the strength; the trifle only needs to appear in the right hands at the right moment.

The speech then changes register from evidence to infection. “The Moor already changes with my poison”: dangerous ideas act on the blood and “Burn like the mines of sulphur”, and “Not poppy, nor mandragora” will ever “medicine thee to that sweet sleep” again. Iago claims his work is chemical, a poison past argument and past cure.

5.2 · Othello

“It is the cause”

Othello enters repeating “It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul” without ever naming the cause. The missing noun does the work: judicial language circles an offence it will not name, as though naming it might invite examination. ‘Put out the light, and then put out the light’ sets candle against life, and in wanting to preserve her ‘whiter skin’ and ‘monumental alabaster’ he makes Desdemona a monument before she is dead.

The soliloquy acts out justice, not rage (she must die, ‘else she’ll betray more men’), inventing a public duty for a private killing. Set it beside the final speech, where Othello, calling himself “An honourable murderer”, asks Venice to “Speak of me as I am”, of “one that loved not wisely, but too well”. Readers must decide how far that last self-account is honest reckoning, and how far one more managed story.

Language across the play

Six patterns of language.

A pattern matters when its meaning shifts. Note the speaker, the situation and the sense each time; a list of similar words is only the start.

Honest

Othello leaves Venice trusting “Honest Iago” with his wife, and the epithet recurs to the end. Iago himself declares that “Men should be what they seem”, a principle spoken by the one man in the play who is not. Track who uses the word, of whom, and how long the audience can hear it without wincing.

Proof

Othello believes certainty will protect him: “Give me the ocular proof”. But every proof he receives (reported words, a handkerchief in another man’s hand, overheard laughter) is an object plus Iago’s interpretation. The play keeps evidence and the account of evidence apart; Othello’s tragedy is that he stops doing so.

Poison and medicine

Iago promises in the first scene to “poison his delight”, and in 3.3 observes that “The Moor already changes with my poison”. Jealousy is described as an infection, something no argument reaches and no drug, ‘Not poppy, nor mandragora’, can cure. Ask what the metaphor removes from Othello’s responsibility, and what it leaves.

Monsters

Iago ends Act I vowing that “Hell and night / Must bring this monstrous birth to the world’s light”, then warns Othello against “the green-ey’d monster” he is busy breeding. Othello’s own “Chaos is come again” makes love the only order holding monstrosity back. Follow when the monster stops being a figure of speech.

Black and white

The play’s colour language begins in Iago’s and Roderigo’s street insults and is later heard inside Othello’s own reasoning: “Haply, for I am black” appears among his explanations of Desdemona’s supposed betrayal. By 5.2, whiteness (skin like snow, “monumental alabaster”) has become a fatal aesthetic. Trace how prejudice moves from other voices into his.

The handkerchief

The object’s history changes with its use: in 3.4 “Did an Egyptian to my mother give” makes it a charm, while in 5.2 it is “an antique token / My father gave my mother”. Follow it from Desdemona to Emilia, Iago, Cassio and Bianca, and note that its meaning is assigned by whoever last tells its story.

Text, source and setting

Two early texts and an Italian source.

The section covers the 1622 Quarto, the 1623 First Folio and the Cinthio tale behind the plot.

1622 Quarto

First printed nearly twenty years after the court record

The quarto, printed by Nicholas Okes for Thomas Walkley, appeared in 1622 as The Tragoedy of Othello, The Moore of Venice. Its title page records performance at the Globe and Blackfriars by the King’s Men, and its stage directions are often fuller than the Folio’s. It is the play’s first printing, long after the Whitehall performance of 1 November 1604.

1623 Folio

A fuller text with the oaths removed

The First Folio, one year later, prints about 160 lines not found in the quarto, including the Willow Song and Othello’s Pontic Sea speech, while omitting many of the quarto’s oaths, a difference often linked to the regulation of stage oaths after 1606. Modern editions combine readings from both texts, and the choices affect Desdemona’s voice, Othello’s rhetoric and staging detail.

Cinthio

Cinthio’s tale and the play

Giraldi Cinthio’s Hecatommithi supplies the Moor, the Ensign, the wife, the captain and the handkerchief plot; only Desdemona receives a name from the source. Shakespeare adds the public defence of the marriage in Venice, Iago’s command of audience and victim, and the Cyprus setting under Ottoman pressure, where the removal of the external war leaves the play’s violence entirely internal.

Study topics

Three topics for detailed analysis.

Each topic starts from textual evidence and builds towards a lesson, a set of notes or an essay plan.

Evidence route

List everything Othello accepts as proof.

Work through 3.3 to 4.1 recording each item (a warning, a refusal to speak, the handkerchief, the watched conversation) and, beside it, who supplied the interpretation. None of the evidence shows what Othello concludes from it. The list makes visible how “ocular proof” is satisfied by staged sight.

Language route

Follow the verse to its breaking point.

Set the Senate speech beside the farewell catalogue of 3.3 (“Farewell the tranquil mind! Farewell content!”), the broken prose around “Goats and monkeys!” in 4.1, and the recovered formality of 5.2. Then ask what the final style performs, and for whom Othello is speaking when he asks to be reported truly.

Performance route

Decide what the audience shares with Iago.

His confidences give spectators knowledge no one on stage has. Track where a production lets him amuse the house and where it makes complicity uncomfortable. Then consider casting: the play’s stage history runs from blackface conventions to productions such as the RSC’s in 2015, where a Black actor’s Iago changed what the theatre asked audiences to watch.

Discussion and essays

Questions for analysis.

Answers should compare at least two scenes and account for what has changed in language, knowledge or staging between them.

01

What actually convinces Othello?

Order the moments of 3.3 and identify where inference replaces sight. Decide whether any single piece of evidence would work on its own.

02

Why does Iago give so many motives?

He names the lost promotion, suspicion of his own wife and simple hatred. Test each against what he does, and consider what it means that none fits.

03

Does the play give Desdemona a voice or take one away?

Set her Senate defence beside the willow scene and her dying answer, “Nobody; I myself”. Ask who speaks for her at each point.

04

How does Venice use and suspect its general?

Compare the Senate’s need for Othello with Brabantio’s accusation, and follow “Haply, for I am black” to where the state’s suspicion becomes his own.

05

What does Emilia’s knowledge cost?

She handles the handkerchief, argues in 4.3 that husbands teach wives their faults, and dies for telling the truth. Weigh what she knew, and when.

06

Who controls the story at the end?

Put Iago’s “Demand me nothing” beside Othello’s “Speak of me as I am”. Decide whose refusal or request the play finally honours.
