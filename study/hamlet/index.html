Shakespeare study

Hamlet, William Shakespeare

This Astor Study Edition presents the complete play with scene summaries that keep the revenge plot, the court’s spying and the family grief connected. Line numbering supports precise reference; explanatory notes unpack compressed syntax, courtly and legal idiom, the vocabulary of theatre and the religious language that surrounds death, prayer and burial throughout the action.

The supporting sections set the play beside its materials: the older Amleth story in Saxo Grammaticus and Belleforest, the conventions of Elizabethan revenge tragedy, and the three early printed texts whose differences no editor can fully reconcile. Delay is treated as several different kinds of waiting, for proof, for the right moment, for a conscience to settle, and not as one flaw to be diagnosed.

The study material reads the soliloquies as a sequence instead of isolated set pieces, follows what the play allows Ophelia and Gertrude to choose and what it only reports about them, and treats the Mousetrap as the moment when theatre itself becomes Hamlet’s instrument of proof. Mortality is tracked from the Ghost’s return through “the undiscover’d country” to the skull that can be held in the hand.

Character, theme and performance work leads into passage analysis, discussion and essay planning. The central question is not simply why Hamlet delays. It is what would count as proof at a court where everyone is acting, and what acting on it costs everyone within reach.

Plot and structure

Five acts, from the Ghost’s appearance to Fortinbras’s arrival.

The summary identifies who is watching each scene, who arranged the watching, and what each apparent proof changes.

Act I · Command

A dead king speaks; a living king performs

Sentries on the cold battlements see the dead king’s Ghost before Hamlet enters, so Denmark is disturbed before its prince is. Claudius manages mourning, marriage and state business in one fluent speech, while Hamlet answers in asides—“A little more than kin, and less than kind”—and in private wishes to dissolve. The Ghost names Claudius as his murderer and demands revenge; Hamlet swears, decides “to put an antic disposition on”, and already feels the task as a burden: “The time is out of joint”.

Act II · Watching

A court where everyone is watched

Polonius sends Reynaldo to spy on his own son in Paris, then offers Ophelia’s account of Hamlet’s disordered visit as evidence of love-madness. Claudius sets Rosencrantz and Guildenstern on their old friend. Hamlet meets questioning with performance—“Words, words, words”—until the players arrive and an actor’s tears for Hecuba shame him into a plan: “The play’s the thing” wherein he will “catch the conscience of the King”.

Act III · Proof

A play catches a king; a sword misses one

Hamlet weighs being and not being while Claudius and Polonius listen from concealment, then turns on the baited Ophelia: “Get thee to a nunnery”. His staged murder—“The Mousetrap”—breaks Claudius’s composure, but a reaction is not a confession. Finding the king apparently at prayer, Hamlet delays for the sake of damnation; in Gertrude’s closet he kills the listener behind the arras and finds he has murdered Polonius, not the king.

Act IV · Removal

Grief is converted into political weapons

Claudius ships Hamlet to England with a sealed commission for his death. Ophelia returns mad, distributing flowers and songs—“we know what we are, but know not what we may be”—and Laertes storms the palace demanding vengeance for Polonius. Claudius redirects that fury into a rigged fencing match with a poisoned foil and a poisoned cup. Gertrude reports Ophelia’s drowning in the speech beginning “There is a willow grows aslant a brook”.

Act V · Graves

The graveyard, and the duel

Gravediggers joke over skulls until Hamlet holds a known one: “Alas, poor Yorick”. Ophelia’s funeral procession interrupts the meditation. Back from the sea, Hamlet accepts the match—“There’s a special providence in the fall of a sparrow”; “The readiness is all”. The poisoned instruments expose Claudius only after Gertrude and Hamlet are struck. Hamlet kills the king, gives his dying voice to Fortinbras and ends: “The rest is silence”. Horatio’s farewell—“Good night, sweet prince”—shares the stage with an arriving army.

Critical reading

Analysis of three major passages.

The three passages form a sequence: what death is allowed to be changes from a wish, to a question, to a thing held in the hand.

1.2 · Hamlet

“O that this too too solid flesh would melt”

Before Hamlet knows anything of murder, he wishes his body would “resolve itself into a dew”, and the wish is blocked only by divine law against “self-slaughter”. The world is “an unweeded garden”; the disgust precedes the evidence. What actually fills the speech is the marriage: the speed of it—“A little month”—and the revulsion of “incestuous sheets”. Grief for his father keeps spilling over into judgement of his mother.

“Frailty, thy name is woman” converts one woman’s remarriage into a verdict on the sex, and a reader should notice what the generalisation conceals: Gertrude’s actual motives are never heard. The last line matters most. “But break, my heart, for I must hold my tongue” imposes silence before the Ghost has imposed any task. The tragedy begins with feeling that has no permitted outlet, in a court where saying what one thinks is already dangerous.

3.1 · Hamlet

“To be, or not to be”

The most famous speech in the play never says “I”. “To be, or not to be, that is the question” opens in infinitives, and the pronouns that follow are “we” and “us”, as if a private crisis could be handled as a philosophical proposition. Sleep first promises an ending; one word inside the comparison—dream—reopens everything, and death becomes “the undiscover’d country” from which no traveller returns, in a play commanded by a returned traveller.

“Thus conscience does make cowards of us all” cuts two ways, because the word means both moral scruple and consciousness itself: thinking, awareness. Either sense accuses Hamlet, and he chooses neither aloud. The staging sharpens the irony. Ophelia has been placed in his path as bait, with Claudius and Polonius listening from concealment, so the most inward speech in the play has an audience it may or may not know about.

5.1 · Hamlet

“Alas, poor Yorick”

The graveyard meditation is in prose, not verse, and its subject is no longer an undiscovered country. Death is a skull passed up by a joking sexton, and it belonged to someone Hamlet loved: “a fellow of infinite jest” who carried the child prince on his back. Memory and decay occupy the same object—“My gorge rises at it”—and the questions turn physical: “Where be your gibes now?” There is nowhere for the answer to come from.

“To what base uses we may return” extends the thought to emperors: “Imperious Caesar, dead and turn’d to clay” might stop a hole in a wall. Horatio calls the tracing too curious; Hamlet insists on following matter all the way down. Then the general lesson becomes a particular grief. Ophelia’s funeral enters the same graveyard, and the man who has just handled anonymous skulls must watch a known body lowered into the ground—“Sweets to the sweet”—and hear how little ceremony her death has been allowed.

Text, sources and stage

Three early texts and no single Hamlet.

The section covers the early printed texts, the story of Amleth behind the plot, and the performance history in which each generation remakes the part.

Three texts

Q1, Q2 and the Folio disagree

The 1603 First Quarto is much shorter than the other witnesses and orders some material differently; its title page advertises performance in London and at the two universities. The 1604/5 Second Quarto is the longest early text, and the 1623 First Folio includes some material absent from Q2 while omitting some Q2 passages. So editors work with three early texts and no single authority, and every staged Hamlet is already a cut, arranged or edited version of a play too long for most performances.

Sources

Amleth before Hamlet

The older Amleth story appears in Saxo Grammaticus and later in Belleforest’s Histoires tragiques, and it already contains a murdered king, a dangerous uncle and a son protected by feigned madness. Shakespeare inherits the machinery of Elizabethan revenge tragedy—ghost, command, delay, play-within-a-play—and turns it inward. Revenge becomes a question of proof, conscience and salvation, which is why the Ghost’s ‘Remember me’ starts an investigation instead of ending one.

Performance

Each age casts its own prince

Thomas Betterton made the part his own after the Restoration; in the nineteenth century it became the test every star actor had to pass, and Sarah Bernhardt’s Hamlet of 1899 showed that the part had never belonged only to men. On screen, Laurence Olivier’s 1948 film, Kenneth Branagh’s complete-text version of 1996 and Michael Almereyda’s corporate New York Hamlet of 2000 each decide what the play is about by deciding what to cut, keep and watch. A study of any production can begin from that single question.

Study topics

Three topics for detailed analysis.

Each topic starts from textual evidence and builds towards discussion or an essay plan.

Soliloquy route

Read the soliloquies as one argument.

Take the speeches in order, from “O that this too too solid flesh would melt” through “To be, or not to be” to the graveyard prose. For each, record what death is allowed to be—wish, question, object—and which pronouns Hamlet uses. The route shows a mind revising its own terms, not repeating one complaint.

Surveillance route

Who watches whom.

List every scene of concealed listening: Reynaldo’s errand, the nunnery scene, the Mousetrap, the closet. Note who set each watch, who knows about it and who dies of it. Then ask which of Hamlet’s own acts—the antic disposition, the staged murder—are surveillance by other means.

Proof route

Test the evidence against a standard.

Set out what Hamlet actually obtains: a ghost’s testimony, a startled king at a play, a sealed commission read at sea. For each, ask what standard it satisfies—legal, theatrical, theological—and what it would justify. The delay stops looking like weakness and becomes an argument about certainty.

Discussion and essays

Questions for analysis.

Answers should compare at least two scenes and account for what a production can choose to show.

01

What would count as proof?

Weigh the Ghost’s word, Claudius’s reaction to the Mousetrap and the intercepted commission. Decide which finally licenses revenge, and whether the play endorses that standard.

02

Where does the performance of madness end?

Follow the “antic disposition” from its announcement through the nunnery scene, the closet and the graveyard struggle. Identify a moment where the performance is clearly controlled, and one where control is arguable.

03

What are Ophelia and Gertrude allowed to choose?

Collect the instructions each woman receives and the scenes where she is watched, used or reported. Set Ophelia’s obedience and madness beside Gertrude’s silences, and ask what the play lets either say for herself.

04

Is the revenge the Ghost asked for ever performed?

Compare the command of Act I with the killing of Act V, done in the open, after poison has already struck. Use Laertes and Fortinbras as parallel avengers with different methods and outcomes.

05

Who owns the ending?

Hamlet leaves his story to Horatio and his dying voice to Fortinbras. Decide whether the final authority on the stage of bodies is the friend’s account, the soldier’s order or the silence Hamlet names.

06

What changes between the third act and the fifth?

Put “the undiscover’d country” beside Yorick’s skull and “The readiness is all”. Ask whether Hamlet finds an answer, abandons the question or learns to act without one.
