Shakespeare study

Romeo and Juliet, William Shakespeare

This Astor Study Edition presents the complete play with scene summaries that keep the four-day timeline visible: how quickly a feast becomes a marriage, a duel becomes a banishment and a rescue plan becomes a tomb. Line numbering supports accurate quotation; explanatory notes clarify Petrarchan convention, bawdy wordplay, duelling terms, household ritual and the religious imagery the lovers make their own.

The supporting sections place the play in its early print history, comparing the short 1597 quarto with the fuller 1599 text behind most modern editions, and trace the story back through Arthur Brooke’s 1562 poem to its Italian tellings. They show what Shakespeare changed: months compressed into days, and Mercutio, Tybalt, the Nurse and Paris built up until the world around the lovers presses on every choice.

Character analysis treats the feud as something particular people keep alive. Tybalt makes an inherited quarrel a matter of his own honour; Mercutio dies in a fight that is not his; the Nurse and Friar Lawrence offer the lovers adult help that fails them at different speeds. Juliet’s movement from obedient daughter to a woman deciding alone in the dark is followed scene by scene, not asserted.

The critical material and essay questions centre on one problem the play leaves open. The Prologue calls the lovers star-cross’d before they have met, yet every disaster arrives through a decision, a temper or a mischance that could have gone otherwise. The edition gives readers the evidence to argue about fate, haste and blame for themselves.

Plot and structure

Five acts, from a street brawl to a shared tomb.

The summary keeps the timeline in view and identifies who applies pressure to the lovers at each stage.

Act I · Meeting

A feast held inside a feud

A servants’ quarrel becomes a street battle, and the Prince rules that the next disturbance will be paid for with life. Capulet entertains Paris’s suit for Juliet and holds a feast; Romeo attends it still sighing over Rosaline. Meeting Juliet, he builds a sonnet with her and kisses her twice before either learns the other’s name. Juliet’s discovery is exact: “My only love sprung from my only hate”.

Act II · Marriage

A night of vows and a secret wedding

From her window Juliet asks why a name should matter, ‘Deny thy father and refuse thy name’, and once Romeo answers she moves from his borrowed poetry to arrangements: a messenger, a time, a marriage. Friar Lawrence agrees, hoping the match will turn the households’ rancour to love, but sends the pair to the altar with a warning: “These violent delights have violent ends”. The Nurse carries the messages, and the lovers are married in secret.

Act III · Turn

Two deaths change the play’s weather

Tybalt comes looking for Romeo; Romeo, now secretly his kinsman, will not fight him and cannot say why. Mercutio fights instead and is fatally wounded under Romeo’s peacemaking arm, cursing both houses as he dies. Romeo kills Tybalt and cries “O, I am fortune’s fool!”. The Prince banishes him. After one wedding night the lovers part at dawn, and Capulet, misreading Juliet’s grief, fixes her marriage to Paris within days.

Act IV · Plan

A borrowed death to escape a wedding

Friar Lawrence gives Juliet a potion that will counterfeit death for forty-two hours, meaning to recall Romeo by letter so that he is waiting in the vault when she wakes. Alone, Juliet rehearses everything that could go wrong—poison, waking early, madness among the bones—then drinks to Romeo. The Capulet house finds her cold on her wedding morning, and the prepared feast turns to a funeral.

Act V · Tomb

The message fails and the houses pay

Quarantine against plague stops the Friar’s letter; Balthasar’s news of Juliet’s death reaches Mantua instead. Romeo answers—“Then I defy you, stars!”—buys poison from a starving apothecary and reaches the tomb minutes too early, killing Paris and himself before Juliet wakes to her “happy dagger”. Over the bodies the Prince forces the fathers to see their children as “Poor sacrifices of our enmity”.

Critical reading

Analysis of three major passages.

Each passage sets the play’s inherited forms—sonnet, love rhetoric, curse—against the particular people who speak them.

Prologue · Chorus

“A pair of star-cross’d lovers”

The play opens with a sonnet that gives the ending away. Before anyone falls in love, the Chorus announces the “death-mark’d love”, the deaths themselves and the peace they will buy. The wording spreads responsibility deliberately: the lovers are star-cross’d, but the wound is civic—“civil blood makes civil hands unclean”—and it is the parents’ rage, not the stars, that only “their children’s end” can remove.

The choice of form is the first interpretation of the story. A sonnet was the era’s machinery of courtship; here it is used to deliver an obituary, promising “two hours’ traffic” whose outcome is fixed. Knowing the end changes what an audience watches for. Suspense about whether gives way to attention to how, and every joke, delay and accident in the first two acts is shadowed by the sentence already passed.

2.2 · Romeo

“Juliet is the sun”

Beneath the window Romeo does not compare Juliet to the sun; he identifies her with it—“It is the east, and Juliet is the sun!”—and rebuilds the sky around her, demoting the moon to an envious rival. The rhetoric of light, worship and distance is inherited equipment, the same kit he used on Rosaline, and Juliet hears none of it. He is composing both halves of a conversation, as the sonnet tradition taught young men to do.

What makes the scene new is that this mistress talks back. Juliet’s first subject is names, ‘What’s in a name?’, and once she knows Romeo is listening her concerns are concrete: his safety, his honesty, and what time tomorrow to send the messenger. The scene places extravagant metaphor and practical arrangement side by side and lets each test the other.

3.1 · Mercutio

“A plague o’ both your houses”

Mercutio dies joking. His wound is “not so deep as a well, nor so wide as a church door”, and tomorrow will find him “a grave man”. The wit matters because of what it cannot cover: he repeats the curse on both houses three times, and he belongs to neither. The feud’s first death in the play falls on its most detached observer, hurt under the arm Romeo raised to make peace.

Romeo’s response deserves equal weight. Within a hundred lines he has turned down a duel, watched his friend die, killed Tybalt and cried ‘O, I am fortune’s fool!’, blaming fortune for an act he has just chosen. The scene is the play’s hinge: comedy largely leaves with Mercutio, the Prince’s law takes hold, and every later event follows from the banishment pronounced here.

Text, sources and stage

An old story, two quartos and a design problem.

The section covers the early printings, the tale Shakespeare inherited and the decisions performance keeps having to make.

Two quartos

The 1597 and 1599 texts differ substantially

The first quarto of 1597, printed by John Danter, is much shorter than the text most readers know and has long been classed among the so-called bad quartos, a label modern accounts treat with caution. The 1599 quarto, printed by Thomas Creede for Cuthbert Burby and presented as corrected, augmented and amended, became the basis for the later quartos and the 1623 Folio. The play was not entered in the Stationers’ Register until 1607, after both. Modern editions are built from this layered history, which is why wording can differ between the copies students carry.

Sources

A tale already old when Shakespeare found it

The story of divided lovers, feigned death and double suicide is ancient—Ovid’s Pyramus and Thisbe carries the pattern—and the Romeo material itself passed through Luigi da Porto, Matteo Bandello and Pierre Boaistuau before reaching English in Arthur Brooke’s 1562 poem and William Painter’s 1567 prose version. Shakespeare’s changes are the useful study: Brooke’s months become a few days, and Mercutio, Tybalt, the Nurse and Paris grow from functions into people, so that haste and the surrounding household do the work Brooke gave to time and moralising.

Performance

Every production must decide what Verona is

The feud has been staged as Renaissance ritual, modern street violence, gang conflict and family machinery; the balcony can be any raised place that holds the lovers close and apart. Two later choices shape the meaning most: how much the audience likes Mercutio, which decides how hard 3.1 hits, and whether the fathers’ reconciliation over the bodies feels credible, formal or hollow. The main edition page follows these decisions through the play’s long stage, ballet, opera and screen afterlife.

Study topics

Three topics for detailed analysis.

Each topic can support a lesson, a set of notes or an essay plan.

Form route

Watch the sonnet break.

Begin with the Prologue’s sonnet, then the sonnet the lovers build together at the feast, ending in “You kiss by the book”. Mark where formal rhyme and shared lines appear later, and where they fail to. The play’s move from patterned courtship to broken, urgent speech is measurable on the page, and it maps the lovers’ move from ritual into danger.

Time route

Give every scene a clock.

Note the day and hour of each scene and who sets each deadline: the feast, the dawn partings, Capulet advancing the wedding, the forty-two hours of the potion, the moment Romeo arrives at the tomb. Set the Friar’s “Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast” beside Juliet’s “Gallop apace”, then ask who in Verona actually leaves the lovers time to be slow.

Blame route

Build the case from one intervention.

Do not begin with fate. Begin with one adult decision—the Friar marrying the lovers, the Nurse switching her advice to Paris, Capulet’s ultimatum, the undelivered letter—and follow how it is justified at the time and judged at the end. The Prince closes the play promising that “Some shall be pardon’d, and some punished”; the essay writes the sentences he leaves open.

Discussion and essays

Questions for analysis.

Answers should compare evidence from at least two acts and account for the Prologue’s foreknowledge.

01

Are the lovers destroyed by fate or by timing?

Test the Prologue’s star-cross’d claim against the play’s actual mechanics: a refused duel, a moved wedding, a quarantined letter, a tomb reached minutes too early.

02

Is haste the lovers’ fault or their only option?

Weigh the Friar’s warnings and “too rash, too unadvis’d, too sudden” against the deadlines that adults, not lovers, impose from Act III onwards.

03

Who controls the language of love?

Compare the feast sonnet, Romeo’s speech beneath the window and Juliet’s replies. Track when each lover borrows convention and when each invents.

04

What has the ending actually settled?

The feud ends, gold statues are promised and the Prince names a “glooming peace”. Decide whether the play presents the reconciliation as achievement, indictment or both.
