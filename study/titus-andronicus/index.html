Study editions/Titus Andronicus

William Shakespeare

Titus Andronicus

Titus comes home from ten years of war against the Goths with four surviving sons, two in coffins, and a captive queen. Before the funeral is over he has Tamora's eldest boy hacked apart and burned on the family tomb, and every horror in the next four acts answers that one decision. Tamora marries the new emperor, her sons kill Lavinia's husband and cut out her tongue and hands, Titus loses a hand and two more sons to a trick, and the play ends with a queen eating her children at dinner. It is Shakespeare's first tragedy and his most extreme.

c. 1592Early, and possibly the earliest of the tragedies. Ben Jonson's 1614 joke about the play's admirers implies a first audience around 1589.
24 January 1594Philip Henslowe entered a performance of Titus & ondronicus at the Rose in his account book.
1594John Danter's quarto for Edward White and Thomas Millington, the first printing of any Shakespeare play. One copy survives, in the Folger.
No single narrative sourceOvid's Philomela, Seneca's Thyestes, the Lucrece story and a mass of Roman material, combined by Shakespeare himself.

Where it happens

  • Rome The Capitol, the Pantheon and the Andronici tomb. The play's Rome belongs to no datable reign, which leaves it a city of rites, elections and burials with no year attached.
  • The Rose, Bankside Where Henslowe recorded a performance on 24 January 1594, the earliest firm evidence that the play was being acted.
  • Stationers' Hall, London Where John Danter entered the play on 6 February 1594, two weeks after the Rose performance and just before printing it.
  • Folger Shakespeare Library, Washington Holds the only surviving copy of the 1594 quarto, found in Sweden in 1904 and sold to Henry Clay Folger the year after.
  • Swan Theatre, Stratford-upon-Avon Where Deborah Warner staged the play in 1987, in the production that settled its modern reputation.
