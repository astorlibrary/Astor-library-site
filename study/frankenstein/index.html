Mary Shelley study

Frankenstein, Mary Shelley

This Astor Study Edition presents the complete novel with chapter summaries that keep the nested accounts connected: who is speaking, who is listening, and what each teller wants the listener to conclude. Reading notes and explanatory material clarify the language of galvanism-era science, Genevan law, Alpine and Arctic geography, and the three books from which the creature assembles his sense of the world.

Contextual sections separate the novel from its legend. The summer of 1816, the ghost-story challenge and the scientific arguments about electricity and life are treated as background that Shelley used; what she made of them is a story about ambition, secrecy and the refusal of care. The 1818 and 1831 texts are kept distinct, so that a reader always knows which version a claim describes.

Character analysis treats Victor, the creature, Walton, Elizabeth, Justine and Clerval as parts of a single argument about recognition. The frame narrative is examined as a method: every major account is told to someone, remembered under strain, and passed through the pen of a man with ambitions of his own. Isolation is followed as a condition each narrator partly suffers and partly chooses.

Critical material and essay questions lead from close reading into debate. The central question is not whether the creature is human. It is what creation obliges, and the edition gives the evidence on both sides: a being who reasons and pleads, real violence that no pleading can undo, and a creator whose language always finds a way to be absent from his own decisions.

Plot and structure

Four letters and twenty-four chapters.

The summary follows the 1831 text, whose chapters run continuously; the 1818 first edition divides the same story into three volumes. The novel begins and ends in the polar sea.

Letters I–IV · Frame

An explorer finds an audience for a warning

Robert Walton writes to his sister, Margaret Saville, as he sails towards the pole, asking “What may not be expected in a country of eternal light?” The ice closes on his ship, a giant figure passes on a sledge, and a dying man is taken aboard. Hearing his own hopes echoed, Victor Frankenstein offers his history so that Walton’s ambition “may not be a serpent to sting you, as mine has been”.

Chapters 1–5 · Making

A life is assembled and immediately disowned

Victor recalls a happy Genevan childhood, his mother’s death and his studies at Ingolstadt, where secret work among graves and charnel-houses gives him the power to animate lifeless matter. “It was on a dreary night of November” that he sees his success; within hours he has fled the room, and a long illness stands in for the care he never gives.

Chapters 6–10 · Consequence

The abandoned being reaches Victor’s family first

News comes from Geneva: William, the youngest Frankenstein, has been strangled, and the servant Justine is convicted on planted evidence and executed. Victor, certain of the truth, says nothing. Seeking relief in the mountains above Chamonix, he meets his creation on the ice, and the being he calls devil and “wretch” demands a hearing: “I am thy creature”.

Chapters 11–16 · Testimony

The creature gives his own account of becoming

The creature describes first sensations, fire, hunger and the De Lacey family, from whom he secretly learns language, that “godlike science”, and then history, property and parentage. He reads Paradise Lost “as a true history”. When the family he loves drives him away, and the child he seizes proves a Frankenstein—“Frankenstein! you belong then to my enemy”—benevolence turns to revenge.

Chapters 17–23 · Bargain

A promise is made, broken and paid for

The creature demands a female companion and Victor consents, then destroys the half-finished work in the Orkneys under his creation’s eyes. The answer is a sentence: “I shall be with you on your wedding-night.” Clerval is strangled, and Victor is tried for the murder before marrying Elizabeth—who is killed, as promised, while he patrols elsewhere with pistols meant for his own defence.

Chapter 24 · Pursuit

Walton’s ending

Victor follows the creature north across Russia and the frozen sea until Walton’s ship receives him. Dying, he advises Walton to “Seek happiness in tranquillity and avoid ambition”, then half withdraws the lesson. In the final letters the creature stands over the body, argues his case a last time and is “borne away by the waves and lost in darkness and distance”.

Critical reading

Analysis of three passages.

Each reading follows exact wording from the 1831 text: who acts, who is merely acted upon, and which phrases carry responsibility.

Chapter 5 · Victor

“It was on a dreary night of November”

The famous opening gives weather and season, not an action. Victor collects “instruments of life” around him, yet the moment of success is nearly withheld: what the sentence shows is “the dull yellow eye of the creature open”, an event on the creature’s side of the room. Months of labour, described at length in the previous chapter, pass into success inside a subordinate clause, and the judgement that follows is aesthetic: ‘the beauty of the dream vanished, and breathless horror and disgust filled my heart’. Victor condemns how the being looks, not what he himself has done.

The naming begins immediately. ‘The miserable monster whom I had created’ disowns with its noun and confesses with its relative clause; authorship survives inside the grammar of rejection. Victor then dreams of Elizabeth turning to his mother’s corpse, wakes to find the creature reaching towards him, reads the gesture as menace and runs. Nothing in the chapter is done to him except an eye opening and a hand stretched out. The decisive act of the creation scene is the flight: the first thing this creator makes, after a body, is an orphan.

Chapter 10 · The creature

“I ought to be thy Adam”

On the glacier the creature answers Victor’s insults with facts and grammar. ‘I am thy creature’ replaces ‘devil’ and ‘monster’ with a relation Victor cannot deny, and ‘thy part, the which thou owest me’ makes that relation a debt: obedience is offered on a condition, as one side of a contract the maker has never kept. The old pronouns matter. “Thy” and “thou”, by 1818 the language of prayer and intimacy, address Victor as something between a father and a god being petitioned.

The allusions then refuse a single role. “I ought to be thy Adam” measures the distance between creation and care, since Adam was made and then recognised; “the fallen angel” adds the outcast who repays exclusion with harm. Claiming both, the creature describes an injustice and issues a warning in the same breath. His summary, ‘misery made me a fiend’, is a claim to weigh, not to accept: suffering explains the growth of hatred without excusing everything he does afterwards.

Walton’s final letters · The creature

“Lost in darkness and distance”

The ending returns the whole history to Walton’s pen, and the last judgements arrive already compromised. Victor’s deathbed advice—“Seek happiness in tranquillity and avoid ambition”—is withdrawn almost as it is spoken, since he cannot stop hoping that another might succeed where he failed. The creature, finding the body, is given the novel’s last argument: ‘Am I to be thought the only criminal, when all humankind sinned against me?’ The question is addressed to Walton, but Walton has already heard Victor’s version and helped to correct it.

What follows is self-sentencing without a court. “I, the miserable and the abandoned, am an abortion” takes Victor’s vocabulary of disgust and turns it inward, and the promised funeral pile lies beyond the last page. The novel ends on an unwitnessed future: the creature is “borne away by the waves and lost in darkness and distance”, with no body, no verdict and no reply from Margaret Saville, to whom every word has been addressed. Shelley closes the pursuit and leaves the argument—grief, guilt, eloquence and violence together—in the reader’s keeping.

Text and origins

Two authorial texts and one famous summer.

The novel has two major textual lives, and its beginnings in 1816 are part of its meaning without explaining it.

1818

Anonymous, in three volumes

The first edition was published in London by Lackington, Hughes, Harding, Mavor & Jones, without Mary Shelley’s name on the title page, with a preface by Percy Bysshe Shelley and a dedication to William Godwin, her father. An epigraph from Paradise Lost frames the story through creation, accusation and abandonment before it begins. Readers of the 1818 text often find Victor more plainly responsible for his own choices, with less language of destiny to shelter behind.

1831

Revised for Bentley’s Standard Novels

Shelley revised the novel for the one-volume 1831 edition and added a new introduction explaining the ghost-story competition and the book’s origin. The revisions make fate, providence and moral warning more explicit, and this became the most widely circulated nineteenth-century form of the novel. Theodor von Holst’s frontispiece gave the creature his first major printed image in an authorised book. Quotations on this page follow the 1831 text.

1816

Geneva, ghost stories and Romantic science

Bad weather kept the Villa Diodati circle indoors, and Byron’s challenge to write a ghost story produced both Frankenstein and Polidori’s The Vampyre. The novel belongs to contemporary debates about electricity, chemistry, anatomy and the limits of experiment—Galvani, Aldini, Erasmus Darwin and Humphry Davy stand in its background—but Shelley wrote an ethical narrative about ambition, secrecy and abandonment, not a forecast of any particular machine.

Study topics

Three topics for detailed analysis.

Each topic can support a lesson, a set of notes or an essay plan.

Custody route

Follow the chain of testimony.

The creature speaks to Victor, Victor to Walton, Walton to a sister who never answers. Map each handover: what the listener wants, what the teller withholds, and where accounts are edited—Victor takes Walton’s notes of his own story and “corrected and augmented them”. Then ask whose interests the finished manuscript serves, and whether any voice in the novel reaches us unmediated.

Naming route

Track what the being is called.

List every noun Victor uses—“wretch”, “monster”, “fiend”, “dæmon”—against the names the creature claims for himself: “creature”, Adam, “the fallen angel”. Note who is present at each naming and what each word makes permissible. The novel’s central injustice can be measured in vocabulary before a single act of violence occurs.

Decision route

Put the choice back where Victor pleads destiny.

At the creation, the flight from the apartment, Justine’s trial, the promise on the ice and the destruction in the Orkneys, write down what Victor knows, what he fears, what options remain and what he does. His narration prefers destiny to decision; the route restores the road not taken, which is where responsibility lives.

Discussion and essays

Questions for analysis.

Answers should compare evidence from at least two narrators and quote exact wording.

01

Who owns this story?

Every account passes through Walton’s letters, and Victor corrects the record of his own life. Decide whether the frame protects the creature’s testimony or contaminates it, and what changes if you imagine the same events told in one voice.

02

What does a creator owe?

Begin with “thy part, the which thou owest me” and define the duty being claimed. Test it against what Adam receives in Paradise Lost, what parents owe children elsewhere in the novel, and what granting the demand for a companion would have risked.

03

Is eloquence evidence?

The creature is the novel’s most persuasive speaker. Compare the effect of his words on Victor, on blind De Lacey and on Walton with the effect of his appearance on everyone who sees him, and decide what the gap between the two responses proves.

04

Which Frankenstein should we read?

The 1818 text gives Victor fewer appeals to destiny; the 1831 revision strengthens fate and warning. Choose one episode and argue how the choice of text shifts the blame—then say what is gained by reading both.
