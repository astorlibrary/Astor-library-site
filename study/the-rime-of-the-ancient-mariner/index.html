Samuel Taylor Coleridge

The Rime of the Ancient Mariner

An old sailor stops a man on his way into a wedding and will not let him go. The story he tells has a crime in it that takes two lines and no explanation, a punishment that falls hardest on two hundred men who did not commit it, and a release that arrives while he is not trying for it. It ends with a neat moral about loving all creatures, spoken by a man who is still, decades later, compelled to seize strangers, and heard by one who goes home stunned and skips the party.

Sources

Quotations are taken from the Project Gutenberg text of The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (eBook #151), and references give the part, since the source text prints the seven parts without line numbers. Quotations follow this transcription of the late text in seven parts, the version Coleridge settled in his collected editions and the one the Astor edition prints as the 1834 text. It is the verse only: it carries no line numbers, and it omits the Latin epigraph and the prose marginal gloss added in 1817, so the gloss is described here but never quoted. The spelling is the revised one (the 1798 printing has the mock-antique ‘Ancyent Marinere’), and readers comparing the first printing will find stanzas there that Coleridge later cut.
