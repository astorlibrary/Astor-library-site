Study editions/The Picture of Dorian Gray

Oscar Wilde

The Picture of Dorian Gray

A painter finishes the best thing he will ever do, a clever man says something careless about youth in front of the sitter, and the sitter wishes the picture could age instead of him. Wilde then spends twenty chapters watching what a person becomes when consequences are stored somewhere else. The talk is the pleasure of the book and also its subject: almost every disaster in it starts with a remark made to be admired.

1890In Lippincott’s Monthly Magazine, in thirteen chapters, and attacked in the press within days.
1891Wilde added six chapters, softened some passages, sharpened others, and put a preface of aphorisms in front of the whole thing.
The 1891 bookTwenty chapters and a preface, published in London by Ward, Lock and Co.
Twenty chaptersChapter XI covers eighteen years and contains scarcely a scene — a catalogue of jewels, embroideries and ancestors standing in for a life.

Where the material comes from, and where to take it.

Every quotation on this page was located in Standard Ebooks text of The Picture of Dorian Gray — Wilde’s revised 1891 edition before it was published, and carries its chapter, with the 1891 opening cited as Preface. Wilde’s only novel exists in two authorial forms. This is the twenty-chapter book of 1891 with its preface, not the thirteen-chapter version printed in Lippincott’s Monthly Magazine in 1890.
