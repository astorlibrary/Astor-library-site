Editorial standards

How the pages are made and checked.

Astor Library publishes complete texts with notes, summaries, study guides and free resources. This page sets out what is checked, and against what.

Every page starts from the book itself. Summaries are written from a full reading of the text, not from other summaries. Facts about a book, such as its dates, printings and performances, come from named sources, and interpretation is labelled as interpretation.

01 · Quotations

Every quotation is checked word for word.

Each quotation in a study guide is compared with a named public-domain text of the book before it is published, and the guide says which text that is. References give act, scene and line for plays, line numbers for poems and the chapter or section for prose, so any quotation can be found in a printed edition.

02 · Facts

Dates and history come from named sources.

Publication dates, textual history, performances and historical context are checked against records such as library catalogues, facsimiles and archives. Each book page lists the sources it used and links to them.

03 · Interpretation

Opinion is marked as opinion.

Where critics disagree, the page sets out the main position and the strongest reply to it, with the passages each side relies on. Summaries record what happens, in order, without choosing a reading for you.

04 · Corrections

Mistakes are corrected.

When a better source turns up or an error is found, the page is changed. To report one, write to support@astorlibrary.com.

Sources

Sources are named and linked.

Book pages end with the sources they used, linked where they are online. Primary texts come first, then established collections such as the Folger Shakespeare Library, the British Library, the Library of Congress, university archives and author societies.

The free guides are separate from the editions but are written the same way. They help with reading and teaching; they are not a substitute for the book.

Browse the book pages or open the free resource library.

The test for a note

Every note has to tell you something.

A note defines a word, identifies a reference, gives the context a passage needs, summarises an event or supports a reading. A note that does none of these is cut.
