Definition and history

Epic makes one life answer to a much larger history.

Epic scale does not come only from length, gods or battles. An epic joins an individual action to the fate of a household, people or future state. Achilles’s anger changes the course of the war at Troy; Odysseus comes home to find out whether Ithaca can be his home again. Aeneas’s journey is burdened by a city and empire that do not yet exist.

These poems also remember other tellings. Virgil writes with Homer in view; Milton invokes both classical epic and the book of Genesis while making the Fall unfold in English blank verse. Moby-Dick is a novel, but its invocations, catalogues, voyage and enormous adversary reach deliberately for the scale of epic.

Reading method

Features to examine.

01 · Beginning

The story opens in the middle

Epic often begins after decisive events have already occurred. The missing past returns through song, memory and report, and often the hero has to tell it himself, as Odysseus does to the Phaeacians and Aeneas to Dido.

02 · Repetition

A repeated scene always changes something

Feasts, arrivals, councils, recognitions and acts of hospitality recur with variations. The pattern teaches a reader what to expect, and the difference shows whether this household or this ruler treats a guest as custom requires.

03 · Scale

Small things are described as carefully as gods

A meal, a wound, a garment or a door gets the same attention as the gods and the history of a nation. Epic holds on to such details, and an object handed down can carry a family’s memory with it.

Close reading

Compare each repeated scene with the one before.

When a feast, arrival or act of storytelling recurs, place it beside the earlier version. Who welcomes the stranger? Who asks for a name too soon? What is offered, withheld or violated? Epic uses such familiar scenes to tell a good host from a bad one and a just ruler from an unjust one.

In translation, listen for register as well as plot. No English version of Homer or Virgil is neutral: each makes choices about pace, dignity and names. With Milton, listen for the long unrhymed sentences that run on across the line endings.

Terminology

Four terms used in this guide.

Invocation

An opening address to a muse or god, asking for the song and announcing its subject.

In medias res

Beginning in the middle of the action, with earlier events supplied later.

Epic simile

A long comparison that leaves the story for several lines, often to describe a scene from ordinary life.

Nostos

The Greek word for homecoming: the journey home, and the recovery of one’s household and place there.
