Close reading 59 · Michael Robartes and the Dancer

“Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold”

Written in the wake of the Great War, the Russian Revolution and the Irish troubles, twenty-two lines that have supplied more titles, headlines and epigraphs than any poem of the century—and that end not with a prophecy but a question.

W. B. YeatsThe Second Coming

Composed in January 1919 and collected in Michael Robartes and the Dancer (Cuala Press, 1921). The wording below follows that 1921 printing; two lines were altered in later collected editions, as the notes record.

How to read this page: read the two stanzas as two different kinds of speech—diagnosis, then vision. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Michael Robartes and the Dancer1921

Turning and turning in the widening gyre
The falcon cannot hear the falconer1;
Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold2;
Mere anarchy is loosed upon the world,
5The blood-dimmed tide is loosed, and everywhere
The ceremony of innocence is drowned3;
The best lack all conviction, while the worst
Are full of passionate intensity.4

Surely some revelation is at hand;
10Surely the Second Coming is at hand.
The Second Coming! Hardly are those words out
When a vast image out of Spiritus Mundi5
Troubles my sight: a waste of desert sand;
A shape with lion body and the head of a man,
15A gaze blank and pitiless as the sun,
Is moving its slow thighs, while all about it
Wind shadows of the indignant desert birds.
The darkness drops again but now I know
That twenty centuries of stony sleep
20Were vexed to nightmare by a rocking cradle6,
And what rough beast, its hour come round at last,
Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?7

Michael Robartes and the Dancer (1921), “The Second Coming” · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered

1

The gyre

A gyre is a spiral, and in Yeats’s private system of history—set out later in A Vision—civilisations unwind in widening spirals until they reverse. But the poem needs none of that to work: the image is a trained hawk circling out beyond its handler’s voice. Control has a radius; the bird has passed it. The first two lines give the whole poem’s argument as a picture—a centre that was obeyed, and a circling thing that can no longer hear it.

2

The line that became a proverb

“Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold” is so familiar that its craft is easy to miss. Two clauses, two semicolons, plain monosyllables, and a rhythm that itself loosens across the line. Yeats does not say what falls apart; the abstraction is deliberate. The line has been borrowed for every crisis since—Achebe took it for a novel’s title—because it names collapse without specifying a cause, and every age supplies its own.

3

Innocence as ceremony

Not innocence itself but “the ceremony of innocence”—the rituals, forms and courtesies by which a culture keeps innocence possible. Yeats was a poet of ceremony; for him custom and rite were what held a civilisation together against the “blood-dimmed tide”. The phrase says that what drowns in anarchy is not just goodness but the ordered forms that let goodness exist.

4

The diagnosis

The first stanza ends with its most quoted couplet: the good are paralysed, the bad are fervent. Yeats wrote it in January 1919 with the Russian Revolution and the war’s aftermath in view, and the Irish conflict about to intensify. The couplet has been applied to every political moment since, and part of its power is its symmetry—best/worst, lack/full—which makes despair sound like a law of nature.

5

Spiritus Mundi

Latin for “the spirit of the world”—Yeats’s name for a universal store of images, a collective memory from which poets and visionaries draw. The image that rises from it is a sphinx: lion body, man’s head, a gaze “blank and pitiless as the sun”. It is not the Christ of the Second Coming but something older and inhuman, waking in the desert. The 1921 text has “a waste of desert sand” and “Wind shadows”; Yeats later revised these to “somewhere in sands of the desert” and “Reel shadows”—the version most readers know.

6

The cradle that vexed the sleep

“Twenty centuries of stony sleep” is the Christian era, and the “rocking cradle” that “vexed” it to nightmare is the cradle at Bethlehem. The image inverts the nativity: Christ’s birth did not end the old stone gods’ sleep peacefully but troubled it, and now, the cycle turning, the nightmare wakes. Yeats’s history runs in two-thousand-year reversals; the poem stands at one of the hinges.

7

Slouching

The famous close is a question, not a prophecy—“what rough beast…?” The speaker “knows” only that something is coming, not what. “Slouches” is the poem’s most brilliant verb: not marching, not rising, but slouching, the sullen shamble of something that need not hurry. And it goes “to be born”—the beast is not arriving but about to begin. The poem ends on the threshold of a birth it dreads, and leaves the reader there.

What the wording changes

A prophecy that everyone has borrowed and no one can date.

“The Second Coming” may be the most quoted poem in English of the twentieth century, and it is quoted almost entirely in fragments—the falcon, the centre, the best and the worst, the rough beast. Read whole, it is stranger than its fragments suggest. The first stanza is public and diagnostic, a description of a world coming loose; the second is private and visionary, a poet reporting an image that “troubles my sight”. Between them sits the word “Surely”, twice, in the tone of someone trying to convince himself that chaos must at least mean something. The poem is not confident. It is a man watching disorder and hoping, then fearing, that it is a sign.

The historical moment is specific and the poem refuses to name it. Yeats drafted it in January 1919, two months after the Armistice, with Bolshevik revolution in Russia, chaos in Germany, and Ireland moving toward the guerrilla war that would begin that month. Drafts show more direct references that he cut. What survived is deliberately vast—“the world”, “everywhere”, “twenty centuries”—so that the poem describes not an event but a condition, which is why it has been re-applied to every subsequent crisis. Its generality is its design, not its vagueness.

Behind the poem lies Yeats’s idiosyncratic theory of history, worked out with his wife’s automatic writing and published as A Vision in 1925: history moves in gyres, two-thousand-year cycles, each civilisation replaced by its opposite. The Christian era, begun at Bethlehem, is ending; something antithetical—pitiless, inhuman, sphinx-like—is due. Readers need not accept the system to feel the poem, and most do not; but knowing it explains why the beast goes to Bethlehem. It is not the Antichrist of Christian apocalypse but the next god, and the poem’s terror is that it does not know whether to welcome or dread what is coming to be born.

Take one question away

The poem ends with a question it does not answer. Is the rough beast a warning against what is coming—or a confession that the poet half-wants it?

Before reading

Separate the two stanzas.

Mark the first stanza as diagnosis (what is happening) and the second as vision (what the speaker sees). Note where the “I” first appears. The poem shifts from public statement to private sight, and the join is the word “Surely”.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Trace the borrowings.

  1. List every phrase from the poem you have met elsewhere—in titles, headlines, speeches.
  2. For each, find what it was applied to, and whether the application fits the poem’s sense.
  3. Decide why the first stanza is quoted far more than the second.
  4. Compare the 1921 lines “a waste of desert sand” and “Wind shadows” with the later revisions—which are stronger, and why?
Essay bridge

Ceremony and its enemies.

For an essay on Yeats’s politics, read “The Second Coming” beside “A Prayer for My Daughter”, the poem that follows it in the same 1921 book, which asks “How but in custom and in ceremony / Are innocence and beauty born?” The two poems answer each other: one drowns the ceremony of innocence, the other prays for its return.
