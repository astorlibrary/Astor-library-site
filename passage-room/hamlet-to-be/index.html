Close reading 01 · Hamlet

“To be, or not to be”

The speech is famous because it seems to offer a single question. Its sentences do something less settled: they test an answer, revise it, raise the stakes and stop themselves—and they never once say “I”.

William ShakespeareAct 3, Scene 1

Probably written around 1600–01. The speech survives in three early versions that differ significantly; the wording below follows the Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read the whole speech once without stopping. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Hamlet3.1

To be, or not to be, that is the question1:
Whether ’tis nobler in the mind2 to suffer
The slings and arrows of outrageous fortune,
Or to take arms against a sea of troubles3,
5And by opposing end them? To die—to sleep,
No more; and by a sleep to say we end
The heart-ache, and the thousand natural shocks
That flesh is heir to: ’tis a consummation
Devoutly to be wish’d. To die, to sleep.
10To sleep, perchance to dream—ay, there’s the rub4,
For in that sleep of death what dreams may come,
When we have shuffled off this mortal coil,
Must give us pause.

There’s the respect
15That makes calamity of so long life.
For who would bear the whips and scorns of time,
The oppressor’s wrong, the proud man’s contumely,
The pangs of dispriz’d love, the law’s delay,
The insolence of office, and the spurns
20That patient merit of the unworthy takes,
When he himself might his quietus make
With a bare bodkin?5 Who would these fardels bear,
To grunt and sweat under a weary life,
But that the dread of something after death,
25The undiscover’d country, from whose bourn
No traveller returns6, puzzles the will,
And makes us rather bear those ills we have
Than fly to others that we know not of?

Thus conscience does make cowards of us all7,
30And thus the native hue of resolution
Is sicklied o’er with the pale cast of thought,
And enterprises of great pith and moment,
With this regard their currents turn awry
And lose the name of action. Soft you now,
35The fair Ophelia!8 Nymph, in thy orisons
Be all my sins remember’d.

Hamlet, Act 3, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered from the start of the speech

1

No “I”

The infinitives make the opening feel general: “to be”, not “shall I live?” In thirty-three lines the speech never uses “I” or “me”—its pronouns are “we” and “us”, its examples anyone’s. Hamlet thinks about his own life and death in the grammar of a philosophical proposition, which is both his brilliance and his method of keeping the question at arm’s length. Nothing in the speech mentions Claudius, the Ghost or revenge.

2

A moral ranking

“Nobler” asks which choice has greater worth. Hamlet does not begin with happiness or relief; he weighs suffering and resistance as rival claims about honour—and locates the contest “in the mind”, where nobility cannot be seen or proved. The question of what a noble action would even look like shadows the whole play.

3

An impossible action

A person can take arms against an army, but not a sea. The mixed image gives resistance heroic energy and makes its object boundless at the same time: the fight is dressed in armour, and the enemy is water. Editors once tried to “correct” the line; it needs no correcting—the impossibility is the meaning. Opposing troubles like these can “end them” only by ending the one who opposes.

4

The metaphor changes sides

Sleep first promises an end to pain—“a consummation / Devoutly to be wish’d”, a completion prayed for like a religious hope. Then the metaphor produces its own objection: sleep includes dreaming. “The rub” is a term from bowls, an obstacle that deflects the bowl’s course; Hamlet’s own figure of speech deflects him. “Shuffled off this mortal coil” adds a further picture—“coil” meaning turmoil, the tangle of bodily life—before the thought lands on the plainest phrase in the speech: “Must give us pause.”

5

A legal discharge, a small knife

“Quietus” is the term for the settling of an account—a debt marked paid; a “bodkin” is a mere pin or small dagger; “fardels” are burdens, a porter’s bundles. The vocabulary makes suicide sound like bookkeeping done with a household object: the grandeur of “to be, or not to be” has narrowed to a clerk’s transaction. The list before it—oppression, insulted merit, the law’s delay—is striking for how ordinary its griefs are: a prince’s catalogue of sufferings that mostly belong to people without power.

6

The country no map shows

A “bourn” is a boundary or frontier. Death becomes unexplored territory from which “no traveller returns”—a strange claim in a play whose first act is commanded by a returned traveller. Whether Hamlet has forgotten the Ghost, discounts it (a devil, perhaps, as he worried a scene earlier), or speaks of bodily return rather than apparition, the speech quietly contradicts the plot around it. The dread it names is not punishment but uncertainty itself: “others that we know not of”.

7

“Conscience” cuts two ways

In Shakespeare’s English the word means both moral conscience and consciousness—thinking, awareness. Both senses work, and they accuse differently: moral scruple makes cowards, or thought itself does. The next lines choose the second: resolution’s healthy colour is “sicklied o’er” by the pale complexion of thinking, and great enterprises “lose the name of action”—not action itself, but the name; even here, Hamlet is precise about words.

8

The philosophy is interrupted

“Soft you now”—hush, break off: Ophelia enters, placed there as bait by her father and the King, who are listening behind an arras. The most inward speech in the play has eavesdroppers, and possibly—directors have staged it both ways—a speaker who knows it. Hamlet’s greeting asks her prayers to remember his sins: courteous, tender, and a reminder that sin, judgment and the afterlife were the speech’s unspoken furniture throughout.

What the wording changes

The speech thinks by revising itself.

Hamlet first makes death sound simple: sleep, an ending, release from the shocks inherited by flesh. Then one word inside that comparison—“dream”—reopens everything. The repeated infinitives give the speech a measured surface, but the punctuation records interruption: colon, dash, question, pause. Its form matters because Hamlet is not reporting a finished belief. We hear the act of thought, including the moment when an attractive answer becomes frightening, and the moment when a general principle—bear known ills rather than fly to unknown ones—quietly decides the question the opening posed.

The text itself has a history worth knowing. The 1603 first quarto—a garbled version, possibly reconstructed from memory by actors—prints “To be, or not to be, ay there’s the point”, and places the speech a scene earlier; the fuller 1604 quarto and the 1623 Folio give the version read today, with dozens of small differences between them. The comparison is instructive: “that is the question” holds the problem open where “there’s the point” reaches for a conclusion. Even the speech’s textual life repeats its subject—the same thought, revised.

This is also why the speech should not be reduced to indecision, or to a simple debate about suicide. Hamlet follows consequences with unusual seriousness, and what stops him is not weakness but the honest admission that the evidence runs out at death’s border. The cost is named without self-pity in the final lines: thinking of this quality turns enterprises aside. The play then supplies the irony the speech cannot see—within minutes Hamlet is cruel to Ophelia in prose, and the great question dissolves into the noise of the plot against him.

Take one question away

Does Hamlet fail to reach an answer—or discover that the question was never as simple as it sounded?

Before reading

Mark the pronouns and the stops.

Circle every pronoun—you will find “we” and “us”, never “I”. Then mark every dash, colon and question mark. The speech’s two most revealing patterns are who it avoids mentioning and where it interrupts itself.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Follow one metaphor to its collapse.

  1. Assign each group a metaphor: the sea of troubles, sleep and dreams, the undiscovered country, the quietus, the fardels.
  2. State what the metaphor first offers Hamlet.
  3. Find the exact word where it turns against him, if it does.
  4. Order the metaphors from most comforting to most frightening, and defend the order.
Essay bridge

Set the soliloquies side by side.

For an essay on thought and action, place this speech between “O that this too too solid flesh would melt” in 1.2—grief wishing for dissolution—and the graveyard’s prose meditation on Yorick’s skull in 5.1, where death is no longer an undiscovered country but a thing held in the hand. The argument of the play can be traced through what death is allowed to be in each.
