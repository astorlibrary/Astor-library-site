Passage Room/The Yellow Wall-Paper/Out at last

Close reading 27 · The Yellow Wallpaper

“I’ve got out at last”

A woman confined to a nursery for a “rest cure” has been studying the wallpaper all summer. On the last night she frees the woman she found behind its pattern—and speaks, for the first time, with complete authority.

Charlotte Perkins GilmanThe final entries

First published in The New England Magazine, January 1892. The story is a series of secret journal entries; by this page they have shortened to single racing lines.

How to read this page: decide, as you read, who holds power in each sentence—it changes hands. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Yellow WallpaperFinal entries

Why, there’s John at the door!

It is no use, young man, you can’t open it!1

How he does call and pound!

Now he’s crying for an axe.

It would be a shame to break down that beautiful door!

“John dear!” said I in the gentlest voice2, “the key is down by the front steps, under a plantain leaf!”

That silenced him for a few moments.

Then he said—very quietly indeed, “Open the door, my darling!”

“I can’t,” said I. “The key is down by the front door under a plantain leaf!”

And then I said it again, several times, very gently and slowly, and said it so often that he had to go and see, and he got it, of course, and came in. He stopped short by the door.

“What is the matter?” he cried. “For God’s sake, what are you doing!”

I kept on creeping just the same, but I looked at him over my shoulder.

“I’ve got out at last3,” said I, “in spite of you and Jane4! And I’ve pulled off most of the paper, so you can’t put me back5!”

Now why should that man have fainted?6 But he did, and right across my path by the wall, so that I had to creep over him every time!7

The Yellow Wallpaper · public-domain text of the closing entries

1

“Young man”

For the whole story John has managed his wife with diminutives—“blessed little goose”, “little girl”. Now she addresses him, in her head, as “young man”: the tone of an adult amused by a child at a locked door. The story’s power relation inverts in two words. He pounds and cries for an axe; she considers the door’s beauty. Panic has changed sides.

2

Gentleness as strategy

All summer, gentleness was the register of her submission—the voice in which she agreed to rest, to eat, to stop writing. She now deploys the same voice to direct him: the key’s location, repeated “very gently and slowly” until he obeys. It works—“he had to go and see”. The docile tone the cure demanded of her has become an instrument she uses on its enforcer.

3

Who got out?

Grammatically the speaker is the woman from behind the wallpaper: the narrator has torn the paper to release her, and by these final entries the two have merged—earlier that day she wondered whether the creeping women outside “all come out of that wallpaper as I did”. “Out at last” is triumphant and true: someone has escaped the pattern. Whether a self survived the escape is the question the story leaves creeping round the room.

4

The Jane problem

Jane has never been mentioned before. She may be Jennie, John’s sister and housekeeper, misprinted or shortened—or she may be the narrator’s own, never-before-given name, in which case the sentence says she has escaped herself: the dutiful wife who kept the journal. The text supports both readings and settles neither. One word, placed at the climax, blows open the whole question of who has been speaking.

5

The paper as prison wall

Tearing the wallpaper is treated as demolishing a cell: with the pattern gone, “you can’t put me back”. The logic is mad and exact. The paper’s pattern—strangling, barred, full of trapped heads in her descriptions—was the story’s image for everything that held her: the cure, the marriage, the diagnosis. She has read the room correctly; only her literalism is insane.

6

The physician faints

John has stood for medical authority all story—“a physician of high standing”, laughing at her fancies, prescribing the rest. Confronted with the result of his treatment, he drops unconscious: the classic feminine swoon, performed by the man of science. Her question—“why should that man have fainted?”—is asked in genuine puzzlement, and “that man” finishes the demotion begun by “young man”: husband, doctor and gaoler reduced to an obstacle with no name.

7

“Every time”

The last two words of the story reveal that the creeping is circular: she is rounding the walls again and again, crossing his body on each circuit. Escape has delivered her into an orbit. The final image holds both truths together: she is free of him at last, and she is creeping in a circle inside a locked room.

What the wording changes

Victory and catastrophe in the same sentences.

The ending is written so that liberation and breakdown are the same event, sentence by sentence. Every sign of the narrator’s new power, the calm, the command over John, the finished project, is also a symptom. Gilman does not let the reader keep one reading without the other, and a century of criticism has split both ways: the madwoman as defeated victim, and the madwoman as the only person in the house who acted. The prose supports both because it was built to.

The story’s form does quiet work here. These are journal entries—secret writing, “dead paper”, the one activity John forbade—and by the end the entries have contracted into single breathless lines. The style enacts the diagnosis while defeating its treatment: the cure demanded she stop writing, and the story exists because she did not. First-person narration matters medically too. The rest cure operated on the principle that the patient’s own account of herself could be discounted; the story’s entire method is to give us nothing but that account, and let its accuracy shame the experts.

Gilman knew the treatment from inside. She had undergone Silas Weir Mitchell’s rest cure herself in 1887—the story names him: John threatens to send the narrator “to Weir Mitchell in the fall”—and in a 1913 piece, “Why I Wrote The Yellow Wallpaper”, she said she sent the doctor a copy and later heard he had altered his methods. The claim is hers and historians have not confirmed it, though her intention is on record: she wrote the story, she said, ‘to save people from being driven crazy’.

Take one question away

If “Jane” is the narrator’s own name, what exactly has she escaped from—and is there anyone left who escaped?

Before reading

Track the voices.

Mark every sentence spoken aloud and every sentence thought. Note which register each uses—the gentle wifely voice, the amused observer, the triumphant escapee. The horror of the ending lives in how smoothly the narrator moves between them.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Put the diagnosis on trial.

  1. List John’s treatments across the story: rest, no writing, no visitors, the nursery.
  2. Beside each, note the narrator’s condition when it was imposed and after.
  3. Write the one-paragraph report John might file after this scene.
  4. Compare his imagined report with the journal we actually read. Which document would the era have believed? Which do we?
Essay bridge

The room as argument.

For an essay on setting or symbol, inventory the room’s details—barred windows, nailed-down bed, the ring in the wall, a “nursery” at the top of the house—and the narrator’s early, innocent explanations for each. The story teaches close reading by example: she misreads her prison as a nursery, then reads it correctly through the paper.
