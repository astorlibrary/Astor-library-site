Passage Room/Robinson Crusoe/The footprint

Close reading 37 · Robinson Crusoe

“the print of a man’s naked foot”

After some fifteen years alone, the castaway who has prayed for human company finds evidence of it—a single footprint on his shore. What follows is one of fiction’s great studies of fear rewriting a world.

Daniel DefoeThe footprint on the shore

Published in 1719 and often called the first English novel. Crusoe narrates his own history, years later, with a tradesman’s exactness. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: watch how carefully he checks the evidence—and how little the checking helps. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Robinson CrusoeThe footprint

The Life and Strange Surprizing Adventures of Robinson Crusoe. Written by Himself

It happened one day, about noon1, going towards my boat, I was exceedingly surprised with the print of a man’s naked foot on the shore2, which was very plain to be seen on the sand. I stood like one thunderstruck, or as if I had seen an apparition.3 I listened, I looked round me, but I could hear nothing, nor see anything; I went up to a rising ground to look farther; I went up the shore and down the shore, but it was all one; I could see no other impression but that one. I went to it again to see if there were any more, and to observe if it might not be my fancy; but there was no room for that, for there was exactly the print of a foot—toes, heel, and every part of a foot4. How it came thither I knew not, nor could I in the least imagine; but after innumerable fluttering thoughts, like a man perfectly confused and out of myself5, I came home to my fortification, not feeling, as we say, the ground I went on, but terrified to the last degree, looking behind me at every two or three steps, mistaking every bush and tree, and fancying every stump at a distance to be a man6.

When I came to my castle (for so I think I called it ever after this), I fled into it like one pursued7.

Robinson Crusoe · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

Noon, going to the boat

The sentence opens like an entry in an account-book: one day, about noon, on the way to the boat. Defoe’s revolutionary realism works by this flatness—the most shattering event in the novel is time-stamped like a delivery. The style has spent two hundred pages recording goats, crops and gunpowder in exactly this voice, which is why the interruption lands so hard: the ledger was not built for apparitions.

2

One print, naked, singular

Every word is load-bearing. A “print” is evidence, not presence—a person reduced to a sign. “Naked” says the foot was unshod: not a European sailor but, to Crusoe’s instant assumption, a “savage”—the fear has a colonial shape from its first second. And it is singular: one print, with no track leading to or from it. Readers have puzzled over that impossibility for three centuries; the strangeness is the point, whether Defoe planned it or not.

3

Thunderstruck, as at a ghost

For the sign of a living man, Crusoe reaches for supernatural language: thunderstruck, an apparition. The inversion is exact—he has spent years praying to be “delivered” from solitude, and the first evidence of company arrives as a haunting. What he wanted in the abstract is terror in the particular. Few novels have caught more honestly the difference between longing for human beings and meeting the fact of one.

4

The empiricist at work

Crusoe does everything a rational man should: listens, looks, climbs for a vantage, walks the shore both ways, returns to re-examine the print, and tests the hypothesis that it “might not be my fancy”. The verification is forensic—“toes, heel, and every part of a foot”. Defoe, writing in the first generation of English empiricism, gives his narrator its method entire. The evidence is confirmed; the method then proves useless against what the evidence means.

5

“Out of myself”

The phrase is casual idiom and precise psychology at once. Crusoe’s self has been fifteen years in the making on this island—king, as he elsewhere jokes, of all he surveys. One footprint dislocates it: “innumerable fluttering thoughts”, the ground unfelt beneath him. The composed narrator writing years later can name the state; the man on the sand was simply gone from himself.

6

The island rewritten

Fear repopulates the landscape: every bush, tree and stump becomes a man. For fifteen years the island’s objects were resources—timber, shade, forage. In one afternoon they are all possible enemies. Nothing on the island has changed except its meaning; Defoe shows perception itself as the thing that fear conquers first, in a sentence any anxious reader recognises from the inside.

7

The castle renamed by flight

The parenthesis is quietly devastating: “for so I think I called it ever after this”. The home he built as a settlement becomes, from this day, a “castle”—a word that admits siege. He flees into it “like one pursued”, though nothing pursues; the next sentence confesses he cannot even remember whether he used the ladder or the rock-hole. The fortification was always defensive; now its builder finally feels why.

What the wording changes

The evidence is checked; the meaning runs wild anyway.

The scene’s structure is a collision between two halves of Crusoe—and of the novel’s new realism. One half is the auditor: the man who counts, measures, verifies, and narrates in the steady past tense of a report. The other is the imagination, which Defoe releases the moment verification is complete. The order matters. Fancy is not corrected by evidence here; it is licensed by it. Once the print is certainly real, everything else—bushes, stumps, the silence—becomes uncertainly hostile, and the empirical method has no further purchase.

The fear also has a shape worth naming. Crusoe never once imagines the print belongs to a potential friend, rescuer or fellow castaway; his thoughts run immediately to “savages” and devouring. The assumption comes from the trade Crusoe was in—he was shipwrecked on a voyage to buy enslaved people—and from the century’s picture of the Caribbean. The novel, in time, half-examines this: the man Crusoe eventually meets becomes Friday, whom he loves after his fashion and never stops mastering—naming him, teaching him “Master” as his own name.

For years afterwards—the novel is explicit—Crusoe lives differently: no fires by day, no visible smoke, a second retreat built, the coast reconnoitred with dread. One print, never explained, reorganises an entire life. That may be the scene’s deepest realism. It understands that safety is a story we tell about our surroundings, and that a single anomalous fact—checked, confirmed, and utterly silent about its meaning—can end the story for good.

Take one question away

Crusoe verifies the footprint completely and understands it not at all. What is the difference between evidence and meaning that this scene insists on?

Before reading

Feel the style first.

Read any earlier paragraph of Crusoe’s island housekeeping—goats, raisins, fence-posts—then this passage. Note three ways the voice stays identical and one way it breaks. Defoe’s horror works because the bookkeeper keeps the books even while terrified.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Track the investigation.

  1. List Crusoe’s verifying actions in order: listen, look, climb, walk, return, re-examine.
  2. Mark the exact sentence where investigation stops and imagination takes over.
  3. List what fear does next: the renamed castle, the man-shaped stumps, the flight from nothing.
  4. Write one sentence on why the checking made the fear worse, not better.
Essay bridge

From the print to Friday.

For an essay on the novel and empire, follow the footprint forward: the years of hiding, the discovered bones, the rescue of the man Crusoe names Friday, and the vocabulary of that first meeting—kneeling, foot on head, the word “Master” taught before “yes” and “no”. Ask what the single anonymous print allowed Crusoe to imagine, and what the actual man was permitted to be.
