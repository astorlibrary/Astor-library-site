Passage Room/The Taming of the Shrew/Katherina’s last speech

Close reading 84 · The Taming of the Shrew

“Thy husband is thy lord, thy life, thy keeper”

The play’s loudest woman ends it with its longest speech, instructing two other wives in obedience. Whether she is broken, in on the joke, or saying something the play cannot quite control is the question every production has to answer—and the text will not answer it for them.

William ShakespeareAct 5, Scene 2

Probably written around 1592. At the wedding feast, Petruchio has wagered that his wife will come when called; Katherina comes, and is told to lecture the other wives. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read it three ways—straight, ironic, and as a performance for an audience of men—before deciding anything. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Taming of the Shrew, Act 5, Scene 2 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · two stretches omitted at the ellipses · lines numbered within the extract

1

The troubled fountain

An angry woman is a fountain stirred up—muddy, thick, and no one will drink from it. The image makes female anger an aesthetic fault before it is a moral one: it “blots thy beauty”. Katherina, whose rages filled the play’s first acts, is describing her former self as undrinkable water. Whether she is repudiating that self or reciting a lesson is exactly what the speech will not settle.

2

The catalogue of lordship

Lord, life, keeper, head, sovereign: five titles for a husband in a line and a half. The vocabulary is the Elizabethan homily on marriage, which the Church read from pulpits, and the speech reproduces it faithfully—so faithfully that some hear parody in the excess. What follows is an economic argument: he labours by sea and land; she lies warm; obedience is “too little payment for so great a debt”. The bargain is stated, and the audience is invited to check it.

3

Subject and prince

The analogy that governed Tudor thinking about order: as the subject to the prince, so the wife to the husband. Katherina extends it—a disobedient wife is “a foul contending rebel / And graceless traitor”. The political frame makes the speech more than domestic: it places marriage inside the whole hierarchy of the state, which is why the play has been read as an argument about power and not only about a marriage.

4

“My reason haply more”

Here the speech admits what the play has shown: her mind was as big as any man’s, her heart as great, her reason perhaps greater. The concession is made in the past tense—“hath been”—and then withdrawn: “our lances are but straws”. But a woman who says her reason was perhaps greater than a man’s is not, in that line, submitting. The speech contains its own counter-argument, and every ironic reading starts here.

5

The hand beneath the foot

The gesture that ends the speech: she offers to place her hand under Petruchio’s foot, “if he please”. It is the play’s most contested moment. Done literally, it is abject; done as theatre, it is a dare—and Petruchio does not take it. Whether the hand is offered in earnest, in irony, or as a public performance the couple have privately agreed, the text gives the gesture and withholds the tone.

6

Kiss me, Kate

Petruchio’s reply is not to accept the foot but to ask for a kiss. “There’s a wench” is coarse and delighted; “kiss me, Kate” repeats the line from their first meeting, when the kiss was a taunt. Whatever the speech meant, its reward is not submission received but affection asked for—which is either the play softening its own doctrine or the doctrine getting its way with a smile.

What the wording changes

The speech is the play’s problem, and the play knows it.

No speech in Shakespeare has been performed in more contradictory ways. Katherina has delivered it as a woman crushed, as a woman winking, as a woman who has found a game she and her husband can play against the world, and as a woman saying more than she means in order to win a bet. Each reading has textual support and each leaves something out. The straight reading has the Elizabethan marriage homily behind it and the play’s title in front of it; the ironic reading has “my reason haply more” and Petruchio’s kiss; the collaborative reading has the wager, which only she could have won for him. Shakespeare wrote a speech that can be all three, and the four-century argument is evidence of the design rather than a failure of it.

The frame matters and is usually cut. The play opens with an Induction in which a drunken tinker, Christopher Sly, is tricked into believing he is a lord and shown “The Taming of the Shrew” as an entertainment. The whole comedy is a play within a play, performed to a man who has been made to believe a lie about his own status. That frame does not tell us what Katherina’s speech means, but it tells us the play is interested in what it takes to make someone believe they are what they are told—which is the speech’s subject too.

What the speech cannot be is neutral. Its language of lords and sovereigns, subjects and traitors, labour and payment, is the language of a society that did treat wives as subjects, and the play’s comedy runs on that society’s assumptions even when it tests them. Modern productions must decide whether to play the speech against its words or with them, and modern readers must hold both the play’s wit and its world. The speech ends with a hand offered to a foot and a kiss asked for instead; the distance between those two gestures is where the play lives.

Take one question away

Katherina says her reason was perhaps greater than a man’s, and then offers her hand beneath his foot. Which line is the performance, and which is the woman?

Before reading

Three readings.

Before the notes, read the speech aloud straight, then ironically, then as a public performance the couple have agreed. Mark the lines that resist each reading. The lines that resist all three are where the argument lives.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

The wager.

  1. Recall the bet: Petruchio wagers that Kate will come when called, and she does.
  2. Ask who wins money, and who wins the room.
  3. Decide whether the speech is Kate paying a debt, winning a bet, or making a point to the other wives.
  4. Stage the last two lines: does Petruchio take the hand, or the kiss?
Essay bridge

The frame and the speech.

For an essay on the play’s design, connect the Christopher Sly Induction—a man made to believe he is a lord—to Katherina’s speech about lords and subjects. Ask whether the play is about taming a woman, or about how people are taught to believe in the roles they are given, and whether it believes in them itself.
