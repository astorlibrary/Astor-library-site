Passage Room/Cymbeline/Fear no more

Close reading 71 · Cymbeline

“Golden lads and girls all must, / As chimney-sweepers, come to dust”

Two young men who do not know they are princes sing a funeral song over a friend they do not know is a woman, who is not, in fact, dead. Out of that tangle Shakespeare made the most beautiful dirge in English.

William ShakespeareAct 4, Scene 2

Probably written around 1610. Guiderius and Arviragus, raised in a Welsh cave, speak the song over Imogen, disguised as the boy Fidele and drugged into a death-like sleep. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read it first as a song—the stanzas answer each other. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Cymbeline, Act 4, Scene 2 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · four lines of the third stanza omitted · lines numbered within the extract

1

Fear no more

Each stanza opens with the same consolation: the dead are beyond fear. Sun and winter, the extremes of the year, are the first things named: a shepherd’s or an outlaw’s idea of hardship, weather and not war. The song is spoken by boys raised in a cave, and its comforts are the comforts of people who have lived outdoors.

2

Wages

Life is a “worldly task”, and death is going home with the day’s pay. The metaphor is plain labour, a servant or a labourer paid off at evening, and it makes dying sound like the end of a shift. There is dignity in it and no grandeur; the dead have simply finished work.

3

Golden lads and chimney-sweepers

The couplet is the song’s most famous, and it levels everything: gilded youths and soot-black sweeps alike “come to dust”. Some have suggested that in Warwickshire dialect “golden lad” was the dandelion in flower and “chimney-sweeper” the same flower gone to seed—so that the line may hold one image of a plant turning to dust, not two social ranks. Whether or not that was in Shakespeare’s ear, the couplet works both ways, and its music has survived every explanation.

4

The reed and the oak

To the dead, “the reed is as the oak”—the frailest plant and the strongest tree are the same. Behind it lies the fable of the reed that bends and the oak that breaks, but the song goes further: beyond death, weakness and strength mean nothing. Sceptre, learning and medicine follow; power, knowledge and the art of staying alive all “come to dust”.

5

The charms

The last movement changes form: short, alternating lines, like a spell said over a grave. No exorcist, no witchcraft, no unlaid ghost. Guiderius and Arviragus, who know nothing of courts, know the old country protections for the dead, and the song ends as a ritual. The theatre of 1610 would have felt the lines as genuinely apotropaic—words to keep the dead safe.

6

Quiet consummation

“Consummation” is completion, ending—Hamlet uses the word of death too. The song wishes the dead a quiet finish and a famous grave, and then the play’s irony closes over it: the body they sing to is Imogen, alive, drugged, and about to wake beside a headless corpse she will mistake for her husband. The most perfect dirge in Shakespeare is sung over someone who will get up.

What the wording changes

A funeral song, and the ‘corpse’ wakes up minutes later.

The dirge is famous on its own, and it has been set to music and read at real funerals for four centuries. But its context is one of Shakespeare’s strangest. The singers are Cymbeline’s lost sons, kidnapped as infants and raised as mountain boys; the corpse is their sister Imogen, disguised as a boy, who has taken a potion that mimics death; beside her they lay the headless body of the villain Cloten, dressed in Imogen’s husband’s clothes. None of them knows any of this. The song’s clear, levelling wisdom—that all come to dust—is spoken by people entirely mistaken about who is dead, and it is followed by one of the most grotesque scenes in the canon, as Imogen wakes and grieves over the wrong body.

This is the method of Shakespeare’s late romances: to place the most serious poetry inside plots so improbable that they announce themselves as fairy tale. Cymbeline ends with two dozen revelations in a single scene; the dirge sits in the middle like something true in a world of contrivance. Its plainness is the point. Where the plot is baroque, the song is simple, sun, winter, wages, dust, and it says the one thing in the play that no reversal can change. Everyone in the theatre, singers and sleepers alike, will come to dust; the play’s happy ending does not repeal that.

The song’s form deserves notice. It is in trochaic tetrameter, the metre of spells and songs, and its stanzas alternate between the two brothers before they join in the refrain, a shape borrowed from the ballad and the antiphon. Shakespeare gives it, deliberately, to the two characters with the least learning, and lets them speak a wisdom the court has lost. When the brothers turn out to be princes, the play suggests that this song is what royalty should sound like: unlettered, honest, and in tune with the dust.

Take one question away

The dirge is true and sung over a living woman. Does the play’s irony undercut the song, or does the song’s truth survive the plot that surrounds it?

Before reading

Hear the two voices.

Read the song with two readers alternating stanzas and joining for the refrains. The form is call and response. Ask why Shakespeare gives the wisdom to two brothers speaking together, and not to one mourner.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Test the dandelion.

  1. Read “golden lads and girls” and “chimney-sweepers” as social ranks; then as a flower and its seed-head.
  2. Decide which reading you prefer and whether the couplet needs either.
  3. Find the other levelling images (reed and oak, sceptre and dust) and ask what they have in common.
  4. Write a fourth stanza in the song’s metre.
Essay bridge

Poetry inside a fairy tale.

For an essay on the late romances, set the dirge beside Prospero’s “Our revels now are ended” and the statue scene of The Winter’s Tale. In each, the plainest truth in the play is spoken inside an improbable plot. Ask how the romances use impossibility to make their wisdom heard.
