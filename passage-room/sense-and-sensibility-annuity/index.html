Close reading 64 · Sense and Sensibility

“people always live for ever when there is an annuity”

A man promised his dying father he would provide for his sisters. Over one conversation with his wife, three thousand pounds becomes fifteen hundred, becomes an annuity, becomes fifty pounds now and then, becomes nothing. Austen never raises her voice.

Jane AustenChapter II

Published in 1811, Austen’s first novel in print. John Dashwood has inherited Norland; his stepmother and half-sisters have almost nothing. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: keep a running total; the scene is arithmetic. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Sense and SensibilityChapter II

“Certainly—and I think I may afford to give them five hundred pounds a-piece…”

“To be sure it is; and, indeed, it strikes me that they can want no addition at all1. They will have ten thousand pounds divided amongst them.…”

“That is very true, and, therefore, I do not know whether, upon the whole, it would not be more advisable to do something for their mother while she lives, rather than for them—something of the annuity kind I mean.… A hundred a year would make them all perfectly comfortable.”

His wife hesitated a little, however, in giving her consent to this plan. “To be sure,” said she, “it is better than parting with fifteen hundred pounds at once. But, then, if Mrs. Dashwood should live fifteen years we shall be completely taken in.2”

“Fifteen years! my dear Fanny; her life cannot be worth half that purchase.3”

“Certainly not; but if you observe, people always live for ever when there is an annuity to be paid them4; and she is very stout and healthy, and hardly forty.… It has given me such an abhorrence of annuities, that I am sure I would not pin myself down to the payment of one for all the world.”

“It is certainly an unpleasant thing,” replied Mr. Dashwood, “to have those kind of yearly drains on one’s income.… it takes away one’s independence.5”

“Undoubtedly; and after all you have no thanks for it.… It may be very inconvenient some years to spare a hundred, or even fifty pounds6 from our own expenses.”

1

The first cut

Earlier in the same conversation John has already cut his intended gift from three thousand pounds to fifteen hundred (“five hundred pounds a-piece”). Fanny’s first move is to suggest the sisters need nothing at all, on the grounds that they will have ten thousand pounds between them, money from their father’s estate and none of it from John. Austen sets the pattern: nobody says no; every proposal is met with a reason why less would be kinder.

2

“Completely taken in”

Fanny objects to the annuity because Mrs Dashwood might live fifteen years, in which case, she says, “we shall be completely taken in”. The phrase treats a widow’s survival as a swindle. It is the scene’s most revealing slip: for Fanny, money paid to a living relation is money stolen from her, and longevity is a kind of fraud.

3

A life priced

In “Her life cannot be worth half that purchase”, John values his stepmother’s remaining years the way an annuity-broker would, as a purchase price. Austen lets him say it in the language of the market without comment. He thinks he is being businesslike, and does not notice that he is being cruel.

4

The immortal annuitant

The scene’s most famous line is Fanny’s: “people always live for ever when there is an annuity to be paid them”. It is funny because it is exactly what a mean person believes, and Fanny proves it with a family anecdote: her mother “clogged” with payments to old servants, one of whom was rumoured dead and disobligingly turned out not to be. Austen’s comedy depends on hearing selfishness reason in its own voice, fluently, with examples.

5

Independence

John finds the word that lets him off: a yearly payment “takes away one’s independence”. He has made the promised gift an assault on his own liberty. The vocabulary of high principle (independence, one’s fortune being one’s own) is borrowed to dress a breach of faith. Austen’s method throughout is to let the characters supply their own moral language and watch it turn inside out.

6

Even fifty pounds

From three thousand to “a hundred, or even fifty pounds”, and within a page the fifty becomes “such kind of neighbourly acts” as helping with the move. Austen tracks the descent with a bookkeeper’s precision, and the reader keeps the ledger the couple will not keep. The promise to a dying father has been reasoned, in a single conversation, into occasional gifts of fish and game.

What the wording changes

The annuity

Austen’s first published novel opens with money, and this chapter shows how she handles it. The scene is a dialogue with almost no narrator’s judgement, close to a playscript, in which two people talk a solemn promise down to nothing. Nobody lies. Every step is reasonable in isolation: the sisters have some money already; an annuity is a burden; independence matters; gratitude cannot be bought. The reader supplies the running total, and the total is the indictment. The funniest and most damning thing a novelist can do with selfishness is to let it explain itself, and Austen does.

The economics were not abstract to Austen’s readers. Under primogeniture and entail, estates passed to male heirs, and the women of a family depended on the kind of informal promise John Dashwood is here dismantling. After her father died, Austen, her mother and her sister lived partly on the goodwill of her brothers. The comedy of the annuity rests on a real terror: women with no legal claim, whose whole future depends on a brother’s honour and a sister-in-law’s arithmetic. Fanny wins the argument, and the Dashwood women end up in a cottage in Devon.

Fanny Dashwood is also Austen’s first great study in a type she would perfect: the person whose selfishness is so complete that it has the coherence of a philosophy. Her anecdote about annuities, her horror of being pinned down, her certainty that no one is ever grateful, all hang together. Nobody contradicts her in the scene. She is describing the world as it looks from inside a mind that counts everything, and Austen lets that mind speak fluently and trusts the reader to hear what it says.

Take one question away

Nobody in the scene lies, and the promise ends up broken. Where exactly does the dishonesty happen?

Before reading

Keep the ledger.

Write down each sum as it appears: three thousand, fifteen hundred, an annuity of a hundred, fifty, “neighbourly acts”. Note who proposes each reduction and the reason given. The scene is a descending staircase, and each step has a rail to hold.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Argue Fanny’s case.

  1. Take Fanny’s side and make each of her points as persuasively as you can.
  2. Then find the assumption that makes them all work: what she believes money is for.
  3. Identify the one sentence where the mask slips (“completely taken in”, or another).
  4. Discuss why Austen gives Fanny the best lines.
Essay bridge

Sense, sensibility and money.

For an essay on the novel’s title, connect this scene to Elinor’s later negotiations with a world of small incomes and to Marianne’s contempt for prudence. Ask whether “sense” in the novel is a virtue, a survival skill, or the thing John Dashwood has too much of.
