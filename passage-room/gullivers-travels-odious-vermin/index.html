Passage Room/Gulliver’s Travels/Odious vermin

Close reading 45 · Gulliver’s Travels

“the most pernicious race of little odious vermin”

Gulliver has spent pages proudly explaining England to a giant king. The king listens, strokes his tiny guest, and delivers the verdict the whole conversation was built to earn.

Jonathan SwiftPart II, Chapter VI

Published in 1726, at the height of Swift’s war on human self-satisfaction. Gulliver is six inches tall at the court of Brobdingnag, a nation of giants. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: keep the scale in mind—every judgment is spoken by a giant to a creature in his palm. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Gulliver’s TravelsPart II, Ch. VI

My little friend Grildrig, you have made a most admirable panegyric upon your country1; you have clearly proved, that ignorance, idleness, and vice, are the proper ingredients for qualifying a legislator2; that laws are best explained, interpreted, and applied, by those whose interest and abilities lie in perverting, confounding, and eluding them.

I observe among you some lines of an institution, which, in its original, might have been tolerable, but these half erased, and the rest wholly blurred and blotted by corruptions.3 … It does not appear… that men are ennobled on account of their virtue; that priests are advanced for their piety or learning; soldiers, for their conduct or valour; judges, for their integrity; senators, for the love of their country; or counsellors for their wisdom.

… I cannot but conclude the bulk of your natives to be the most pernicious race of little odious vermin4 that nature ever suffered to crawl upon the surface of the earth.5

Gulliver’s Travels, Part II, Chapter VI · public-domain text · ellipses mark shortened stretches

1

Praise that means its opposite

A “panegyric” is a formal speech of praise. The king calls Gulliver’s defence of England admirable—then lists everything it accidentally proved. The whole verdict is delivered as mock-compliment: the more sincerely Gulliver boasted, the more damningly he testified. Swift’s irony works by keeping the courteous surface (“my little friend”) over a demolition.

2

The praise inverted into charge

“You have clearly proved” introduces a series in which each institution is defined by its opposite virtue: legislators qualified by “ignorance, idleness, and vice”; laws interpreted by those skilled in “perverting, confounding, and eluding them”. The rhetorical trick is that these are drawn from Gulliver’s own account. The satire is not the king’s invention; it is Gulliver’s England, merely repeated back without the flattery.

3

A fair man’s reluctance

The king is not a cynic. He concedes the “original” institution “might have been tolerable” before it was “blurred and blotted by corruptions”. Swift makes the judge reasonable and slow, which sharpens the verdict: this is the considered conclusion of a decent outsider who wanted to think better of us and could not.

4

The scale becomes the argument

“Little odious vermin”—the insult depends on size. To a giant, Gulliver’s countrymen literally are tiny crawling things, and Swift lets the physical fact carry the moral one: seen from high enough above, human grandeur looks like an infestation. Part I reversed this, making Gulliver the giant among the petty Lilliputians; here he is the insect. Voyage by voyage, the book changes the reader’s height until no human vanity is left.

5

“Suffered to crawl”

The final clause denies humanity even the dignity of walking upright: nature merely “suffered”—permitted, tolerated—these creatures “to crawl upon the surface of the earth”. It is one of the most contemptuous sentences in English, and Swift assigns it to the most admirable ruler in the book. The verdict is meant to sting precisely because we cannot dismiss the speaker as a monster.

What the wording changes

The satire is built by the reader’s own guide.

The scene’s engine is dramatic irony sustained across a whole chapter. Gulliver, proud and patriotic, has answered the giant king’s questions about England in perfect good faith—its parliament, its courts, its wars, its finances—convinced he is showing off a great civilisation. The king asks the questions a genuinely disinterested intelligence would ask, and each honest answer digs the hole deeper. By the time the verdict lands, it has been earned entirely from Gulliver’s own material. Swift never has to editorialise; he only has to let a boaster be heard by someone with no reason to be impressed.

The changed scale is the method. Swift’s great technical discovery in the Travels is that you can satirise humanity by adjusting its size. Among the tiny Lilliputians, human political passions—the war over which end of an egg to crack—shrink to visible absurdity. Among the Brobdingnagian giants, human pride shrinks to an insect’s. The reader is never allowed a stable vantage from which mankind looks dignified; every book alters the magnification until some new vanity is exposed. This passage is the hinge, where the comfortable superiority Gulliver felt in Lilliput is turned back on him and, through him, on us.

What keeps the book from mere sourness is the king himself. Swift could have made the judge cruel; instead he is patient, humane, and clearly grieved by his conclusion. That decency is strategic. A misanthrope’s contempt is easy to shrug off; a good man’s considered disappointment is not. The passage makes the reader do the hardest thing satire demands: suspect that the outsider is right, and that the ‘most pernicious race of little odious vermin’ is us, seen without the flattery we usually supply ourselves.

Take one question away

Swift gives the harshest verdict on mankind to the kindest character in the book. Why is a fair judge’s contempt harder to dismiss than a cruel one’s?

Before reading

Rebuild Gulliver’s boast.

Every charge the king makes is a reply to something Gulliver said proudly. From the verdict alone, reconstruct the flattering version Gulliver must have given—“our legislators are chosen for…”. The gap between the two is where the satire lives.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Change the altitude.

  1. List what human beings look like from Lilliputian scale (Gulliver the giant) and from Brobdingnagian scale (Gulliver the insect).
  2. Decide what each scale exposes: physical pettiness, moral pettiness, or both.
  3. Rewrite the king’s verdict as a modern outsider’s report on a country you know.
  4. Discuss whether the technique—satire by resizing—still works, and why.
Essay bridge

Toward the Yahoos.

For an essay on Swift’s misanthropy, set this verdict beside Part IV, where Gulliver meets the rational Houyhnhnms and the bestial Yahoos and comes to loathe his own species entirely. Trace the darkening: the king’s reasoned disappointment here becomes, by the final voyage, Gulliver’s own unhinged disgust—and ask whether Swift endorses it or diagnoses it.
