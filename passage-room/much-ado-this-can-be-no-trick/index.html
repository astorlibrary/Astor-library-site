Close reading 69 · Much Ado About Nothing

“This can be no trick”

It is a trick. Benedick has just overheard his friends staging a conversation for his benefit, and steps out of the arbour to reason himself, clause by clause, from confirmed bachelor to man in love. The speech is a masterpiece of self-deception—and it makes him happy.

William ShakespeareAct 2, Scene 3

Probably written around 1598–99. Don Pedro, Claudio and Leonato have let Benedick “overhear” that Beatrice loves him. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: watch each objection get raised and answered by the same man. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Much Ado About Nothing2.3

BenedickAdvancing from the arbour.This can be no trick: the conference was sadly borne.1 They have the truth of this from Hero. They seem to pity the lady: it seems her affections have their full bent. Love me? why, it must be requited.2 I hear how I am censured: they say I will bear myself proudly, if I perceive the love come from her; they say too that she will rather die than give any sign of affection. I did never think to marry: I must not seem proud: happy are they that hear their detractions, and can put them to mending. They say the lady is fair: ’tis a truth, I can bear them witness; and virtuous: ’tis so, I cannot reprove it; and wise, but for loving me3: by my troth, it is no addition to her wit, nor no great argument of her folly, for I will be horribly in love with her. I may chance have some odd quirks and remnants of wit broken on me, because I have railed so long against marriage: but doth not the appetite alter? A man loves the meat in his youth that he cannot endure in his age.4 Shall quips and sentences and these paper bullets of the brain5 awe a man from the career of his humour? No; the world must be peopled.6 When I said I would die a bachelor, I did not think I should live till I were married.

Much Ado About Nothing, Act 2, Scene 3 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

Wrong in the first four words

“This can be no trick”—and the audience has just watched the trick rehearsed. Benedick’s evidence is that the conversation was “sadly borne”, conducted gravely; his friends, in other words, acted well. The comedy of the whole speech is dramatic irony at full stretch: every inference he draws is reasonable, and every premise is false, and the result is the truth anyway, because he does love her.

2

The pivot

“Love me? why, it must be requited.” The question is astonishment; the answer arrives in the same breath, before any deliberation. Benedick decides in four words, and spends the rest of the speech constructing reasons for a decision already made. Shakespeare knew that this is how people persuade themselves: the conclusion first, the argument after.

3

The witness testifies

He examines the evidence for Beatrice’s merits like a court: fair—“I can bear them witness”; virtuous—“I cannot reprove it”; wise, “but for loving me”. The legal vocabulary (witness, reprove, argument) gives his surrender the dignity of a judgment. And the joke at his own expense—loving him is the one blemish on her wisdom—shows the wit surviving the conversion. He is not a fool in love; he is a wit in love, which is the play’s subject.

4

Appetite alters

To excuse his reversal, Benedick reaches for the body: tastes change with age, a man cannot stomach at forty what he loved at twenty. The image makes his new love a matter of maturing digestion rather than surrender—and it is honest in a way the speech’s other arguments are not. People do change. The line is the speech’s one plain truth among its rationalisations.

5

Paper bullets of the brain

The jokes his friends will make at his expense are “paper bullets”—harmless ammunition, wit that only looks like weaponry. Benedick has fired such bullets all play at marriage and at Beatrice; now he disarms them in advance. The phrase is one of Shakespeare’s best for the play’s own medium: Much Ado is a war of paper bullets, and this is the moment its best marksman lays down his gun.

6

The world must be peopled

The clinching argument is the largest and the least personal: humanity must continue, so he must marry. It is comic in its grandiosity—Benedick enlisting the survival of the species to justify a change of heart—and it is followed by the speech’s best joke: “When I said I would die a bachelor, I did not think I should live till I were married.” The vow is kept by redefining it. Wit has found a way to surrender without admitting defeat.

What the wording changes

A man reasons his way to a conclusion he had already reached.

The gulling of Benedick is one of the great comic set-pieces in Shakespeare, and this speech is its payoff. The audience has watched Don Pedro, Claudio and Leonato stage their conversation with theatrical relish, and now watches Benedick take the bait with a solemnity that is funnier for being so reasonable. Every step is logical. The friends seemed sincere; Hero is a reliable source; the lady is fair and virtuous; appetites change; the world must be peopled. Only the first premise is false, and the play’s wisdom is that it does not matter—Benedick’s love was there before the trick, waiting for a pretext.

The speech is in prose, and prose is where Benedick lives. His wit works by turning, qualifying, joking against himself; verse would slow it down. Watch the sentence rhythms: short bursts of decision (“Love me? why, it must be requited”), long trains of justification, and the deflating aphorisms that close each movement. This is the rhythm of a mind talking itself into something and enjoying the talk. The parallel scene, in which Beatrice is gulled in turn, is written in verse and is shorter and more tender—Shakespeare gave each of his two wits the medium that suits them.

What makes the scene more than a joke is that the self-deception produces truth. Benedick and Beatrice have been fighting a “merry war” since before the play began, and the trick works because it tells them what they already knew. The friends’ lie is a kindness; the lovers’ conversions are real; and the marriage that results survives the play’s darker plot—Hero’s slander and mock death—because it was built on two people who could laugh at themselves. “The world must be peopled” is comic, and it is also the play’s answer to its own cynicism about love: the species goes on because people find reasons.

Take one question away

Benedick is deceived into a love that turns out to be true. Is the speech a portrait of self-deception, or of a man finally telling himself the truth?

Before reading

Number the arguments.

List every reason Benedick gives for changing his mind, in order. Then mark which are about Beatrice, which are about himself, and which are about the human race. The proportions are the comedy.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Play the arbour.

  1. One reader takes Benedick; the others are the hidden friends, reacting silently.
  2. Find the line where the friends should have to stifle laughter.
  3. Find the line where the audience should stop laughing and believe him.
  4. Decide whether both can be the same line.
Essay bridge

Two gullings.

For an essay on the play’s symmetry, set this prose soliloquy beside Beatrice’s ten-line verse response to her own gulling in the next scene. Ask why Shakespeare gives Benedick prose and Beatrice verse, and what each medium says about how the two characters love.
