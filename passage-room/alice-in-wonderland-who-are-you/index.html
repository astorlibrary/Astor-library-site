“Who are you?”

A child who has changed size several times before lunch meets a caterpillar who demands to know who she is. The rudest question in Wonderland is also philosophy’s oldest—and for once, the child’s answer is the rigorous one.

Lewis CarrollChapter V · Advice from a Caterpillar

Published in 1865, grown from a story told to the Liddell children on a river outing in 1862. The italics are Carroll’s own. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: take the jokes seriously—each pun carries a real problem. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Alice in WonderlandChapter V

The Caterpillar and Alice looked at each other for some time in silence: at last the Caterpillar took the hookah out of its mouth, and addressed her in a languid, sleepy voice.

“Who are you?1” said the Caterpillar.

This was not an encouraging opening for a conversation. Alice replied, rather shyly, “I—I hardly know, sir, just at present—at least I know who I was when I got up this morning, but I think I must have been changed several times since then2.”

“What do you mean by that?” said the Caterpillar sternly. “Explain yourself!”

“I can’t explain myself, I’m afraid, sir,” said Alice, “because I’m not myself, you see3.”

“I don’t see,” said the Caterpillar.

“I’m afraid I can’t put it more clearly,” Alice replied very politely, “for I can’t understand it myself to begin with; and being so many different sizes in a day is very confusing4.”

“It isn’t,” said the Caterpillar.

“Well, perhaps you haven’t found it so yet,” said Alice; “but when you have to turn into a chrysalis—you will some day, you know—and then after that into a butterfly, I should think you’ll feel it a little queer, won’t you?5”

“Not a bit,” said the Caterpillar.

“Well, perhaps your feelings may be different,” said Alice; “all I know is, it would feel very queer to me.”

“You!” said the Caterpillar contemptuously. “Who are you?”

Which brought them back again to the beginning of the conversation.6

Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland, Chapter V · public-domain text

1

The question under everything

Carroll’s italics land on you: not your name or business, but what you are. It is simultaneously the rudest possible opening—no greeting, from a stranger three inches high with a hookah—and the book’s deepest question, which Alice has already asked herself in Chapter II: “Who in the world am I? Ah, that’s the great puzzle!” Wonderland keeps handing the puzzle back to her, here in its bluntest form.

2

Identity given a timetable

Alice’s answer is scrupulously exact: she knows who she was this morning, but has “been changed several times since then”. For once the child’s answer is the rigorous one—she refuses to claim continuity she cannot verify. Behind the joke sits a genuine philosophical problem, old as the ship of Theseus: what makes a changing thing the same thing? Alice, who has been nine feet tall and three inches small before lunch, has better evidence than most philosophers.

3

Idioms turned literal

“Explain yourself!” demands the Caterpillar, meaning: clarify your statement. Alice answers the words instead of the idiom: she cannot explain her self, having lost track of it—“because I’m not myself, you see.” Another dead idiom (“I’m not myself today”) stands up and walks. This is Carroll’s signature method: Wonderland is a country where figures of speech are enforced literally, and the comedy doubles as a logician’s tour of how much nonsense ordinary language quietly contains.

4

“It isn’t.”

Alice offers her experience—many sizes in one day is confusing—and is flatly contradicted about her own feelings. The Caterpillar’s two-word replies parody a certain kind of adult conversation: assertion without argument, delivered from above (he is, after all, on a mushroom). Carroll’s child readers recognised the tone instantly; his adult readers eventually notice it is theirs.

5

The chrysalis argument

Alice’s comeback is genuinely good logic. You will change too, she points out—chrysalis, then butterfly—so your confidence about identity has a scheduled expiry. She has found the one creature whose whole existence is metamorphosis and asked whether transformation will feel “queer”. “Not a bit” is all the answer she gets; but the reader can score the exchange, and the child has won it.

6

The perfect circle

The conversation ends where it began, the same italicised question repeated—now “contemptuously”—and the narrator drily closes the loop: “Which brought them back again to the beginning.” Circularity is Wonderland’s favourite shape (the Caucus-race, the mad tea-table rotating endlessly), and it is also what unanswerable questions do. The scene has made no progress and lost nothing: the question was never going to be answered, only survived—which Alice, politely, does.

What the wording changes

Nonsense with rigorous rules.

Carroll—Charles Dodgson, mathematical lecturer at Christ Church, Oxford—built Wonderland’s nonsense out of logical materials: literalised idioms, syllogisms run backwards, definitions enforced past the point of sense. This scene is the method at its purest. Every joke is a real problem wearing motley: personal identity through change, the limits of introspection (“I can’t understand it myself to begin with”), the impossibility of comparing inner experience (“perhaps your feelings may be different”). Generations of philosophers have cited the exchange, not as decoration but because Carroll got the problems exactly right.

What steadies Alice—here and everywhere—is not certainty but manners. She answers rudeness “rather shyly”, “very politely”, “swallowing down her anger”; when she cannot say who she is, she can still say “sir”. The book’s quiet joke is that politeness turns out to be the durable core of self: Alice’s size, memory and even her recitations change (her poems keep coming out wrong), but her civility survives every transformation. In a world with no stable rules, being well brought-up functions as an identity.

The scene also carries the book’s tenderest undertone. Alice’s changes of size are a child’s actual condition—growing is involuntary, unsettling and constant, and adults keep demanding you explain yourself mid-transformation. The Caterpillar, so certain that becoming a butterfly will feel like nothing, is every grown-up who has forgotten what growing felt like. Carroll, who preferred the company of children and mourned their growing up, wrote the forgetting into a smoking insect and let a seven-year-old refute it.

Take one question away

Alice cannot say who she is, yet no reader doubts that she is someone. What is the “self” that survives all her changes—and can you define yours any better than she can?

Before reading

Collect the changes.

List every transformation Alice has undergone before this scene—shrinkings, growings, the pool of her own tears, the White Rabbit’s house. Her answer to the Caterpillar is a factual report; the list is her evidence.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Enforce the idioms.

  1. Find the two idioms the scene literalises: “explain yourself” and “I’m not myself”.
  2. Collect three more everyday idioms and write the Wonderland version of each—what happens if the words are obeyed exactly?
  3. Score the chrysalis argument as a formal debate: claim, counter-example, rebuttal. Who wins, and on what grounds?
  4. Decide what the Caterpillar could have said to actually answer Alice—and why Carroll made sure he didn’t.
Essay bridge

The great puzzle, twice.

For an essay on identity in the book, pair this scene with Chapter II, where Alice, alone, asks “Who in the world am I?” and tests whether she has turned into one of her schoolfriends by reciting her lessons. Alone, the question frightens her; face to face with the Caterpillar, she handles it with logic and manners. Trace what the difference says about how the book thinks selves are held together.
