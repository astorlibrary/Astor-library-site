Passage Room/The Man Who Was Thursday/Lo! it is Victoria

Close reading 77 · The Man Who Was Thursday

“he does say Victoria, and lo! it is Victoria”

Two poets argue in a London garden at sunset: one says poetry is disorder, the other that the most poetic thing in the world is a train arriving where the timetable said. Chesterton’s whole nightmare of a novel begins with a paradox about punctuality.

G. K. ChestertonChapter I · The Two Poets of Saffron Park

Published in 1908, subtitled A Nightmare. Gabriel Syme, the poet of order, answers Lucian Gregory, the poet of anarchy. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: read it as a duel of paradoxes, and score each blow. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Man Who Was ThursdayChapter I

“It is you who are unpoetical,” replied the poet Syme. “If what you say of clerks is true, they can only be as prosaic as your poetry. The rare, strange thing is to hit the mark; the gross, obvious thing is to miss it.1 We feel it is epical when man with one wild arrow strikes a distant bird. Is it not also epical when man with one wild engine strikes a distant station?2 Chaos is dull3; because in chaos the train might indeed go anywhere, to Baker Street or to Bagdad. But man is a magician, and his whole magic is in this, that he does say Victoria, and lo! it is Victoria.4 No, take your books of mere poetry and prose; let me read a time table, with tears of pride.5 Take your Byron, who commemorates the defeats of man; give me Bradshaw, who commemorates his victories.”

The Man Who Was Thursday, Chapter I · public-domain text

1

The reversal

Gregory the anarchist has claimed that poetry is disorder and that the sad clerks on the Underground are sad because their trains arrive. Syme turns the claim inside out in one balanced sentence: hitting the mark is rare and strange; missing it is gross and obvious. The chiasmus is Chesterton’s signature move—take the received opinion, swap its terms, and show that the swap is the truth. Order, not chaos, is the miracle.

2

Arrow and engine

Everyone grants that an archer hitting a distant bird with one arrow is epic. Syme demands the same admiration for an engine hitting a distant station. The parallel is exact—one wild arrow, one wild engine—and the word “wild” is the trick: the train is as wild as the arrow, and the wonder is that it is aimed. Chesterton loved to find romance in what the age had filed under machinery.

3

Chaos is dull

Three words that reverse the whole Romantic inheritance. Chaos, Syme says, is boring, because in chaos anything may happen and so nothing matters: Baker Street or Baghdad, it is all the same. Meaning requires that a thing could have been otherwise and was not. The anarchist wants freedom from destination; Syme says destination is the only thing that makes a journey a story.

4

Man the magician

The paragraph’s climax: “he does say Victoria, and lo! it is Victoria.” The biblical “lo!” makes a railway arrival sound like a miracle in Genesis—a word spoken, and the thing exists. Chesterton’s point is theological as well as comic: the human power to intend a result and achieve it is a small image of creation, and every kept promise is a kind of magic.

5

Bradshaw and Byron

Bradshaw’s was the Victorian railway guide, the great timetable; Byron is the poet of magnificent failure. Syme will read the timetable “with tears of pride” because it records victories—every train that arrived—while Byron records defeats. The joke is outrageous and Chesterton means it: the ordinary world keeping its word is more heroic than any Romantic ruin.

What the wording changes

The nightmare begins with a defence of the ordinary.

The Man Who Was Thursday is a book that will not stay still—a spy story, a farce, a theological allegory and a nightmare in turn, in which a poet-policeman infiltrates a council of anarchists and discovers that every member is a detective in disguise. But it opens calmly, with an argument in a suburban garden, and the argument is the key to everything after. Syme’s defence of the timetable is Chesterton’s defence of order, sanity and the world as given, against the fashionable nihilism of the 1900s that treated destruction as creativity. The rest of the novel drags Syme through chaos, chases, duels, a masked Sunday, until order is found, terrifyingly, at the centre.

The paradox is the method. Chesterton wrote in reversals—he called it standing on his head to see the world the right way up—and this passage shows the technique at its cleanest: take the anarchist’s premise (poetry is disorder), invert it (order is the true poetry), and prove the inversion with an image no one can refuse (the train that arrives). It is not merely clever. The argument that chaos is dull, because meaning requires that things could have been otherwise, is a serious claim about freedom, and Chesterton spent his career making it against the century’s drift.

There is a further, gentler point in the choice of examples. Syme praises the clerks, the Underground, Bradshaw’s guide—the unglamorous machinery of ordinary London life that the aesthetes despised. Chesterton found the miraculous in the reliable: a lamppost, a cab, a train that gets to Victoria. The novel’s subtitle calls it a nightmare, and it is; but the nightmare is what happens when a man who loves the ordinary world is made to doubt it, and the timetable he wept over is what he is fighting to get back.

Take one question away

Syme says chaos is dull and a train arriving on time is epic. Is the paradox a joke, a serious philosophy, or Chesterton’s way of making the second look like the first?

Before reading

Find the reversals.

Mark every sentence that takes a common opinion and turns it round: rare/obvious, arrow/engine, chaos/dull, Byron/Bradshaw. Chesterton’s prose is built from inversions. Count them, and ask why the trick does not grow tiring.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Argue for Gregory.

  1. Restate the anarchist’s case as strongly as you can: poetry is disorder, the clerks are sad because their trains arrive.
  2. Answer it with Syme’s train.
  3. Find the weak point in Syme’s argument (does a train that arrives really resemble an arrow?).
  4. Decide who wins, and whether the novel agrees.
Essay bridge

Order at the centre of the nightmare.

For an essay on the novel, follow Syme’s defence of order through the unmasking of the anarchists to the revelation of Sunday in the final chapters. Ask whether the book proves Syme right, complicates him, or suggests that order and chaos were the same face all along.
