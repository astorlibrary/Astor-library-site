Passage Room/Tess of the d’Urbervilles/The black flag

Close reading 26 · Tess of the d’Urbervilles

“‘Justice’ was done”

Hardy ends his novel without showing its final event. Two watchers on a hill, a flag on a tower, one sentence of terrible irony—and then the only thing left for the living to do.

Thomas HardyChapter LIX · the final page

Serialised in censored form in 1891 and published complete the same year, subtitled “A Pure Woman Faithfully Presented”. Wintoncester is Hardy’s name for Winchester.

How to read this page: notice first what the passage refuses to describe. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

TessChapter LIX

Upon the cornice of the tower a tall staff was fixed. Their eyes were riveted on it. A few minutes after the hour had struck something moved slowly up the staff, and extended itself upon the breeze. It was a black flag.1

“Justice” was done2, and the President of the Immortals, in Æschylean phrase3, had ended his sport with Tess4. And the d’Urberville knights and dames slept on in their tombs unknowing5. The two speechless gazers bent themselves down to the earth, as if in prayer, and remained thus a long time, absolutely motionless: the flag continued to wave silently. As soon as they had strength, they arose, joined hands again, and went on.6

Tess of the d’Urbervilles, Chapter LIX · public-domain text

1

News by signal

A black flag raised over a county gaol announced that an execution had taken place; crowds outside Victorian prisons watched for it after public hangings ended in 1868. Hardy gives us the death of his heroine only as this administrative signal, seen from a distance, at the scheduled hour. Four flat words—“It was a black flag”—carry the whole event, and the restraint is more devastating than any scaffold scene.

2

Punctuation as protest

The quotation marks around “Justice” are the narrator’s only open comment on the hanging. They concede that the law has been correctly applied—Tess killed Alec—while refusing the word its meaning. Everything the novel has shown—the assault, the dead child, the desertion, the trap of circumstance—is compressed into two inverted commas. It may be the most argumentative punctuation in Victorian fiction.

3

A god borrowed from Aeschylus

The phrase adapts a title of Zeus from Aeschylus’s Prometheus Bound—the play in which a god tortures a benefactor of mankind. “President” is deliberately cold: a chairman, an office-holder, presiding over immortals as over a committee. By flagging the borrowing (“in Æschylean phrase”) Hardy half-shields himself—it is a literary allusion, not a creed—yet the sentence still drew accusations of blasphemy, and he spent prefaces to later editions insisting the image was figurative.

4

“Sport”

Sport is play, game, the hunt. The word makes Tess’s whole history—coincidences, missed letters, wrong meetings—the amusement of a power that has now lost interest. Whether that power is a god, blind chance, or simply the novelist’s own plotting is left open; on any reading, the universe of the sentence is one where suffering has a spectator but not a purpose.

5

The ancestors sleep through it

The novel began with the discovery that Tess’s poor family descended from the ancient d’Urbervilles—the vanity that set every disaster moving. At the end the knights and dames “slept on… unknowing”: lineage, which cost Tess everything, notices nothing. The clause closes the novel’s longest irony with a single indifferent verb.

6

The last verbs belong to the living

Angel Clare and Liza-Lu, Tess’s young sister, are never named in the paragraph—only “the two speechless gazers”. They bow “as if in prayer” (Hardy will not confirm that prayer is what it is, or that anyone hears it), and then the ending gives them three plain actions: arose, joined hands, went on. No consolation is stated, no future promised. Continuation is all the novel will grant—and, characteristically for Hardy, it grants it walking.

What the wording changes

An ending built out of refusals.

Hardy refuses the deathbed scene, the courtroom, the scaffold—every set-piece a Victorian novelist was expected to provide. Tess’s last appearance in the novel is her arrest at Stonehenge, asleep on a stone like an offering; after that she exists only as a flag. The withholding is moral as well as artistic: the narrator, who has called her “a pure woman” on the title page, will not let her death become spectacle for the reader as it is for the law.

The famous sentence works because the rest of the paragraph is so quiet. Around the Æschylean thunderclap stand sentences of almost bureaucratic flatness—the staff, the hour, the flag, the tombs. Hardy’s irony always operates this way: enormous claims about the universe delivered once, briefly, inside prose that then returns to observed fact. Readers who complained that the “President of the Immortals” was blasphemous were, in a sense, responding accurately to its weight; Hardy’s defence—that no literal belief was intended—was also accurate. Both things are true, which is Hardy.

And the ending looks forward as well as down. Tess herself, before her arrest, asked Angel to marry Liza-Lu—“she had all the best of me without the bad of me”. The joined hands on the hill carry that strange, self-erasing bequest. Whether the gesture is hope, obedience to the dead, or one more thing done to Tess rather than by her, the novel declines to say: it simply sets the two walking, and stops.

Take one question away

The quotation marks say the law’s justice is not justice. Does the novel offer any justice of its own—or only witnesses?

Before reading

List what is missing.

Before analysing what the passage says, list what a conventional novel would show here: the trial, last words, tears, a moral. Every absence is a decision. Keep the list beside you as you read the notes.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Weigh one sentence.

  1. Copy out: “‘Justice’ was done, and the President of the Immortals, in Æschylean phrase, had ended his sport with Tess.”
  2. Remove the quotation marks, then the phrase “in Æschylean phrase”, then swap “sport” for “dealings”. Read each version.
  3. State what each removal changes about who is being blamed.
  4. Decide: is the narrator angry at God, at the law, at the reader’s expectations—or performing anger he cannot direct anywhere?
Essay bridge

The subtitle on trial.

For an essay on Hardy’s purposes, set this ending against the subtitle “A Pure Woman” and the November 1891 preface—an author openly quarrelling with his society’s moral vocabulary. Consider whether the novel proves the subtitle, or whether the subtitle instructs us how to read the novel.
