Close reading 22 · The Rime of the Ancient Mariner

“Water, water, every where”

The Mariner has shot the albatross, and the wind has died. What follows is one of poetry’s great images of stasis: a ship painted onto its own ocean, and thirst surrounded by sea.

Samuel Taylor ColeridgePart II

First published as the opening poem of Lyrical Ballads (1798). Coleridge revised it repeatedly; the wording below follows his later revised text on Project Gutenberg.

How to read this page: read the stanzas aloud—the ballad metre is doing half the work. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The RimePart II

The ship hath been suddenly becalmed.All in a hot and copper sky,
The bloody Sun, at noon,
Right up above the mast did stand,
No bigger than the Moon.1

115Day after day, day after day2,
We stuck, nor breath nor motion;
As idle as a painted ship
Upon a painted ocean.3

And the Albatross begins to be avenged.Water, water, every where4,
120And all the boards did shrink;
Water, water, every where,
Nor any drop to drink.5

The very deep did rot: O Christ!
That ever this should be!6
125Yea, slimy things did crawl with legs
Upon the slimy sea.

The Rime of the Ancient Mariner, Part II · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered as in the poem · with Coleridge’s 1817 marginal gloss

1

A sun gone wrong

Copper sky, bloody sun: the colours are metallic and feverish, and the sun—usually poetry’s giver of life—stands directly overhead, shrunk to the moon’s size. The two lights of the natural world have collapsed into each other. The ship is near the equator, but the description works less as geography than as a universe out of joint.

2

Repetition as imprisonment

The ballad stanza wants to move—four beats, three beats, a rhyme pulling forward. Coleridge jams it. “Day after day, day after day” spends a whole line arriving nowhere, and the metre itself enacts the calm: verse built for storytelling made to mark time instead.

3

The world becomes a picture

A painted ship cannot move; a painted ocean cannot lift it. The simile is doubly strange—it compares the real (in the tale) to the artificial, draining the scene’s life from inside. It also quietly describes the poem’s own condition: a made thing, holding a ship motionless in words the way a painting would in oils.

4

Abundance as torment

The most famous stanza in the poem is built on a single cruel paradox: surrounded by the thing they are dying for want of. The repetition of “water, water” is the sound of obsession—a thirsty mind circling one word—while the shrinking boards make even the ship thirst.

5

“Nor”, not “and not”

The line is almost always misquoted as “and not a drop to drink”. Coleridge wrote “Nor any drop”: tighter, more archaic, and rhythmically harder, landing the stanza on the flat impossibility. The misquotation smooths what the poet deliberately left spare—a useful small lesson in how memory edits poems.

6

Rot, and the oath

“The very deep did rot” extends the punishment to the sea itself, and the exclamation—“O Christ! / That ever this should be!”—breaks the narrating voice into prayer or horror. Note what disgusts him: “slimy things” crawling on a “slimy sea”. In Part IV the Mariner will look at these same creatures, see their beauty, and bless them—the turning point of the whole poem. His curse begins to lift at the moment his disgust does.

What the wording changes

Punishment arrives as stillness, not storm.

The Mariner’s crime was a single crossbow shot at a bird that had brought good winds. The poem’s justice is disproportionate and eerie, and this passage is where its scale first shows: not shipwreck but suspension, a crew held in heat and silence while the sea itself degenerates. Coleridge’s supernatural works through subtraction—no wind, no motion, no drink—which is why the stanzas feel closer to nightmare than to adventure.

The passage also shows what Coleridge did with the ballad, the old anonymous form of border minstrelsy that he and Wordsworth were reviving in Lyrical Ballads. Traditional ballads use repetition for momentum; Coleridge turns it into psychology. When he added the prose marginal glosses in 1817, the note beside these stanzas made the reading explicit: the albatross “begins to be avenged”. The gloss gives the event a cause; the verse itself, tellingly, does not—in the stanzas there is only heat, thirst and rot, with the logic of vengeance left for the margin.

Readers have never settled what the punishment is for, exactly—a bird, after all. That openness is the poem’s engine. What the becalming establishes beyond doubt is the shape of the moral world: a universe in which everything is connected, in which contempt for one living thing can stop the wind, and in which the cure, when it comes, will begin with looking at slimy things until they become “happy living things”.

Take one question away

Why does Coleridge make the sea’s life disgusting here, if the poem’s turning point will be learning to bless that same life?

Before reading

Feel the metre first.

Tap the ballad rhythm through one stanza: four stresses, then three. Then find every place in the passage where repetition stalls that rhythm. The poem’s meaning lives in the difference between the metre’s urge to move and the words’ refusal.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Track a transformation.

  1. List every word in the passage describing the sea and its creatures.
  2. Beside it, list the words used for the same creatures in Part IV (“happy living things”, their “rich attire”).
  3. Identify what changes between the lists: the creatures, or the seer?
  4. Write one sentence stating the poem’s moral as this comparison suggests it—then test your sentence against the shooting of the albatross.
Essay bridge

The gloss against the verse.

For an essay on the poem’s form, compare these stanzas with the marginal gloss Coleridge added in 1817. The margin explains, moralises and smooths; the verse withholds. Which voice do you trust—and what does the gap between them do to the poem’s idea of judgment?
