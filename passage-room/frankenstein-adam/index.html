Passage Room/Frankenstein/The creature’s claim

Close reading 07 · Frankenstein

“I ought to be thy Adam”

On the glacier, the creature refuses Victor’s names for him and supplies a history of his own. Two figures from Paradise Lost let him ask what a creator owes—and explain what abandonment has made of him.

Mary ShelleyChapter 10 in the 1831 text

The novel was first published in 1818 and revised for the 1831 edition. This wording follows the widely available 1831 text.

How to read this page: begin with the relationship each pronoun establishes—“I”, “thy”, “thou”. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to follow the creature’s argument.

FrankensteinMontanvert

I am thy creature1, and I will be even mild and docile to my natural lord and king if thou wilt also perform thy part, the which thou owest me2.

Oh, Frankenstein, be not equitable to every other and trample upon me alone, to whom thy justice, and even thy clemency and affection, is most due. Remember that I am thy creature; I ought to be thy Adam3, but I am rather the fallen angel4, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed. Everywhere I see bliss, from which I alone am irrevocably excluded.

I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend5. Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous.

Frankenstein, 1831 text, Chapter 10 · public-domain text

1

A relation Victor cannot erase

Victor calls him “devil”, “monster” and “insect”. “Thy creature” replaces insult with fact: Victor made the life he now wishes to disown.

2

Creation makes a duty

“Owest” turns origin into obligation. The creature offers obedience conditionally and argues that the creator–created relation binds both sides.

3

What should have been

“Ought” marks the distance between creation and care. Adam is made, recognised and placed within a world; the creature is animated and immediately abandoned.

4

Created and cast out

The fallen angel introduces Satan beside Adam. The creature imagines himself as both abandoned innocent and rebellious outcast, a comparison that carries sympathy and threat together.

5

A cause is not an acquittal

The sentence claims that suffering transformed his character. It asks us to recognise the consequences of rejection without making every later act morally weightless.

What the wording changes

The creature argues before he tells his story.

Victor first sees a hated body and reaches for destruction. The creature answers with grammar, analogy and a demand to be heard. “Thy” repeatedly returns responsibility to Victor: the being before him is not an accident from elsewhere but the work of his hands.

The Biblical allusions refuse a simple role. Adam is the first man and the cared-for creation; Satan is the proud rebel driven from joy. By claiming both positions, the creature describes an injustice and anticipates what exclusion may produce. His comparison does not settle whether he is innocent. It makes the relation between injury and agency harder to dismiss.

“Misery made me a fiend” is therefore a claim we must test against the narrative he is about to give. Shelley allows suffering to explain the growth of hatred while keeping the question of responsibility open. Victor has duties as a creator; the creature remains answerable for what he chooses to do with his pain.

Take one question away

What does the creature gain—and what does he risk—by describing himself as both Adam and the fallen angel?
