Close reading 68 · The Winter’s Tale

“O, she’s warm!”

Sixteen years after his jealousy killed his wife, a king is shown her statue. It moves. The scene is Shakespeare’s most audacious ending, and it works by asking the audience to do exactly what the king is asked to do: awake their faith.

William ShakespeareAct 5, Scene 3

Probably written around 1610–11. Paulina, who has kept Hermione hidden for sixteen years, presents her to Leontes as a statue. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: notice how carefully Paulina builds permission before anything moves. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Winter’s Tale5.3

PaulinaIt is requir’d
You do awake your faith.1 Then all stand still;
Or those that think it is unlawful business
I am about, let them depart.

LeontesProceed:
No foot shall stir.

PaulinaMusic, awake her: strike! Music.’Tis time; descend; be stone no more; approach;
Strike all that look upon with marvel.2 Come;
I’ll fill your grave up3: stir; nay, come away.
Bequeath to death your numbness, for from him
Dear life redeems you. You perceive she stirs.

Hermione comes down from the pedestal.

PaulinaStart not; her actions shall be holy as
You hear my spell is lawful. Do not shun her
Until you see her die again; for then
You kill her double. Nay, present your hand:
When she was young you woo’d her; now in age
Is she become the suitor?4

LeontesEmbracing her.O, she’s warm!
If this be magic, let it be an art
Lawful as eating.5

PaulinaThat she is living,
Were it but told you, should be hooted at
Like an old tale6; but it appears she lives,
Though yet she speak not.

The Winter’s Tale, Act 5, Scene 3 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · Polixenes and Camillo’s interjections omitted

1

The condition

“It is requir’d / You do awake your faith.” Before the miracle, a demand: those who cannot believe should leave. Paulina addresses Leontes, and Shakespeare addresses the audience. The statue scene breaks every rule the play has set up so far, since Hermione’s death was reported and mourned sixteen years ago, and the line asks us to accept it as an act of faith. Sceptics may depart. No one does.

2

The spell

Paulina’s command is a string of imperatives—descend, be stone no more, approach, strike with marvel—spoken over music. It sounds like conjuration, and she has just insisted her business is not “unlawful”. The scene walks a careful line: in 1611 a resurrection by magic risked the charge of witchcraft, so Paulina insists her spell is lawful while the stage performs a wonder. The ambiguity is deliberate and it is the source of the awe.

3

“I’ll fill your grave up”

A strange, physical promise: the grave Hermione supposedly occupies will be filled in, as if death were a hole that can be backfilled once its tenant leaves. “Bequeath to death your numbness”—give death the stillness, keep the life. Paulina treats death as a bad bargain that can be renegotiated, and the whole late-romance mode of the play is in that audacity.

4

The wooing reversed

Leontes must offer his hand, and Paulina frames it as courtship turned around: he wooed her young; now, “in age”, she is the suitor, descending to him. The line admits the sixteen years: Hermione is not restored to youth, and the statue shows her older. The reunion is between two people who have lost a third of their lives, and the play does not pretend otherwise.

5

Warmth

The proof is tactile and monosyllabic: “O, she’s warm!” After all the talk of stone and chisel, Leontes touches her and finds body heat. Then the play’s theology of wonder in two lines: if this is magic, let it be “an art / Lawful as eating”—as natural, as necessary, as blameless. The comparison to eating brings the miracle down to the level of daily survival, which is exactly where the play wants it.

6

Like an old tale

Paulina says the truth—that Hermione lives—would be “hooted at / Like an old tale” if merely reported. The play’s title is The Winter’s Tale: an old wives’ story told by the fire. Shakespeare names the improbability, hands it to a character to voice, and then stages it anyway, trusting that what cannot be told can be shown. “It appears she lives”—the verb is appears. Faith is asked for, not proof.

What the wording changes

A resurrection staged as theatre, and defended as theatre.

Nothing prepares the audience for Hermione’s return. In Act 3 her death is reported by Paulina herself, with such conviction that Leontes spends sixteen years in penance; the play’s source, Greene’s Pandosto, keeps the queen dead. Shakespeare’s alteration is his boldest ending, and he protects it with the machinery of this scene: a chapel, a curtain, a keeper, a demand for faith, music, and a slow, permitted descent. The statue is a device for making the audience believe something they were told was impossible—and the device is theatre itself, openly displayed. Paulina is a director; Leontes and the audience are asked to watch and not stir.

The scene’s vocabulary keeps touching the forbidden. “Spell”, “magic”, “wicked powers”, “unlawful business”—Paulina names the accusation of witchcraft before anyone can make it, and insists on lawfulness at every step. In a country where King James had written a treatise on demonology, the care was necessary. But the play wants more than a legal defence. Leontes’ “art / Lawful as eating” claims for the theatre’s wonder the status of ordinary nourishment, and Paulina’s “old tale” claims for the improbable the truth of showing over telling. The scene is an argument about what art may do, made by doing it.

What it cannot do is give back what was lost. Mamillius, the son, is dead and stays dead; Antigonus was eaten by a bear; sixteen years are gone, and Hermione returns aged, silent to her husband, speaking only to bless her daughter. The reunion is real and it is partial, and Shakespeare’s late romances specialise in exactly this—grace that arrives after the damage, not instead of it. “O, she’s warm!” is the sound of a man receiving more than he deserved and less than he destroyed, and the play ends a few lines later without pretending the two are equal.

Take one question away

Paulina asks Leontes to awake his faith, and Shakespeare asks the same of us. Is the statue scene a miracle, a trick, or a demonstration that in the theatre there is no difference?

Before reading

Track the permissions.

Mark every moment Paulina asks for consent or offers an exit: “awake your faith”, “let them depart”, “my spell is lawful”. The miracle is negotiated before it happens. Ask why.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Stage the descent.

  1. Decide how long Hermione stands still before she moves, and what the audience should feel in the wait.
  2. Choose whether Leontes touches her hesitantly or seizes her.
  3. Decide whether Hermione should look at Leontes at all before Perdita.
  4. Discuss what each choice says about forgiveness.
Essay bridge

The tale and the source.

For an essay on the ending, compare the statue scene with the close of Greene’s Pandosto, where the queen remains dead and the king kills himself. Ask what Shakespeare gains by the resurrection, what he refuses to restore, and why he lets Paulina call the truth “an old tale” in the very scene that proves it.
