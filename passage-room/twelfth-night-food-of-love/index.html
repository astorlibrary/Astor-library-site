Close reading 31 · Twelfth Night

“If music be the food of love”

The play opens with a duke, an orchestra and a theory of desire. Within four lines Orsino has tested the theory on himself—and proved it, in the least flattering way possible.

William ShakespeareAct 1, Scene 1

Probably written around 1601–02; a performance was recorded at the Middle Temple in February 1602. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: remember that musicians are on stage, obeying him. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Twelfth Night1.1

DUKEIf music be the food of love, play on,
Give me excess of it; that, surfeiting,
The appetite may sicken and so die.1
That strain again, it had a dying fall2;
O, it came o’er my ear like the sweet sound
That breathes upon a bank of violets,
Stealing and giving odour.3 Enough; no more;
’Tis not so sweet now as it was before.4
O spirit of love, how quick and fresh art thou,
That notwithstanding thy capacity
Receiveth as the sea, nought enters there,
Of what validity and pitch soever,
But falls into abatement and low price5
Even in a minute! So full of shapes is fancy,
That it alone is high fantastical.6

Twelfth Night, Act 1, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

A cure by overdose

The famous first line is a conditional, and the request is stranger than its fame suggests. Orsino does not ask for music to feed his love but to kill it: gorge the appetite until it sickens. He wants to be free of a feeling he is also luxuriating in—the speech’s whole comedy and pathos are in that contradiction, announced before the play is three lines old.

2

The dying fall

A “fall” is a cadence—the phrase of melody that sinks to its close—and “dying” makes the technical term languish. Orsino asks for one strain to be repeated: he is curating his own melancholy, replaying the saddest bar. Fashionable Elizabethan gentlemen cultivated love-melancholy as a mark of refinement; Shakespeare gives the fashion an orchestra.

3

Sound becomes scent

The simile slides senses together: music arrives at the ear the way a breeze crosses violets, “stealing and giving odour”—taking scent from the flowers and delivering it onward. It is genuinely lovely, and deliberately excessive; the line luxuriates exactly as its speaker does. Even the verbs are double: the sweetness is stolen and given, which is not a bad description of how Orsino loves.

4

The theory proves itself

Four lines after demanding excess, he has had too much: “Enough; no more”. The repeated strain is already staler than it was. His own prescription—surfeit kills appetite—has worked instantly, just not on love; only on the tune. The moment is easy to miss and worth pausing on, because it is the play’s first demonstration that Orsino’s feelings are weather, changing by the minute while announcing themselves as eternity.

5

Love as a devouring sea

The spirit of love has the sea’s capacity: everything that enters it—whatever its “validity and pitch”, its worth and height—falls “into abatement and low price / Even in a minute”. The vocabulary is a merchant’s: value, price, depreciation. Orsino means it as praise of love’s vastness, but what he actually describes is a solvent in which everything loses value the moment he possesses it—an exact forecast of how the play will treat his passion for Olivia.

6

“Fancy” names the theme

‘Fancy’ is love and imagination together, the faculty that makes ‘shapes’, images of the thing desired. ‘High fantastical’ means imaginative beyond anything else. Orsino intends a compliment to love; the play will spend five acts asking whether he loves Olivia or the shapes his fancy has made of her—a woman he barely sees, who has refused him, and whom he woos entirely by messenger.

What the wording changes

A lover in love with the loving.

Nothing happens in this scene: that is its point. Orsino reclines, commands music, revises his commands, and converts every sensation into another image of his own condition. When his messenger returns from Olivia’s house with a refusal—she intends to veil herself for seven years to mourn her brother—Orsino hears even that as a compliment to himself: a woman who loves a dead brother so well will love a living duke wonderfully. The opening establishes a court where feeling is performed, savoured and never tested against another person.

The play then builds its plot as the test. Viola arrives from the sea—the real sea, which has apparently drowned her brother, not the metaphorical sea of Orsino’s appetite—and enters his service disguised as a boy. Her love for Orsino is everything his speech is not: unspoken, patient and directed at an actual, daily-observed person. When the two finally discuss love in 2.4, Shakespeare sets the two styles against each other openly—Orsino generalising grandly about men’s and women’s passions and contradicting himself between sentences, Viola answering with the history of a love that “never told” itself.

The speech also opens the play’s frame of music. Illyria is the most musical of Shakespeare’s comedy worlds—Feste sings for every household—and the final word of the play is a song. But where Orsino uses music as an appetite’s sauce, the songs themselves keep telling plainer truths: present mirth, coming death, the rain that raineth every day. The play indulges Orsino’s fantasy and surrounds it with clearer voices, which is Twelfth Night’s way with every kind of self-delusion it stages.

Take one question away

Orsino asks music to cure him of love. Does anything in the speech suggest he wants the cure to work?

Before reading

Direct the musicians.

Mark every order Orsino gives the players: play on, that strain again, enough, no more. The speech is also a scene—musicians starting, repeating and stopping. Decide how they might exchange glances by the fourth command.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Audit the imagery.

  1. List the image fields: food and appetite, music, flowers and scent, the sea, commerce.
  2. For each, note what it implies love does—nourishes, sickens, sweetens, swallows, devalues.
  3. Decide which single image Orsino would be most alarmed to hear read back to him.
  4. Write the one-sentence reply Olivia might send if she heard this speech.
Essay bridge

Two accounts of love in 2.4.

For an essay on love in the play, put this speech beside the scene where Orsino and the disguised Viola compare men’s and women’s constancy—he claiming no woman’s heart could hold his passion, she answering with “Patience on a monument”. Track which speaker generalises and which testifies, and what the difference costs each of them.
