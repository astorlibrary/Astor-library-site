Close reading 03 · Macbeth

“Tomorrow, and tomorrow”

Lady Macbeth is dead and an army is approaching. Macbeth answers the news by turning life into a series of figures—candle, shadow, actor, tale—and taking the substance from each one.

William ShakespeareAct 5, Scene 5

Probably written around 1606. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text and has been checked against the Folger text.

How to read this page: read the speech once without stopping. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Macbeth5.5

She should have died hereafter1.
There would have been a time for such a word.
Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow2,
Creeps in this petty pace from day to day,
To the last syllable of recorded time3;
And all our yesterdays have lighted fools
The way to dusty death. Out, out, brief candle4!

Life’s but a walking shadow; a poor player,
That struts and frets his hour upon the stage5,
And then is heard no more: it is a tale
Told by an idiot, full of sound and fury,
Signifying nothing.

Macbeth, Act 5, Scene 5 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

A death put in another time

“Hereafter” may mean later, or simply the time that follows. The line can sound callous, stunned or helpless. Macbeth does not say that Lady Macbeth should not have died; he imagines a different time in which the news might have found an adequate response.

2

Repetition without arrival

The same word is renewed twice without bringing a different future. Its long opening syllable can be stretched in performance, while “creeps” makes time move with painfully small, almost bodily effort.

3

Time becomes language

A syllable is a unit of speech, and “recorded” turns history into something written or remembered. Macbeth imagines time as a script uttered piece by piece until there is nothing left to say.

4

An order that can only extinguish

The repeated imperative gives Macbeth one last command, but its object is a life already described as brief. The candle has illuminated the fools’ path only towards death; putting it out ends both light and duration.

5

The actor loses the audience

The image is openly theatrical. A player fills an hour with restless movement, then disappears into silence. “Poor” reduces the grandeur of performance, while “his hour” makes the apparent intensity temporary from the start.

What the wording changes

Each comparison gives life a form, then empties it

The speech moves through time before it moves through metaphor. “Hereafter”, “Tomorrow” and “yesterdays” divide experience into tenses, but none offers a living present. Future days repeat, past days merely show fools the route to death, and the present is occupied by a word for which Macbeth says there should have been another time.

Candle, shadow, player and tale then give life four possible forms. Each is withdrawn. A candle is brief and ordered out; a shadow lacks substance; an actor is heard only for an hour; a tale has sound but, in Macbeth’s account, no significance. The figures do not build towards explanation. They successively deny light, body, permanence and meaning.

It matters that Macbeth speaks these lines. The tragedy need not endorse his conclusion as a universal truth. A man who tried to force the future into one promised shape now imagines all futurity as repetition. A messenger enters immediately afterwards with a story Macbeth first struggles to believe: Birnam Wood appears to move, and the equivocal promise on which he relied acquires its fatal sense.

Take one question away

Has Macbeth discovered that life means nothing, or made all life resemble the future his own choices have destroyed?

Before reading

Sort the image fields

Underline words of time. Circle words connected with theatre or storytelling. Box light and darkness. Read the speech aloud once at a steady pace, then decide which line most resists that pace.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

What does each image take away?

  1. Assign candle, shadow, player or tale to each group.
  2. State what the image first gives to life.
  3. Find the word that removes or diminishes it.
  4. Arrange the four images from least to most despairing.
  5. Defend the order, then test it against another group’s.
In an essay

Place the ending beside the beginning

For an essay on time, ambition or tragedy, set this speech beside “the future in the instant” in 1.5 or Macbeth’s wish that the murder might be the “be-all and the end-all” in 1.7. The change is from a future imagined as possession to time imagined as empty repetition.
