Close reading 33 · Richard II

“this sceptred isle”

The most quoted hymn to England in the language is not a hymn. It is the front half of an accusation—twenty lines of glory suspended in mid-sentence, waiting for a verb that arrives like a verdict.

William ShakespeareAct 2, Scene 1

Probably written around 1595. John of Gaunt, dying, speaks of the England his nephew King Richard governs. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read to the end before judging the tone—the sentence’s grammar is its argument. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Richard II2.1

GAUNTMethinks I am a prophet new inspired1,
And thus expiring do foretell of him:
His rash fierce blaze of riot cannot last,
For violent fires soon burn out themselves;
Small showers last long, but sudden storms are short;

This royal throne of kings, this sceptered isle2,
This earth of majesty, this seat of Mars,
This other Eden, demi-paradise3,
This fortress built by Nature for herself
Against infection and the hand of war,
This happy breed of men, this little world,
This precious stone set in the silver sea4,
Which serves it in the office of a wall
Or as a moat defensive to a house,
Against the envy of less happier lands;
This blessed plot, this earth, this realm, this England,
This nurse, this teeming womb of royal kings,
…
This land of such dear souls, this dear dear land,
Dear for her reputation through the world,
Is now leased out—I die pronouncing it—
Like to a tenement or pelting farm.5
England, bound in with the triumphant sea,
Whose rocky shore beats back the envious siege
Of wat’ry Neptune, is now bound in with shame,
With inky blots and rotten parchment bonds6;
That England that was wont to conquer others
Hath made a shameful conquest of itself.

Richard II, Act 2, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · four lines omitted at the ellipsis

1

A dying man’s licence

Elizabethans widely held that the dying spoke with prophetic truth—Gaunt says so himself moments earlier: the tongues of dying men enforce attention. “New inspired” and “expiring” share a root: breath. His last breath is claimed as inspiration, which licenses what no subject could otherwise safely say about a king.

2

“This… this… this”

The engine of the speech is anaphora—the same demonstrative hammered at the head of phrase after phrase, more than a dozen times. Each “this” holds England up again at a slightly different angle: throne, earth, seat, fortress, breed, world, stone, plot, realm, nurse, womb. Grammatically, every one of them is a subject waiting for its verb—and the verb is withheld for twenty lines. The suspense is the meaning.

3

Eden, at second hand

“Other Eden” and “demi-paradise” both qualify themselves: a second Eden, half a paradise. Even at the height of praise the language remembers that the first Eden was lost through misgovernment of a garden. The play returns to the image later, when Richard’s gardeners compare the kingdom to a garden “full of weeds” for want of tending—Gaunt’s metaphor made into a whole scene.

4

Jewel, wall, moat

The most beautiful line in the speech sets England as a gem in silver—then immediately converts the setting into defensive architecture: the sea as wall and moat against “the envy of less happier lands”. Beauty and siege-mentality in one image. The islanded self-sufficiency Gaunt praises is precisely what makes the coming betrayal internal: no invader is needed where the king himself signs the damage.

5

The verb at last

After the long suspension, the predicate lands: “Is now leased out… Like to a tenement or pelting farm.” A tenement is rented property; “pelting” means paltry. Richard has been funding his court by leasing the royal revenues—farming out England—and Gaunt’s sentence enacts the fall: twenty lines of majesty crash into landlord’s vocabulary. The interruption—“I die pronouncing it”—stamps the verdict with his death.

6

Conquered by paperwork

The England that repelled Neptune is “bound in with shame, / With inky blots and rotten parchment bonds”—defeated not by armies but by legal documents, the leases and bonds Richard has signed. The closing paradox seals it: the conqueror of others has made “a shameful conquest of itself”. In a play obsessed with the difference between the king’s two bodies—the office and the man—the wound to the realm is a signature.

What the wording changes

The praise exists to price the betrayal.

Quotation has reversed this speech’s meaning. Detached from its sentence, the catalogue—sceptred isle, demi-paradise, precious stone—has served for four centuries as straightforward patriotism, recited in wars and printed on tea-towels. In the play, every glory is accumulated for one purpose: to establish the value of what Richard has cheapened. The higher the pile, the harder the fall onto “tenement or pelting farm”. Reading the speech whole restores its structure: it is an audit, not an anthem.

The politics are pointed. Gaunt’s complaint is not that Richard is cruel but that he has confused owning with governing—treated the realm as revenue. “Landlord of England art thou now, not king”, he tells Richard to his face a few lines later. The play then shows the consequence: Gaunt dies, Richard seizes his estates to fund the Irish wars, and that seizure—property law broken by the crown itself—gives Bolingbroke both grievance and pretext. The deposition that follows begins here, in an argument about leases.

Shakespeare wrote the play about a king deposed two centuries earlier, but the subject stayed dangerous: the deposition scene was left out of printed editions during Elizabeth’s reign, and supporters of the Earl of Essex famously commissioned a performance of the play on the eve of his rising in 1601. Gaunt’s speech shows why the material never went safely to sleep—it teaches an audience to distinguish the country from the current occupant of its throne, and to love the first while auditing the second.

Take one question away

Does loving the sceptred isle and indicting its king make Gaunt a patriot, a rebel—or does the speech show those can be the same thing?

Before reading

Hunt the verb.

Read the long sentence with one task: find its main verb. Note the line where you expected it, and the line where it arrives. Everything between is held breath—count the “this” phrases suspended on the way.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

The tea-towel test.

  1. Draft the version of this speech that gets quoted—stop where the patriotic anthology stops.
  2. Mark exactly which lines the anthology must cut for the tone to survive.
  3. State in one sentence what the full sentence says that the excerpt cannot.
  4. Discuss: is quoting the first half dishonest, or has the excerpt become a legitimate poem of its own?
