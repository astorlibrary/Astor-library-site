Close reading 42 · The War of the Worlds

“intellects vast and cool and unsympathetic”

The most influential opening in science fiction does one thing above all: it reverses the direction of looking. Mankind, accustomed to the microscope’s eyepiece, is placed on the slide.

H. G. WellsBook One, Chapter 1 · The Eve of the War

Serialised in 1897 and published as a book in 1898. The narrator writes some years after the invasion, from a world that survived it. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: notice who is watching whom in every sentence. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The War of the WorldsI.1

No one would have believed in the last years of the nineteenth century1 that this world was being watched keenly and closely by intelligences greater than man’s and yet as mortal as his own; that as men busied themselves about their various concerns they were scrutinised and studied, perhaps almost as narrowly as a man with a microscope might scrutinise the transient creatures that swarm and multiply in a drop of water2. With infinite complacency men went to and fro over this globe about their little affairs, serene in their assurance of their empire over matter.3 It is possible that the infusoria under the microscope do the same. No one gave a thought to the older worlds of space as sources of human danger, or thought of them only to dismiss the idea of life upon them as impossible or improbable. It is curious to recall some of the mental habits of those departed days. At most terrestrial men fancied there might be other men upon Mars, perhaps inferior to themselves and ready to welcome a missionary enterprise. Yet across the gulf of space, minds that are to our minds as ours are to those of the beasts that perish4, intellects vast and cool and unsympathetic, regarded this earth with envious eyes, and slowly and surely drew their plans against us5. And early in the twentieth century came the great disillusionment.6

The War of the Worlds, Book One, Chapter 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

Written from afterwards

“No one would have believed” is retrospective: the narrator speaks from a time when everyone, now, believes—because it happened. The opening installs survivor’s knowledge in the first five words, and its subject is not Martians but belief: the whole first paragraph is about what late-Victorian minds could and could not imagine. The disaster begins as a failure of imagination.

2

Mankind on the slide

The microscope was the era’s emblem of scientific mastery—the instrument through which man scrutinised “transient creatures” in a water-drop. Wells turns it around in a single simile: we are the creatures, swarming and multiplying, and something is at the eyepiece. The word “transient” does quiet work—seen from above, human lifespans look like the infusoria’s afternoon.

3

“Empire over matter”

The sentence is built to deflate: infinite complacency, little affairs, serene assurance—and then the giveaway phrase, “their empire over matter”. Wells wrote at the height of British imperial confidence, and the word is chosen to sting. The next sentence completes the demotion: “It is possible that the infusoria under the microscope do the same.” Perhaps the microbes are complacent too.

4

The ladder extended

The comparison is a proportion: Martian minds are to ours as ours are to “the beasts that perish”—a phrase lifted from the Psalms, where it humbles human pride. Victorian science had placed man atop a ladder of being; Wells simply adds a rung above him and lets the existing logic do the rest. Everything mankind had told itself about its right to use lesser creatures now applies downward—to mankind.

5

Vast, cool, unsympathetic

The tricolon is the most quoted phrase in scientific romance, and its middle term is the masterstroke. “Vast” awes; “unsympathetic” chills; but “cool” is worse than either—not hatred, just temperature. These minds are what the age claimed to admire: pure detached intelligence, science without sentiment. The verbs that follow are patient—regarded, drew their plans, “slowly and surely”—the rhythm of an experiment being prepared, with us as the culture dish.

6

The flattest sentence in the chapter

After the long, clause-stacked Victorian sentences, seven words: “And early in the twentieth century came the great disillusionment.” The understatement is surgical—“disillusionment” for planetary invasion, as though the loss were merely of an idea. It was: the idea of security. The paragraph’s subject was complacency, and its last word names what complacency turns into when the watching stops being theoretical.

What the wording changes

The invasion is aimed at a state of mind.

Wells’s opening attacks before the Martians do, and its target is the reader’s own assurance. Every device—the reversed microscope, the extended ladder of minds, the empire vocabulary—takes a habit of late-Victorian self-congratulation and turns it into the mechanism of doom. The famous complacency is not a character flaw the story punishes incidentally; it is the very thing the Martians exploit, and the very thing the prose dismantles in the reader, sentence by sentence, before a single cylinder falls.

A page later, Wells makes the political point explicit. Before we judge the Martians too harshly, the narrator says, we should remember “what ruthless and utter destruction our own species” has inflicted—the Tasmanians, “in spite of their human likeness”, swept out of existence by European immigrants in fifty years: “Are we such apostles of mercy as to complain if the Martians warred in the same spirit?” The invasion novel, invented here in its modern form, arrives with its moral already installed: the Martians are the British Empire with better equipment, and Surrey is experiencing colonisation from the receiving end.

The ending completes the opening’s logic rather than escaping it. Humanity does not defeat the Martians; the “transient creatures” of the first paragraph do—the invaders are destroyed by earthly bacteria, “the humblest things” upon the planet. The hierarchy of intellects, so carefully erected in the opening, is overturned from below: the microscope’s own subjects finish the war. It is the book’s neatest symmetry, and its most Darwinian—survival goes not to the vast and cool, but to the adapted.

Take one question away

Wells makes the Martians everything his age admired—intellect without sympathy. Is the book a warning about aliens, or about the direction science was already facing?

Before reading

Mark the watchers.

In each sentence, identify who watches and who is watched: Martians and men, man and microbe, narrator and “those departed days”. The paragraph is a tower of observation with mankind in the middle—sketch the tower.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Rebuild the first sentence.

  1. Break the long opening sentence into its clauses and list them in order.
  2. Rewrite it as three plain sentences. What urgency and irony drain away?
  3. Find where Wells places “as mortal as his own”—why plant the Martians’ weakness in the first sentence?
  4. Compare the paragraph’s last sentence with its first: measure what the prose has done to “belief” between them.
Essay bridge

The Tasmania paragraph.

For an essay on the novel and empire, set this opening beside the narrator’s comparison to the destruction of the Tasmanians a page later, and beside the curate and artilleryman episodes—two failed responses to becoming the colonised. Ask what the novel offers in place of complacency: humility, adaptation, or only the luck of the bacteria.
