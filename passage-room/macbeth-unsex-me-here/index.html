“Unsex me here”

Lady Macbeth has just heard that Duncan will sleep beneath her roof. Her response is not a statement of confidence but a sequence of appeals: to spirits, body, night and concealment.

William ShakespeareAct 1, Scene 5

Probably written around 1606. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text and has been checked against the Folger text.

How to read this page: read the speech once without stopping. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Macbeth1.5

The raven himself is hoarse
That croaks the fatal entrance of Duncan
Under my battlements. Come, you spirits
That tend on mortal thoughts1, unsex me here2,
And fill me, from the crown to the toe, top-full
Of direst cruelty! make thick my blood3,
Stop up th’ access and passage to remorse,
That no compunctious visitings of nature
Shake my fell purpose, nor keep peace between
Th’ effect and it!

Come to my woman’s breasts,
And take my milk for gall4, your murd’ring ministers,
Wherever in your sightless substances
You wait on nature’s mischief! Come, thick night,
And pall thee in the dunnest smoke of hell
That my keen knife see not the wound it makes,
Nor heaven peep through the blanket of the dark5
To cry, “Hold, hold!”

Macbeth, Act 1, Scene 5 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

Death enters the thought

“Mortal” can mean human, but here it also carries the force of death-dealing. Lady Macbeth does not merely ask to stop feeling. She summons agents associated with the murderous thought she wants to complete.

2

Not simply “make me a man”

The command asks for sexed identity and bodily association to be stripped away or remade. The rest of the speech specifies what she rejects: milk, remorse and the “visitings” of a nature she believes may interrupt her purpose.

3

Conscience as circulation

The imperative turns resolve into a physical alteration. Blood must be thickened and its passages stopped, as though pity could travel through the body and reach the intention before it becomes an act.

4

An exchange, not an absence

Milk, associated here with nurture, is not simply removed. It is traded for gall, a bitter substance. Lady Macbeth imagines cruelty as a replacement that can be poured into the body she already has.

5

Darkness becomes a covering

The domestic “blanket” is made to hide a wound from heaven. Even the knife is given sight. Lady Macbeth wants not only to escape discovery but to prevent the act, its instrument and any moral witness from seeing one another.

What the wording changes

Power begins as a request for alteration

The speech can sound commanding because its verbs are imperatives. Yet every command is addressed to something beyond Lady Macbeth. She does not announce that pity is absent; she asks for the route to remorse to be closed. She does not claim that the body is already cruel; she imagines it filled, thickened and converted.

That tension matters. Her will appears fierce, but it depends upon a body she treats as liable to resist. The images move from crown and blood to breasts and milk, then outward to knife, night and heaven. Private resolve expands into an attempt to reorganise the whole field of the murder so that no warning can pass between thought and effect.

The gendered language is therefore exact and unstable. Lady Macbeth uses contemporary associations between womanhood, maternity and pity in order to reject them. The play does not confirm that those qualities naturally belong to women: it shows a speaker treating them as obstacles and later using a similarly narrow idea of manhood to control Macbeth.

Take one question away

If Lady Macbeth already possesses the cruelty she claims to need, why must so much of the speech be a plea?

Before reading

Mark before you interpret

Circle every command. Underline the parts of the body. Draw a box around anything connected with sight or concealment. Which group of words appears first, and which has the final word?

Classroom task · 12 minutes

Build and test one claim

  1. In three groups, take body, conscience or darkness.
  2. Choose the two words that make your group’s image most forceful.
  3. Write one sentence explaining what Lady Macbeth wants to control.
  4. Now challenge it: what does the need to ask the spirits reveal about the limit of that control?
In an essay

Move from phrase to pattern

For a question about gender or power, place “unsex me here” beside Lady Macbeth’s attack on Macbeth’s manhood in 1.7 and her body’s repeated hand-washing in 5.1. An argument can then trace not a fixed “strong woman”, but changing attempts to command bodies, roles and action.
