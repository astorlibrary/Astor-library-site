Close reading 85 · The Comedy of Errors

“I to the world am like a drop of water”

Shakespeare’s shortest play is a farce of twins and mistaken identity. Before the mistakes begin, one twin stands alone in a strange city and says what it feels like to be looking for a self you have lost. The farce does not entirely forget it.

William ShakespeareAct 1, Scene 2

Probably written around 1594. Antipholus of Syracuse has arrived in Ephesus searching for the twin brother and mother lost in a shipwreck years before. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read the seven lines slowly—they are the still point before the play starts running. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Comedy of Errors1.2

Antipholus of SyracuseHe that commends me to mine own content
Commends me to the thing I cannot get.1
I to the world am like a drop of water
That in the ocean seeks another drop2,
5Who, failing there to find his fellow forth,
Unseen, inquisitive, confounds himself.3
So I, to find a mother and a brother,
In quest of them, unhappy, lose myself.4

Enter Dromio of Ephesus.

Here comes the almanac of my true date.5
What now? How chance thou art return’d so soon?

The Comedy of Errors, Act 1, Scene 2 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered within the extract

1

Content he cannot get

A merchant has just wished him well—“I commend you to your own content”—and Antipholus turns the pleasantry over: to be commended to his own content is to be sent to the one thing he cannot reach. The word “content” means both happiness and what is contained; he has neither. The soliloquy opens by taking a polite formula literally, which is what the whole play will do with names and faces.

2

The drop and the ocean

The image is one of the most beautiful in early Shakespeare: a drop of water falls into the sea to find another drop, and cannot, because the moment it enters the ocean it is no longer distinguishable. The search for a lost twin is figured as a search that destroys the searcher’s identity. Antipholus is looking for someone exactly like himself, and the looking dissolves the self that looks.

3

Unseen, inquisitive, confounds himself

Three words for the drop’s fate: it cannot be seen, it goes on searching, and it ‘confounds’ itself, mixes itself into what it searches and is lost. The verb will echo through the play, where confusion is the plot’s engine. But here it is quiet and metaphysical: to seek your double is to lose your singleness.

4

Lose myself

The application: seeking a mother and a brother, “I… lose myself”. In the next scene he will be mistaken for his brother by his brother’s wife, servant and creditors, and the loss of self will become a farce of the wrong man being greeted, fed and arrested. The soliloquy predicts the plot as an inner condition before the plot makes it an outer one. The joke of mistaken identity has, for seven lines, a soul.

5

The almanac of my true date

Dromio enters—the wrong Dromio, though Antipholus does not know it—and is greeted as “the almanac of my true date”: his servant, born the same hour, is the calendar that proves when he was born. The line is affectionate and, in context, comic: the man who has just described losing himself now hails a stranger as the record of his identity. The errors begin here, one line after the meditation ends.

What the wording changes

A farce with a metaphysical opening.

The Comedy of Errors is Shakespeare’s shortest and most mechanical play—two sets of identical twins, separated at birth, loose in the same city for one day. The plot is borrowed from Plautus and doubled, and it runs like clockwork: mistaken greetings, misdelivered gold, a wife who dines with the wrong husband, a merchant arrested for a debt he never incurred. Yet Shakespeare gave the machinery this quiet passage near its start, and it changes how the machine feels. The drop of water is not a joke. It is a young man saying that the search for his family has made him unsure he still exists.

The image of the drop in the ocean recurs in the play—Adriana, the wife, uses it of marriage in Act 2, saying she and her husband are “undividable” as a drop in a gulf—and it links the play’s farce to its theme. Identity in Ephesus is a matter of being recognised: the twins are who others take them for, and the errors show how much of a self is held by other people. Antipholus’s fear that he will “confound himself” is literally what the day does to him. He is fed by a wife who is not his, given a chain he did not order, and finally declared mad. The comedy is that none of it is his fault; the sadness is that the soliloquy already knew.

Shakespeare would return to twins, shipwrecks and a lost sibling in Twelfth Night, and to the drop and the ocean whenever he needed an image for the self dissolving into something larger. Here, at the start of his career, the image is already complete. The play resolves its errors with an abbess, a reunion and a pair of Dromios walking off hand in hand; the drop finds its fellow drop. But the seven lines stand, as a reminder that the farce began from a real fear, and that finding your double also means finding how easily you could be replaced.

Take one question away

Antipholus says seeking his twin makes him lose himself. When the twins are finally reunited, does the play answer that fear, or simply stop asking it?

Before reading

Draw the image.

Sketch the drop of water entering the ocean and searching for another drop. Then explain why the search must fail. The picture is the play’s theme; the plot is the picture staged as farce.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Sad line, comic scene.

  1. Read the soliloquy alone. Note its tone.
  2. Read on to Dromio’s entrance and the first error. Note how the tone changes.
  3. Ask whether the sad opening deepens the comedy or interrupts it.
  4. Find the moment in Act 2 when Adriana reuses the drop-of-water image, and compare.
Essay bridge

Twins and selves.

For an essay on identity in the comedies, set this speech beside Viola’s arrival in Illyria in Twelfth Night and Sebastian’s later wonder at being mistaken. Both plays build farce on lost twins; ask what each says about how much of a self is given by being recognised.
