Passage Room/The Haunted Man/Keep my memory green

Close reading 78 · The Haunted Man and the Ghost’s Bargain

“Lord keep my Memory green.”

A chemist who bargained away his painful memories—and found he had lost his pity with them—sits in firelight with everything restored. Dickens ends his last Christmas book without saying whether any of it happened, and lets a portrait speak instead.

Charles DickensThe ending

Published in December 1848, the fifth and last of Dickens’s Christmas books. Redlaw, the haunted chemist, accepted a phantom’s gift of forgetting sorrow and wrong, and spread the gift like a contagion until Milly Swidger’s goodness undid it. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: notice what the narrator will not confirm, and what he lets the room confirm for him. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Haunted ManThe ending

Some people have said since, that he only thought what has been herein set down; others, that he read it in the fire, one winter night about the twilight time; others, that the Ghost was but the representation of his gloomy thoughts, and Milly the embodiment of his better wisdom. I say nothing.1

—Except this. That as they were assembled in the old Hall, by no other light than that of a great fire (having dined early), the shadows once more stole out of their hiding-places, and danced about the room2, showing the children marvellous shapes and faces on the walls, and gradually changing what was real and familiar there, to what was wild and magical. But that there was one thing in the Hall, to which the eyes of Redlaw, and of Milly and her husband, and of the old man, and of the student, and his bride that was to be, were often turned, which the shadows did not obscure or change.3 Deepened in its gravity by the fire-light, and gazing from the darkness of the panelled wall like life, the sedate face in the portrait, with the beard and ruff, looked down at them from under its verdant wreath of holly4, as they looked up at it; and, clear and plain below, as if a voice had uttered them, were the words.

Lord keep my Memory green.5

The Haunted Man and the Ghost’s Bargain, the ending · public-domain text

1

The narrator declines

Three rationalising explanations are offered—he only thought it, he read it in the fire, the Ghost was his gloom and Milly his conscience—and the narrator sets them out fairly and then refuses all of them: “I say nothing.” The italic “I” is the storyteller stepping forward to step back. Dickens, who had staged the supernatural with total conviction in A Christmas Carol, ends his last Christmas book by declining to certify it. The refusal is the ending’s tact.

2

The shadows return

“—Except this.” The narrator will vouch for one scene: a hall, a fire, and shadows that “stole out of their hiding-places” to dance on the walls. The shadows are the story’s recurring image—the phantom was Redlaw’s own shadow—and here they are domesticated, showing children “marvellous shapes”. The uncanny has become a fireside game. What once haunted Redlaw is now Christmas entertainment, and the change shows how far he has come.

3

The one thing unchanged

The shadows change everything ‘real and familiar’ into ‘wild and magical’, except one thing, which every eye in the room goes back to. Dickens builds the sentence to delay it: the list of gazers, the long subordinate clause, the “which the shadows did not obscure or change”. The reader waits with the family. In a room of shifting shapes, one fixed point holds, and the sentence makes us look for it.

4

The portrait under holly

It is the picture of the college’s founder, in beard and ruff—seen earlier when old Philip explained that the founder’s will left money for holly at Christmas, and that his scroll bore a motto. The portrait “gazing from the darkness… like life” is the book’s answer to its ghost: a dead man whose memory has been kept for centuries, wreathed in the holly he paid for, looking down on the living he has never stopped providing for. Memory, the story argues, is how the dead go on doing good.

5

The last line

The motto is given its own line, set apart, “as if a voice had uttered them”: Lord keep my Memory green. It is a prayer, and it is the moral of a book whose haunted man asked to have his memory erased. Green is the holly’s colour and the colour of what does not wither. Redlaw wanted to forget sorrow and lost his humanity with it; the founder asked to be remembered and became a blessing. Dickens lets the dead man’s prayer close the book, and says nothing more himself.

What the wording changes

A ghost story that ends by declining to be one.

The Haunted Man is the least read of the Christmas books and the most explicitly argued. Its premise reverses A Christmas Carol: where Scrooge is saved by being made to remember, Redlaw is nearly destroyed by being allowed to forget. The phantom’s gift—to lose all memory of “sorrow, wrong, and trouble”—seems merciful and proves catastrophic, because the memory of suffering is the root of pity, and a man who cannot remember being hurt cannot feel for anyone who is. Dickens wrote the book in 1848, a year of revolutions, and its argument that memory is the ground of sympathy has a public edge: a society that forgets its wrongs will stop caring about them.

The ending’s refusal to explain is unusual for Dickens, and deliberate. The Carol insists on its ghosts; the Chimes and the Cricket hedge toward dreams; The Haunted Man lays out the rationalisations, declines them, and offers instead a scene the narrator can vouch for because it is ordinary—a family, a fire, a portrait. The supernatural machinery is retired, and what remains is what the machinery was for: people gathered in a room, remembering. Dickens had learned, over five Christmas books, that the moral did not need the ghost, and this last ending lets the portrait carry the weight the phantom used to.

The final line has had a life of its own. ‘Lord, keep my memory green’ became a Victorian motto, quoted on memorials and in sermons, and it puts the book’s whole theology of remembrance into six words: the wish is to be kept alive in the minds of the living, doing good after death as the founder does with his holly. It is Dickens’s own hope for his books, and for this one, which he ended by placing a dead man’s prayer under a wreath and stepping silently out of the room.

Take one question away

The narrator says nothing, and a portrait says the last word. Why does Dickens end his last ghost story by handing its moral to a picture?

Before reading

List the explanations.

Write down the three rationalisations the narrator reports, and his response to each. Then ask what the ending would lose if he chose one. The refusal is a decision; find out what it protects.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

The room and the portrait.

  1. List everything in the hall that the shadows change, and the one thing they do not.
  2. Ask why Dickens makes the fixed point a painting, and not a person.
  3. Read the motto aloud as a prayer, as a warning, and as a boast. Which fits the book?
  4. Compare with the last line of A Christmas Carol. Which ending trusts the reader more?
Essay bridge

Two Christmas books about memory.

For an essay on Dickens at Christmas, set The Haunted Man against A Christmas Carol: one man saved by remembering, one nearly ruined by forgetting. Ask what Dickens believed memory was for, and why the argument needed a ghost in 1843 and a portrait in 1848.
