Passage Room/The Call of the Wild/The ecstasy of the hunt

Close reading 50 · The Call of the Wild

“the ecstasy that marks the summit of life”

A stolen house-dog runs a rabbit down through the moonlit snow, and London stops the story to say what the running means: that the highest moment of being alive is the one in which you forget you are alive at all.

Jack LondonChapter III · The Dominant Primordial Beast

Published in 1903. Buck, a domestic dog stolen and sold into the Klondike gold rush, is reverting to the wild. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: notice where the animal’s hunt turns into the narrator’s philosophy. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Call of the WildChapter III

All that stirring of old instincts which at stated periods drives men out from the sounding cities … the blood lust, the joy to kill1—all this was Buck’s, only it was infinitely more intimate. He was ranging at the head of the pack, running the wild thing down, the living meat, to kill with his own teeth2.

There is an ecstasy that marks the summit of life, and beyond which life cannot rise.3 And such is the paradox of living, this ecstasy comes when one is most alive, and it comes as a complete forgetfulness that one is alive.4

This ecstasy… comes to the artist, caught up and out of himself in a sheet of flame; it comes to the soldier, war-mad on a stricken field and refusing quarter; and it came to Buck … He was sounding the deeps of his nature, and of the parts of his nature that were deeper than he, going back into the womb of Time.5 He was mastered by the sheer surging of life, the tidal wave of being6, the perfect joy of each separate muscle, joint, and sinew.

The Call of the Wild, Chapter III · public-domain text · ellipses mark shortened stretches

1

The joy the book will not disown

London names it flatly: “the blood lust, the joy to kill”. The novel does not treat Buck’s descent into predation as a fall to be mourned but as an ascent to be celebrated. Killing is presented as joy, and the reader is asked to feel it as such. This is what disturbs and thrills: an adventure story from the children’s shelf whose moral centre is the ecstasy of taking life.

2

“With his own teeth”

London contrasts modern men who kill “by chemically propelled leaden pellets”—guns, from a distance—with Buck killing “the living meat” directly, “with his own teeth”. The animal’s violence is more “intimate” and, in the book’s logic, more honest than the sportsman’s. Buck does not hide behind technology from what killing is. The passage prefers the wolf to the hunter.

3

The sentence lifts out of the story

Here the narration stops being about a dog and becomes a general law: “There is an ecstasy that marks the summit of life.” London reaches for the tone of scripture or philosophy—a universal claim about all living things. The move is characteristic: he keeps interrupting the adventure to universalise it, insisting that Buck’s experience is the pattern of all intense existence, human included.

4

The paradox at the core

The summit of life “comes as a complete forgetfulness that one is alive”. Full aliveness, London argues, is self-forgetting—the ego dissolved in pure activity. Consciousness of the self is a kind of diminishment; the peak is reached only when it drops away. It is a mystic’s idea in a naturalist’s book: transcendence found at the bottom of the body, in instinct.

5

“The womb of Time”

Buck is “going back into the womb of Time”, sounding parts of his nature “deeper than he”. London’s theory of atavism—that the ancestral wild survives inside the tame, waiting to reawaken—runs through the whole novel. This is popular Darwinism made lyrical: evolution imagined as a memory the body still holds, and going back to the wild as a homecoming.

6

The rhythm of surging

“The tidal wave of being… the perfect joy of each separate muscle, joint, and sinew”—London’s prose swells into incantation, its rhythm imitating the surge it describes. He piles clause on clause, sensory and total, until the sentence itself seems to run with the pack. The style is the argument: to read it is to be swept, briefly, into the self-forgetting rapture the passage claims is the summit of life.

What the wording changes

An adventure story with a philosophy of joy in it.

The Call of the Wild is read as a boys’ dog story and studied as a document of American naturalism, and this passage is where the two meet. On the surface it is simply Buck hunting a rabbit in the snow—thrilling, kinetic, easy to follow. But London keeps breaking the action to install a worldview: that the deepest joy available to any creature, human or animal, is the ecstasy of instinct fully unleashed, and that civilisation, for all its comforts, has cut modern people off from it. The rabbit hunt is a lecture on what it means to be alive, delivered at a dead run.

The philosophy is a vivid, popular fusion of ideas in the air around 1900. From Darwin, London takes descent and the survival of the fittest; from Herbert Spencer and the social Darwinists, the idea that strength and struggle are life’s law; from Nietzsche, filtered and simplified, the celebration of vital power over tame morality. Buck’s reversion dramatises all of it: the veneer of domestication peels away to reveal the “dominant primordial beast” beneath, and the book treats that revelation as liberation. What in a moralist would be a warning, London writes as an ecstasy.

It is worth reading with some resistance. The equation of killing with the “summit of life”, and the celebration of the strong reverting to a state “deeper” than conscience, sit close to a glorification of force that the twentieth century would learn to distrust. London himself held tangled, often contradictory politics, and the novel’s rapturous primitivism can be read as thrilling or as troubling, sometimes in the same paragraph. That doubleness is exactly why the passage repays close attention: it is beautiful, propulsive writing in the service of a claim—that our truest self is the self that forgets itself in violence—which the reader is swept along by before being asked to examine.

Take one question away

London calls the joy of the kill “the summit of life”. Is the passage revealing a truth civilisation hides from us—or seducing us into admiring something we should question?

Before reading

Mark the switch.

Find the exact sentence where the writing stops describing Buck and starts stating a universal law (“There is an ecstasy…”). Everything before is story; everything after is philosophy. London keeps crossing that line—track how often.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Test the claim.

  1. State London’s thesis in one sentence: the summit of life is…
  2. List the three examples he gives (artist, soldier, dog). What do they share?
  3. Find a peak human experience the thesis cannot explain—does it break the argument?
  4. Discuss whether the prose’s excitement is evidence for the claim or a way of smuggling it past you.
Essay bridge

Atavism and the age.

For an essay on the novel and its era, connect “the womb of Time” to the book’s theory of reversion and to the Darwinian and Nietzschean ideas London drew on. Ask what the book gains as art from its philosophy of instinct—and what a reader should hold at arm’s length in its celebration of the strong.
