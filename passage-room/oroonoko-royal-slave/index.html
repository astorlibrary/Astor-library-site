Passage Room/Oroonoko/The royal slave

Close reading 44 · Oroonoko

“a perfect ebony, or polished jet”

A woman writer, decades before the novel had a name, introduces an enslaved African prince to English readers as the most beautiful man she has seen—and reveals, in the praising, the racial arithmetic her own age has taught her.

Aphra BehnThe narrator meets the prince

Published in 1688. Behn was among the first Englishwomen to earn her living by the pen; her narrator claims to have known Oroonoko in the English colony of Surinam. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

Content note

The passage praises a Black man in the racial terms of 1688, defining his beauty by its distance from other Africans. The language is part of what the reading has to confront, not something to read past.

How to read this page: notice what each compliment measures Oroonoko against. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

OroonokoThe prince

He was pretty tall, but of a shape the most exact that can be fancy’d: the most famous statuary could not form the figure of a man more admirably turn’d from head to foot.1 His face was not of that brown rusty black which most of that nation are, but a perfect ebony, or polished jet.2 His eyes were the most awful that could be seen, and very piercing; the white of ’em being like snow, as were his teeth. His nose was rising and Roman, instead of African and flat3: his mouth the finest shaped that could be seen; far from those great turn’d lips, which are so natural to the rest of the negroes.

The whole proportion and air of his face was so nobly and exactly form’d, that, bating his colour, there could be nothing in nature more beautiful4, agreeable and handsome.

Nor did the perfections of his mind come short of those of his person; for his discourse was admirable upon almost any subject … and whoever had heard him speak, would have been convinced of their errors, that all fine wit is confined to the white men5, especially to those of Christendom.

Oroonoko · public-domain text · one clause shortened

1

Beauty measured by a European chisel

The standard of comparison is a classical sculptor—a “statuary”. Oroonoko is beautiful to the degree that he resembles a Greek or Roman statue, the West’s inherited template of the ideal male body. Praise this high still routes his beauty through European art, so that an African prince is complimented by how closely he approaches a marble Apollo.

2

Even his blackness is refined upward

Behn distinguishes Oroonoko’s skin from “that brown rusty black which most of that nation are”: his is “a perfect ebony, or polished jet”—precious, gleaming, worked material. The compliment carries its own insult: he is beautiful because he is unlike other Africans. His singularity is built by lowering everyone who looks like him.

3

“Roman, instead of African”

The nose is praised for not being African; the mouth for lacking “those great turn’d lips”. Feature by feature, Oroonoko is admired for the ways his face departs from a physical type the passage treats as self-evidently inferior. This is the racial thinking of Behn’s moment made visible in a portrait: beauty defined as proximity to whiteness, even in a face the narrator calls the finest she has seen.

4

“Bating his colour”

The three-word phrase is the passage’s hinge—“bating” means except for, setting aside. Nothing in nature is more beautiful than Oroonoko, the narrator says, once you subtract the very thing she has just spent a paragraph re-describing as ebony and jet. The sentence admires and disqualifies in a single breath, and cannot see the contradiction it has written.

5

The claim the book half-breaks

Oroonoko’s eloquence, Behn says, would refute anyone who believed “all fine wit is confined to the white men… of Christendom”. The sentence names a genuine prejudice and pushes against it—this is, for 1688, a startling assertion of a Black man’s intellectual equality. Yet the refutation still works by exception: Oroonoko disproves the rule precisely because he is presented as unlike his people. The book reaches toward equality and keeps hold of the hierarchy at the same time.

What the wording changes

An abolitionist impulse inside a racial frame.

Oroonoko is often called an early anti-slavery text, and the claim is not baseless: Behn makes an enslaved African her tragic hero, grants him nobility, intelligence and eloquence, and narrates his betrayal and horrific execution as an outrage. For a woman writing in the 1680s—when the English slave trade was expanding and almost no one questioned it—centring a novel on the humanity of an enslaved prince was a remarkable act. The book’s sympathy is real and, for its moment, radical.

But the sympathy is engineered through this portrait, and the portrait exposes its limits. Oroonoko earns the reader’s pity by being exceptional—royal, Romanised, statue-shaped, unlike “the rest of the negroes”. Behn’s humanitarianism runs through the very racial hierarchy that slavery depended on: she can imagine a great man wronged, but she builds his greatness by distancing him from ordinary Africans, whom the same sentences dismiss. The book indicts a particular injustice without indicting the system, and cannot picture a hero who looks like the people that system enslaves in their thousands.

That is why the passage rewards close reading rather than either celebration or dismissal. It is a document of a mind reaching past its inherited assumptions and being caught by them—praising a Black man’s wit against the claim that wit is white, while measuring his beauty by how little of Africa his face shows. Behn wrote at the beginning of the English novel and at the beginning of England’s deep entanglement with slavery; Oroonoko holds both beginnings together, and the strain between them is legible in every compliment.

Take one question away

Can a portrait that makes a man beautiful by his distance from his own people also make a case for that people’s humanity—or does the one undo the other?

Before reading

List the comparisons.

For each feature Behn praises—shape, colour, nose, mouth, wit—write down what it is measured against. Every compliment has a hidden yardstick. Collect the yardsticks, and the passage’s frame of reference becomes visible.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Two things at once.

  1. Find the sentences where Behn asserts Oroonoko’s equality or superiority.
  2. Find the sentences where she secures it by contrast with other Africans.
  3. Mark the phrase “bating his colour” and paraphrase exactly what it asks the reader to set aside.
  4. Debate: is the portrait progressive for 1688, complicit, or—unavoidably—both?
Essay bridge

Sympathy and its limits.

For an essay on the book and slavery, trace the exceptionalism of this portrait through to Oroonoko’s death, and ask how the novel treats the enslaved people who are not princes. Set Behn’s partial vision against the fuller abolitionist argument of writers like Frederick Douglass a century and a half later, whose case rested on common humanity rather than aristocratic exception.
