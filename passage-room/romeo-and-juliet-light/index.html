Close reading 16 · Romeo and Juliet

“Juliet is the sun”

Romeo believes he is describing Juliet. He is largely describing his own way of loving: a rhetoric of light, worship and distance that the rest of the scene—and Juliet herself—will put under pressure.

William ShakespeareAct 2, Scene 2

Probably written in the mid-1590s and first printed in 1597. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read the speech once without stopping, noticing that Juliet hears none of it. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Romeo and Juliet2.2

But soft1, what light through yonder window breaks?
It is the east, and Juliet is the sun!2
Arise fair sun and kill the envious moon,
Who is already sick and pale with grief,
That thou her maid art far more fair than she.
Be not her maid since she is envious;
Her vestal livery is but sick and green3,
And none but fools do wear it; cast it off.
It is my lady, O it is my love!
O, that she knew she were!

She speaks, yet she says nothing.4 What of that?
Her eye discourses, I will answer it.
I am too bold, ’tis not to me she speaks.
Two of the fairest stars in all the heaven5,
Having some business, do entreat her eyes
To twinkle in their spheres till they return.

See how she leans her cheek upon her hand.
O that I were a glove upon that hand6,
That I might touch that cheek.

Romeo and Juliet, Act 2, Scene 2 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · four lines omitted after the stars

1

A whisper that opens a flood

“Soft” here means wait—quietly. Romeo hushes himself, then delivers some of the most unrestrained praise in the play. The little self-interruption reminds us that he is trespassing in the Capulet orchard, where being heard could cost his life.

2

Not “like” the sun

Romeo does not offer a comparison; he makes an identification. Juliet is the sun, and the window becomes the east. The grammar matters: a simile keeps two things separate, while this metaphor rebuilds the world so that Juliet stands at its centre and everything else—moon, stars, night—must adjust around her.

3

The moon’s sickly uniform

The moon belongs to Diana, goddess of chastity, so her “vestal livery” is the uniform of virginity. “Sick and green” touches a specific idea: green-sickness, an anaemia Elizabethan medicine associated with unmarried girls. Romeo is urging Juliet, through the image, to stop serving chastity—an argument she has not been asked to hear.

4

Reading a silent woman

The paradox is honest: Juliet’s lips move, or her face is expressive, but no words reach him. Romeo immediately claims that her eye “discourses” and volunteers to answer it. He is composing both halves of the conversation—which is what he had been doing with Rosaline, who never speaks in the play at all.

5

Errands among the spheres

In the older astronomy the stars sat in revolving crystalline spheres. Romeo imagines two stars called away “having some business”—an oddly workaday phrase—and asking Juliet’s eyes to shine in their place. The conceit is conventional Elizabethan love-poetry; what is fresh is how far he pursues it, testing whether eyes in heaven and stars in a face would still outshine each other.

6

From goddess to glove

After the sun, the moon and the spheres, the speech lands on a piece of clothing. The wish is deliberately small: he wants to be a glove she touches without noticing. The drop in scale is the truest line in the speech, with desire measured by how close he could be to her cheek.

What the wording changes

The scene builds the rhetoric so that Juliet can interrupt it.

Everything Romeo says here is said about Juliet, and none of it to her. That is the point of the staging: he praises an unreachable image, exactly as the sonnet tradition taught young men to praise disdainful mistresses, and exactly as he had praised Rosaline an act earlier. The vocabulary of suns, stars and worship is inherited equipment.

What changes is that this mistress talks back. Within moments Juliet speaks, ‘O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?’, and she is talking about names, families and the practical problem of his being a Montague. When she discovers Romeo is listening, her concern is concrete: how he got in, whether he will be murdered, whether his love is honourable and what time tomorrow she should send a messenger. The scene sets extravagant metaphor and plain arrangement side by side, and lets them court each other.

The light imagery is not wasted, though. Throughout the play the lovers describe each other as light in the dark; Juliet will later imagine Romeo cut out in little stars. A tragedy that ends in a dark tomb gives that habit its price: the brightness the lovers see in each other is real to them and invisible to Verona.

Take one question away

If the speech repeats the conventions Romeo used for Rosaline, what—if anything—in its language shows that this love is different?

Before reading

Separate the two audiences.

Mark every phrase Juliet can hear (none) and every phrase the theatre audience hears (all of it). Then note each image of light and each image of sickness or death. Both threads run through the whole play.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Translate the metaphors away.

  1. Rewrite the speech’s claims in plain prose: “Juliet is more beautiful than other women”, and so on.
  2. List what the plain version loses: worship? energy? danger?
  3. Decide which single metaphor is least translatable, and defend the choice.
  4. Compare with Juliet’s first speeches in the same scene: what does she do that Romeo does not?
Essay bridge

Put the balcony beside the first meeting.

At the Capulets’ feast Romeo and Juliet share a sonnet about pilgrims, saints and prayer, each speaking half. Here Romeo composes alone. For an essay on love and language, trace who controls the imagery in each meeting, and what happens to Romeo’s style once Juliet answers it.
