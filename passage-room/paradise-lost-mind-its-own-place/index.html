“The mind is its own place”

Newly fallen, surveying Hell, Satan announces that location does not matter because the mind makes its own. It is the most quotable philosophy in the poem, and the character who speaks it has the strongest motive to believe it.

John MiltonBook I

First published in ten books in 1667 and rearranged into twelve in 1674. Milton, blind from 1652, composed the poem by dictation. The wording below follows the modern-spelling Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read the speech as oratory: a defeated commander rallying himself before he rallies his army. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Paradise Lost, Book I · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered within the extract

1

Greeting his own ruin

“Farewell” and “hail” are ceremonial words, the language of formal welcome, even of coronation. Satan salutes horror as though taking possession of a new kingdom, which is the performance he needs: if he can greet Hell, he was not thrown into it. The balanced antitheses, happy fields against an infernal world, show a speaker making ceremony out of catastrophe as he goes.

2

The unchanged mind

The boast borrows the Stoic ideal: the wise man’s mind stays constant whatever fortune does. But a mind “not to be changed” is also a mind that cannot repent, learn or be forgiven. What Satan advertises as strength, the poem’s theology counts as the mechanism of damnation: he is fixed.

3

The claim itself

Heaven and Hell become states of mind, made from within. The idea has a real philosophical pedigree, and Milton gives it two of the most balanced lines in the poem: place mirrored against place, Heaven and Hell swapping across the caesura. The polish matters, because a mind that claims to make its own reality is showing off that power in the verse itself. Whether the poem endorses the claim is another matter; remember where Satan is standing as he says it.

4

“If I be still the same”

The whole claim balances on that “if”. The poem will answer it: Satan degrades by stages: throned rebel, spy, toad at Eve’s ear, serpent, and finally a hiss. Place cannot change him, but his own choices do. The line is worth remembering nine books later, when nothing about him is the same except the refusal.

5

Freedom’s fine print

“Here at least we shall be free” is the speech’s most sympathetic moment, and two lines later freedom has become “reign”. The liberty Satan offers his followers is his own monarchy relocated. Milton, who had defended a republic and justified the execution of a king, builds the irony carefully: the rebel against Heaven’s throne cannot imagine anything but a throne.

6

The epigram

The line survives because it is perfectly weighted: reign against serve, Hell against Heaven, and the metre landing its stresses exactly where the argument wants them. The balance hides an assumption: that these are the only two options. In the poem’s Heaven, service is repeatedly called freedom; Satan’s antithesis deletes that possibility without anyone noticing, and his best rhetoric usually works that way.

What the wording changes

Satan’s grandeur

Nothing in Paradise Lost is more debated than how to take Satan’s grandeur. Blake thought Milton was on the Devil’s side without knowing it; Shelley thought Satan a finer moral being than Milton’s God; generations of readers have felt the speeches pull against the poem’s official argument. Milton made that pull. He gives Satan the full resources of epic oratory, the tradition of defiant commanders that runs back to Homer, and lets the verse thunder.

But the poem places every speech, and placement is judgement. This one is delivered in plain view of the evidence against it: Satan speaks of the mind making “a Heaven of Hell” while he stands on burning ground beside the lake he was chained on, and the narrator has already described his first speech as “Vaunting aloud, but racked with deep despair”. This is consolation made up on the spot by a character who cannot afford despair in front of his second-in-command, and the poem never adopts it as its own philosophy.

Milton supplies the refutation in Satan’s own voice. Alone on Mount Niphates in Book IV, with no audience to perform for, he reverses this speech almost point by point: “Which way I fly is Hell; myself am Hell.” The mind is its own place; that much proves true. What collapses is the boast that this power makes him free. The mind that makes its own Hell carries it everywhere, which is the poem’s answer to its most quotable line.

Take one question away

If a claim is spoken by an unreliable speaker in desperate circumstances, can it still be true? What exactly does Book IV take back: the idea, or the confidence?

Before reading

Listen for the balance.

Mark every antithesis: farewell/hail, Heaven/Hell, reign/serve, changed/same. Satan thinks in balanced oppositions. Ask what each pair leaves out; the missing third option is usually where the poem stands.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Audience and performance.

  1. Establish who is listening: Beelzebub, just pulled from the burning lake.
  2. Mark the phrases aimed at keeping a follower loyal.
  3. Rewrite two sentences as private thought instead of speech. What changes?
  4. Compare with the Niphates soliloquy in Book IV, where there is no audience. List what Satan admits there that he cannot admit here.
Essay bridge

The reader’s temptation.

For an essay on the poem’s design, consider the argument that Milton makes Satan attractive on purpose: the reader who thrills to this speech experiences, in miniature, how temptation works, and then meets the evidence. Test that reading against this passage’s own ironies as well as the critics’ claims.
