“The mind is its own place”

Newly fallen, surveying Hell, Satan announces that location does not matter because the mind makes its own. It is the most quotable philosophy in the poem—spoken by the character with the strongest motive to believe it.

John MiltonBook I

First published in ten books in 1667 and rearranged into twelve in 1674. Milton, blind from 1652, composed the poem by dictation. The wording below follows the modern-spelling Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read the speech as oratory—it is a defeated commander rallying himself before rallying his army. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Paradise Lost, Book I · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered within the extract

1

Greeting his own ruin

“Farewell” and “hail” are ceremonial words—the language of formal welcome, even of coronation. Satan salutes horror as though taking possession of a new kingdom, which is exactly the performance he needs: if he can greet Hell, he was not thrown into it. The balanced antitheses, happy fields against an infernal world, show a speaker making ceremony out of catastrophe as he goes.

2

The unchanged mind

The boast borrows the Stoic ideal: the wise man’s mind stays constant whatever fortune does. But a mind “not to be changed” is also a mind that cannot repent, learn or be forgiven. What Satan advertises as strength, the poem’s theology counts as the precise mechanism of damnation: he is fixed.

3

The claim itself

Heaven and Hell become states of mind, manufactured from within. The idea has real philosophical pedigree, and Milton gives it two of the most balanced lines in the poem—place mirrored against place, Heaven and Hell swapping across the caesura. The polish is the point: this is the sentence of a mind that makes its own reality, demonstrated in verse. Whether the poem endorses it is another matter; note where Satan is standing as he says it.

4

“If I be still the same”

The whole claim balances on that “if”. The poem will answer it: Satan degrades by stages—throned rebel, spy, toad at Eve’s ear, serpent, and finally a hiss. Place cannot change him, but his own choices do. The line is worth remembering eight books later, when nothing about him is the same except the refusal.

5

Freedom’s fine print

“Here at least we shall be free” is the speech’s most sympathetic moment—and within three lines freedom has become “reign”. The liberty Satan offers his followers is his own monarchy relocated. Milton, who had defended a republic and justified the execution of a king, builds the irony carefully: the rebel against Heaven’s throne cannot imagine anything but a throne.

6

The epigram

The line survives because it is perfectly weighted: reign against serve, Hell against Heaven, and the metre landing its stresses exactly where the argument wants them. Notice what the balance conceals—the comparison assumes those are the only two options. In the poem’s Heaven, service is repeatedly called freedom; Satan’s antithesis deletes that possibility without anyone noticing, which is how his best rhetoric always works.

What the wording changes

Magnificent speech, and the poem knows it.

Nothing in Paradise Lost is more debated than how to take Satan’s grandeur. Blake wrote that Milton was “of the Devil’s party without knowing it”; Shelley admired Satan as a moral being superior to his God; generations of readers have felt the speeches’ pull against the poem’s official argument. The pull is real because Milton made it real. He gives Satan the full resources of epic oratory—the same tradition of defiant commanders that runs back to Homer—and lets the verse thunder.

But the poem places every speech, and placement is judgment. This one is delivered flat on the evidence against it: Satan speaks of the mind making “a Heaven of Hell” while chained lakes of fire surround him, and the narrator has already told us his “dire attempt” torments him inwardly. The speech is not philosophy offered by the poem; it is consolation manufactured by a character who cannot afford despair in front of his second-in-command.

Milton supplies the refutation in Satan’s own voice. Alone on Mount Niphates in Book IV, with no audience to perform for, he reverses this speech almost point by point: “Which way I fly is Hell; myself am Hell.” The mind is its own place—that much proves true. What collapses is the boast that this power makes him free. The mind that makes its own Hell carries it everywhere, which is the poem’s answer to its most quotable line.

Take one question away

If a claim is spoken by an unreliable speaker in desperate circumstances, can it still be true? What exactly does Book IV take back—the idea, or the confidence?

Before reading

Listen for the balance.

Mark every antithesis: farewell/hail, Heaven/Hell, reign/serve, changed/same. Satan thinks in balanced oppositions. Ask what each pair leaves out—the missing third option is usually where the poem stands.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Audience and performance.

  1. Establish who is listening: Beelzebub, just pulled from the burning lake.
  2. Mark the phrases aimed at keeping a follower loyal.
  3. Rewrite two sentences as private thought instead of speech. What changes?
  4. Compare with the Niphates soliloquy in Book IV, where there is no audience. List what Satan admits there that he cannot admit here.
Essay bridge

The reader’s temptation.

For an essay on the poem’s design, consider the argument that Milton makes Satan attractive on purpose: the reader who thrills to this speech experiences, in miniature, how temptation works—then meets the evidence. Test that reading against this passage’s own ironies, not just the critics’ claims.
