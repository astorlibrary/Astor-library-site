“airy nothing”

On the evening of the weddings, Theseus explains that the lovers’ story cannot be true: imagination invents such things. He is a character in a fairy play, dismissing fairies, and his wife is about to correct him.

William ShakespeareAct 5, Scene 1

Probably written around 1595–96. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read both speakers: the exchange is a debate, and the second voice matters. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

A Midsummer Night’s Dream5.1

THESEUSMore strange than true. I never may believe
These antique fables, nor these fairy toys.1
Lovers and madmen have such seething brains,
Such shaping fantasies, that apprehend
More than cool reason ever comprehends.
The lunatic, the lover, and the poet
Are of imagination all compact:2
One sees more devils than vast hell can hold;
That is the madman: the lover, all as frantic,
Sees Helen’s beauty in a brow of Egypt:3
The poet’s eye, in a fine frenzy rolling,
Doth glance from heaven to earth, from earth to heaven;
And as imagination bodies forth
The forms of things unknown, the poet’s pen
Turns them to shapes, and gives to airy nothing
A local habitation and a name.4
Such tricks hath strong imagination,
That if it would but apprehend some joy,
It comprehends some bringer of that joy.
Or in the night, imagining some fear,
How easy is a bush supposed a bear?5

HIPPOLYTABut all the story of the night told over,
And all their minds transfigur’d so together,
More witnesseth than fancy’s images,
And grows to something of great constancy;6
But, howsoever, strange and admirable.

A Midsummer Night’s Dream, Act 5, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

A fable dismissing fables

Theseus will not believe “antique fables”, and he is one: a hero of Greek legend, married to the queen of the Amazons, speaking inside a comedy full of fairies the audience has watched with its own eyes. The irony is gentle. The sceptic gets the play’s most eloquent speech, and delivers it from inside everything he denies.

2

Three makers of the unreal

“Compact” means composed, made entirely of imagination. The grouping is the speech’s famous provocation: the madman, the lover and the poet differ only in what their seething brains produce. The poet comes third, after the lunatic and the lover. Theseus intends the company to be damning; four centuries of poets have quoted it as a compliment.

3

Love re-grades faces

The lover’s madness is a misjudgment of beauty: seeing Helen of Troy’s face in “a brow of Egypt”, which for Elizabethans meant a dark, foreign face, unbeautiful by their conventions. The example is unkind, and accurate about what the play has staged: under Puck’s flower-juice, Lysander and Demetrius switched their devotion overnight while the faces stayed the same.

4

Airy nothing

Imagination “bodies forth / The forms of things unknown”; the pen turns them to shapes and gives “airy nothing / A local habitation and a name”. As an account of what art does, giving the non-existent an address, it is hard to better, which is why it is remembered as praise. In context it is an accusation: poets build houses for nothing. Both readings stand, since the fairies the play has named and housed (Peaseblossom, Cobweb, a bank where the wild thyme blows) are the evening’s chief delight.

5

The bush and the bear

After the grand three comes a homely example: at night, in fear, a bush looks like a bear. The proverb-like couplet makes imagination’s tricks ordinary; everyone has done this walking home in the dark. But the fear is real even when the bear is not, which is close to Hippolyta’s point about the lovers.

6

Hippolyta’s reply

Her rebuttal reasons from evidence. One dreamer might fancy anything; but four minds “transfigur’d so together”, telling one consistent story, “witnesseth” (the word is legal) something more than private images, and the story grows to “great constancy”: coherence, reliability. Independent testimony that agrees defeats the theory of individual delusion. Her closing words, “strange and admirable”, keep the wonder without giving up the logic.

What the wording changes

Theseus and Hippolyta

In its final act the play thinks about itself. Theseus’s theory would, if true, dissolve everything the audience has just watched: the wood, Puck, the transformations, the reconciled lovers. Shakespeare gives the sceptic real eloquence (“cool reason” against “seething brains” is a fair fight) and then places the speech where it must lose, since we, like Hippolyta, hold the evidence of the whole previous night.

The rest of the act settles the argument in practice. The mechanicals perform “Pyramus and Thisbe”, theatre so bad that it cannot sustain illusion for a second: Wall announces he is a wall; Moon explains his lantern. Theseus, watching, turns unexpectedly generous: “The best in this kind are but shadows; and the worst are no worse, if imagination amend them.” The man who dismissed imagination concedes that all theatre runs on the audience’s imaginative charity. He takes back his speech, without announcing it, while watching a play, as we are.

Puck’s epilogue then hands the whole question to the audience: if the play has offended, think “that you have but slumber’d here / While these visions did appear”. The dream defence is offered to us exactly as Theseus offered it about the lovers, and an audience that accepts it ends up in Hippolyta’s position: we all dreamed the same dream together, which is as good a definition of theatre as “airy nothing” is of poetry.

Take one question away

Theseus says imagination sees what is not there; Hippolyta says shared vision is evidence. Which account better describes what an audience does?

Before reading

Set the scene’s clock.

Fix where this exchange sits: the evening of the weddings, after the night in the wood and before the entertainment. The lovers have told their story offstage. List what Theseus knows, what Hippolyta knows and what the audience knows: three bodies of evidence hearing one speech.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Try the triad.

  1. Take the lunatic, the lover and the poet. For each, find Theseus’s account of what they see.
  2. Match each figure to a character from the play’s night. Who was the madman, who the lover, who did the poet’s work?
  3. Test Hippolyta’s standard on the lovers’ story: what makes multiple witnesses convincing, and when does it fail?
  4. Vote: “more strange than true”, or true because shared?
Essay bridge

Shadows and amendment.

For an essay on the play’s idea of theatre, connect three moments: this speech, Theseus’s “The best in this kind are but shadows” during the mechanicals’ play, and Puck’s closing offer that the audience call it all a dream. Together they form the play’s own theory of what imagination builds and what audiences agree to.
