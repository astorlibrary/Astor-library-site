Close reading 55 · Richard III

“Now is the winter of our discontent”

The play opens with its villain alone on stage, explaining the peace, explaining his body, and explaining exactly what he intends to do about both. No character in Shakespeare takes the audience into his confidence so early, or so completely.

William ShakespeareAct 1, Scene 1

Probably written around 1592–93. Richard, Duke of Gloucester, speaks after his brother Edward IV has secured the throne for the house of York. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: watch the speech turn, at line 14, from “our” to “I”. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Richard III1.1

RichardNow is the winter of our discontent
Made glorious summer by this son of York1;
And all the clouds that loured upon our house
In the deep bosom of the ocean buried.
5Now are our brows bound with victorious wreaths,
Our bruised arms hung up for monuments,
Our stern alarums changed to merry meetings,
Our dreadful marches to delightful measures.
Grim-visaged war hath smoothed his wrinkled front2;
10And now, instead of mounting barbed steeds
To fright the souls of fearful adversaries,
He capers nimbly in a lady’s chamber
To the lascivious pleasing of a lute.
But I, that am not shaped for sportive tricks3,
15Nor made to court an amorous looking-glass;
I, that am rudely stamped, and want love’s majesty
To strut before a wanton ambling nymph;
I, that am curtailed of this fair proportion,
Cheated of feature by dissembling nature,
20Deformed, unfinished, sent before my time
Into this breathing world scarce half made up4,
And that so lamely and unfashionable
That dogs bark at me as I halt by them—
Why, I, in this weak piping time of peace,
25Have no delight to pass away the time,
Unless to spy my shadow in the sun,
And descant on mine own deformity5.
And therefore, since I cannot prove a lover
To entertain these fair well-spoken days,
30I am determined to prove a villain6,
And hate the idle pleasures of these days.

Richard III, Act 1, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered from the start of the speech

1

Sun and son

“This son of York” is Edward IV, whose badge was a sun in splendour—so the line puns on sun: the Yorkist sun has turned winter to summer. The seasons are political weather. Notice too that it is “our discontent”, “our house”: for thirteen lines Richard speaks as a loyal member of the family, sharing its triumph. The speech is already a performance of belonging that the rest will strip away.

2

War goes courting

Personified War has smoothed his frown and, instead of mounting armoured horses, “capers nimbly in a lady’s chamber” to a lute. Richard’s contempt is audible in every soft word—capers, nimbly, lascivious pleasing. Peace, in his account, is emasculation: the soldier’s world reduced to dancing and seduction, a stage on which he has no part to play.

3

The turn to “I”

“But I” breaks the speech open. Three times the pronoun is hammered at the line-head—“I, that am… I, that am… I, that am”—anaphora that isolates the speaker from the “our” of the opening. He cannot dance, cannot court a mirror, cannot “strut before a wanton ambling nymph”. The exclusion is presented as fact, but the relish with which he describes what he scorns suggests a man half-glad of the excuse.

4

Blaming nature

Richard’s body is described as a botched manufacture: “rudely stamped” like a bad coin, “cheated of feature” by a “dissembling” nature, “unfinished”, delivered early “scarce half made up”. He makes nature the first villain and himself its victim—a logic that lets him convert his deformity into a licence. The Tudor history Shakespeare inherited had already made Richard’s crookedness the emblem of his wickedness; the play lets Richard seize that emblem and wield it himself.

5

A man watching his own shadow

His only pleasure in peacetime, he says, is to “spy my shadow in the sun / And descant on mine own deformity”—to sing variations (a descant) on the theme of himself. It is a strange, revealing image: solitude, self-regard, and a musical, almost artistic pleasure in his own misshapenness. The villain is also the play’s most gifted performer, and this is where we first see him rehearsing.

6

“Determined”

The famous declaration hinges on a double meaning. “Determined” can mean resolved—Richard has decided to be a villain—or predetermined: fate and his deformed body have fixed the part for him. Both are true at once, and the play never settles which. What is certain is the theatricality: to “prove” a villain is to play one, and the audience has just been cast as his accomplice.

What the wording changes

The villain makes the audience his confidant.

Shakespeare inherited a Richard already villainous—from Thomas More’s history and Holinshed’s chronicle, where the crookback murderer is a settled fact of Tudor propaganda. What he added was intimacy. By opening the play with Richard alone, addressing the theatre directly, he gave the villain the first and closest relationship with the audience, before any other character can compete. We know his plans before his brother Clarence does; we are told the lie about the prophecy before it is used. This complicity is the play’s engine: for four acts we watch Richard succeed, half-appalled and half-delighted, because he keeps turning to tell us how.

The soliloquy’s structure is its argument. It moves from “our” to “I”, from the family’s summer to the individual’s winter, from a description of peace to a resolution against it. Each stage is reasoned: peace is for lovers; I cannot be a lover; therefore I will be a villain. The logic is specious and the speech knows it—the alternative of being neither is simply never entertained—but the momentum carries, because Richard’s vitality is so much greater than anyone else’s on stage. He is the only character who seems fully awake.

The body is the crux, and the play is more subtle about it than its reputation suggests. Richard uses his deformity as an explanation, a weapon and a joke; the play invites us to ask whether it explains anything at all, or whether he has simply chosen the story that licenses what he wants. Twentieth-century performers have tended to play the speech with a wink—the deformity as alibi—and the text supports that: a man who can “descant on mine own deformity” with such pleasure is not its prisoner but its author. The winter of his discontent, in the end, is a season he has decided to keep.

Take one question away

Richard says he is “determined” to prove a villain. Has he decided, or been decided for—and why does the play want us unable to tell?

Before reading

Count the pronouns.

Mark every “our” in lines 1–13 and every “I” from line 14 on. The speech has two halves with two different speakers: the loyal brother and the solitary villain. Find the exact line where one becomes the other.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Test the syllogism.

  1. Write out Richard’s argument as three steps: peace is for lovers; I cannot love; therefore I will be a villain.
  2. Find the missing alternatives the argument skips.
  3. Read the speech once with the deformity as tragic cause, once as convenient excuse. Which reading do the words “descant” and “determined” support?
  4. Decide how much of the speech is confession and how much is performance.
Essay bridge

From confidant to conscience.

For an essay on Richard’s relationship with the audience, set this opening beside the ghost-haunted soliloquy before Bosworth in Act 5, where the “I” that was so confident here splits against itself: “Richard loves Richard, that is, I am I.” Trace how the play withdraws the intimacy it granted in scene one.
