Passage Room/Utopia/Sheep that devour men

Close reading 61 · Utopia

“your sheep… may be said now to devour men”

Before More describes his imaginary island, he lets a traveller describe England, and the traveller’s most famous sentence makes the mildest animal in the kingdom a predator with a landlord behind it.

Thomas MoreBook I

Written in Latin and published in 1516. The speaker is Raphael Hythloday, recounting a dinner-table argument at Cardinal Morton’s. The wording below follows Gilbert Burnet’s 1684 translation, the public-domain text on Project Gutenberg.

How to read this page: notice that the argument is about causes—why there are thieves—before it is about sheep. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

UtopiaBook I

‘But I do not think that this necessity of stealing1 arises only from hence; there is another cause of it, more peculiar to England.’ ‘What is that?’ said the Cardinal: ‘The increase of pasture,’ said I, ‘by which your sheep, which are naturally mild, and easily kept in order, may be said now to devour men2 and unpeople, not only villages, but towns; for wherever it is found that the sheep of any soil yield a softer and richer wool than ordinary, there the nobility and gentry, and even those holy men, the abbots!3 not contented with the old rents which their farms yielded, nor thinking it enough that they, living at their ease, do no good to the public, resolve to do it hurt instead of good. They stop the course of agriculture, destroying houses and towns, reserving only the churches4, and enclose grounds that they may lodge their sheep in them. As if forests and parks had swallowed up too little of the land, those worthy countrymen turn the best inhabited places into solitudes5.’

Utopia, Book I · Gilbert Burnet’s translation · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

Theft as necessity

The phrase is more radical than it sounds. Hythloday has been arguing that England hangs thieves by the score while producing them by policy—first through soldiers and idle retainers thrown out of work, and now, “more peculiar to England”, through enclosure. “Necessity of stealing” treats crime as an effect with economic causes, not a moral failing. It is a sixteenth-century sentence that reads like a nineteenth-century one.

2

The mild beast made monster

The famous inversion: sheep, “naturally mild, and easily kept in order”, now “devour men”. The paradox works because everyone knows sheep are harmless; the reader has to ask what has made them predators, and the answer is the price of wool. ‘Unpeople’ is the key verb: villages and towns emptied by grazing, without a blow struck.

3

The abbots in the dock

Hythloday names the enclosers: nobility, gentry, “and even those holy men, the abbots!” The exclamation mark is Burnet’s, but the sting is More’s. Monastic landlords were among the largest sheep-farmers in England, and More—a devout Catholic who would die for the Church—does not spare them. The critique is of a class behaviour, not a creed, and it crosses the line between laity and clergy without pausing.

4

“Reserving only the churches”

The detail is deadly. Houses and towns are destroyed to make sheep-walks, and the enclosers keep the church standing—empty of its parish. More lets the image speak: a landscape of grazing land punctuated by churches with no one left to attend them. It is a picture of piety preserved as architecture while the people who practised it are driven out.

5

“Those worthy countrymen”

The irony is bitter and controlled: the men who turn “the best inhabited places into solitudes” are “worthy”. Forests and parks—the aristocracy’s hunting reserves—had already “swallowed” land; enclosure completes the work. The passage builds a chain: pasture drives out tillage, tillage drove out people, people without land become thieves, thieves are hanged. The gallows at the end of the chain is where Hythloday began.

What the wording changes

The island of nowhere begins with the England of now.

Utopia is remembered for its second book—the description of the invented island with its communal property, six-hour working day and toleration—but the first book is a sharp, grounded piece of Tudor social criticism, and this is its most famous moment. Hythloday’s argument to Cardinal Morton (a real man, More’s own patron in youth) is structured like a lawyer’s case: England punishes theft with death, yet its own policies manufacture thieves. The sheep passage is the climax of the causal chain. Enclosure of common land for pasture was a live grievance in 1516, provoking royal commissions and, later, rebellions; More gives the grievance its lasting image.

The rhetoric is worth noticing because it is so controlled. The sentence about sheep is a paradox—the harmless made monstrous—but it is immediately explained, step by step, in terms of rents, wool prices and landlords’ choices. More is not writing a lament; he is writing an analysis with one unforgettable metaphor at its head. The irony (“worthy countrymen”, “holy men”) is aimed at a class, and the class includes the Church. That the future saint and martyr wrote this against the abbots is a reminder that Utopia is a more dangerous and less pious book than its author’s later reputation suggests.

There is also the question of who is speaking. Hythloday is a fictional traveller, and More appears in his own book as a cautious character who argues against him. The device lets More say the sharpest things at one remove—a technique of deniability that Swift would inherit for Gulliver. Whether More believed everything Hythloday says has been debated ever since; what is not in doubt is that he found the words, and that “sheep devour men” has outlived every enclosure act. Marx quoted it; so did generations of agrarian reformers. The imaginary island gave the book its title, but the real sheep gave it its teeth.

Take one question away

More puts his fiercest social criticism in the mouth of a fictional traveller and argues against him in his own person. Is that cowardice, craft—or the only way such things could be printed in 1516?

Before reading

Build the chain.

Write the causal sequence Hythloday constructs: wool prices rise, landlords enclose, farms are destroyed, people are displaced, the displaced steal, thieves are hanged. Every link is in the passage or the sentences around it. The sheep are only the most quotable link.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Test the metaphor.

  1. State plainly what “sheep devour men” means in economic terms.
  2. Ask why More blames the sheep in the sentence, and leaves the landlords until later.
  3. Find each ironic honorific—“holy men”, “worthy countrymen”—and say what it accuses.
  4. Discuss whether the metaphor helps the argument or lets the real culprits hide behind the animals.
Essay bridge

From the England of Book I to the island of Book II.

For an essay on Utopia’s structure, trace how the abuses catalogued here—private land, idle wealth, punishment without justice—are answered point by point by the island’s arrangements. Ask whether Book II is a solution, a satire, or a thought-experiment More expected no one to adopt.
