Close reading 52 · The Wind in the Willows

“Afraid! Of Him?”

In the middle of a book about a boastful toad and a cosy riverbank, two small animals meet a god face to face. It may be the strangest, most rapturous chapter in English children’s literature, and it ends by taking the memory away.

Kenneth GrahameChapter VII · The Piper at the Gates of Dawn

Published in 1908. Searching at dawn for a lost baby otter, the Water Rat and the Mole are drawn to an island, where they meet the god Pan. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: notice how awe and tenderness arrive in the same breath. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Wind in the WillowsChapter VII

… he saw the backward sweep of the curved horns, gleaming in the growing daylight; saw the stern, hooked nose between the kindly eyes1 that were looking down on them humourously, while the bearded mouth broke into a half-smile at the corners; … saw, last of all, nestling between his very hooves, sleeping soundly… the little, round, podgy, childish form of the baby otter.2

“Rat!” he found breath to whisper, shaking. “Are you afraid?3”

“Afraid?” murmured the Rat, his eyes shining with unutterable love.4 “Afraid! Of Him? O, never, never! And yet—and yet—O, Mole, I am afraid!”

Then the two animals, crouching to the earth, bowed their heads and did worship. … And with its soft touch came instant oblivion.5 For this is the last best gift that the kindly demi-god is careful to bestow on those to whom he has revealed himself in their helping: the gift of forgetfulness.6

The Wind in the Willows, Chapter VII · public-domain text · ellipses mark shortened stretches

1

The god is terrible and kind

Grahame’s Pan has “curved horns” and a “stern, hooked nose”, the ancient, faintly frightening face of the goat-god, set between “kindly eyes” that look down “humourously”. Every fearsome feature is paired with a gentle one. The divine is awe-inspiring and loving in the same face, and the animals cannot separate the two: dread and comfort arrive together.

2

The god as protector

The vision resolves, “last of all”, on the lost baby otter safe “between his very hooves”. The whole apparition of the pagan god exists, in the story, to return a lost child. Grahame brings the divine down to a riverbank, where Pan is doing the tender work of keeping a baby otter safe until morning. Cosmic awe is placed at the service of ordinary rescue.

3

The question at the centre

“Are you afraid?” is the passage’s pivot, and the whole chapter has been building to how it will be answered. Fear is the expected response to a god; Grahame is about to complicate it. The Mole asks in a whisper, “shaking”: his body has already answered before the Rat speaks.

4

“Unutterable love”

The Rat’s eyes shine with “unutterable love”, and his reply tangles itself: “Afraid! Of Him? O, never, never! And yet—and yet—O, Mole, I am afraid!” The contradiction is exact and unresolved. He loves and is unafraid; he is also afraid. Grahame will not tidy the feeling into one thing, because awe is this doubleness: love and fear together, the “fear of the Lord” that is love at its most intense.

5

“Instant oblivion”

The vision is not allowed to last or to be kept. A breeze’s soft touch brings “instant oblivion”: the animals will wake remembering nothing but a vague, aching happiness. For Grahame, forgetting is a mercy: the memory of having seen a god would be too much to carry back into ordinary life. The passage suggests that some experiences are too great to live alongside, and must be given and then taken away.

6

“The gift of forgetfulness”

The chapter names its own theme: forgetfulness as “the last best gift”. It is a strange and serious idea for a children’s book: that the kindest thing a revelation can do is fade. Pan bestows it, Grahame writes, “lest the awful remembrance should remain and grow, and overshadow mirth and pleasure, and the great haunting memory should spoil all the after-lives” of the little animals. The everyday riverbank life the book loves can only continue if the glimpse of something vast behind it is not retained. Grahame protects his small pastoral world by making its one transcendent moment impossible to keep.

What the wording changes

Pan on the riverbank

The Wind in the Willows is remembered for Toad (the motor-cars, the prison escape, the comedy), but at its centre Grahame placed this chapter, which has almost nothing to do with the plot and belongs to a different order of writing. “The Piper at the Gates of Dawn” is a full-dress religious vision: the great god Pan, described with the reverence of a hymn, encountered at sunrise by two small animals who bow down and worship. Many readers, then and since, have found it the most beautiful thing in the book; some have found it bafflingly out of key. Both reactions respond to the same fact: Grahame smuggled a mystical experience into a talking-animal story and made no apology for it.

Grahame will not simplify awe. Popular religion often splits the divine into a fearsome God or a comforting one; Grahame keeps both, and lets the Rat stammer the contradiction without resolving it. That is closer to how awe actually feels, terrifying and attractive together, than most explicitly religious writing manages, and Grahame does it through a water-rat on a riverbank, in a book for children. The pastoral world of the Thames-side animals turns out to have a god in it, and the god is love and dread together.

Then comes the “gift of forgetfulness”, the chapter’s deepest and strangest idea. Grahame does not let his characters keep the vision, and the reason he gives (that the memory would poison their ordinary happiness with unbearable awe) doubles as a theory of why the book itself works. The Wind in the Willows is finally a hymn to the small, safe, cyclical pleasures of home and river and season; it can only remain so if the glimpse of infinity at its centre is granted and withdrawn. The animals forget so that the riverbank can go on being enough. The book touches the transcendent and then, tenderly, chooses the everyday.

Take one question away

The Rat is unafraid and afraid in the same sentence. Is Grahame confused about awe, or has he caught something about it that clearer writing would miss?

Before reading

Pair the features.

List the details of Pan’s appearance and mark each as fearsome or gentle: curved horns, kindly eyes, stern nose, half-smile. Grahame pairs them deliberately. The pairing is the passage’s whole idea of the divine.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Hold the contradiction.

  1. Read the Rat’s reply aloud, honouring every dash.
  2. State the contradiction plainly: he is ____ and also ____.
  3. Decide whether the two feelings cancel, coexist, or are the same feeling.
  4. Discuss why the chapter ends by erasing the memory: is it mercy, or loss?
Essay bridge

The chapter that does not fit.

For an essay on the book’s design, ask why Grahame set this mystical vision between chapters of Toad’s farce. Consider what the riverbank’s cosy pastoral needs from a glimpse of the infinite, and why the “gift of forgetfulness” is what lets the ordinary world of the book survive its own most sublime moment.
