Close reading 76 · The Legend of Sleepy Hollow

“The cognomen of Crane was not inapplicable”

Before the headless horseman, before the pumpkin, Irving gives his schoolmaster a body—and builds it, joint by joint, out of comic similes until the man is a scarecrow the reader can already see running.

Washington IrvingThe Legend of Sleepy Hollow

Published in 1820 in The Sketch Book of Geoffrey Crayon, Gent., the collection that made Irving the first American writer with a European reputation. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: read the portrait for its similes—there are five, and each turns a man into a thing. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Sleepy HollowIchabod

The cognomen of Crane was not inapplicable to his person.1 He was tall, but exceedingly lank, with narrow shoulders, long arms and legs, hands that dangled a mile out of his sleeves, feet that might have served for shovels2, and his whole frame most loosely hung together. His head was small, and flat at top, with huge ears, large green glassy eyes, and a long snipe nose, so that it looked like a weather-cock perched upon his spindle neck to tell which way the wind blew.3 To see him striding along the profile of a hill on a windy day, with his clothes bagging and fluttering about him, one might have mistaken him for the genius of famine descending upon the earth4, or some scarecrow eloped from a cornfield.5

The Legend of Sleepy Hollow · public-domain text

1

A name that fits

“Cognomen” is Latin for a surname, and the mock-learned word sets the tone: Irving’s narrator is a genial antiquarian, amused by his own vocabulary. “Not inapplicable” is a double negative doing comic work—the name Crane fits perfectly, and the narrator says so as mildly as possible. The whole portrait will be delivered in this voice of polite understatement wrapped around ruthless caricature.

2

Hands and feet

Hands “that dangled a mile out of his sleeves”; feet “that might have served for shovels”. The hyperbole is American tall-tale, and the details are practical: sleeves too short, feet like tools. Ichabod is a Connecticut Yankee schoolmaster, poor and made of angles, and Irving assembles him from the extremities inward, as a caricaturist would draw him.

3

The weather-cock

The head is small, flat, huge-eared, green-eyed, snipe-nosed, and the simile turns it into a weather-vane on a “spindle neck”—an object that swings whichever way the wind blows. The image is the character’s psychology in a picture: Ichabod is credulous, superstitious, and will be turned by every tale of ghosts the Dutch farmers tell him. The body predicts the plot.

4

The genius of famine

Seen on a hilltop in wind, he might be “the genius of famine descending upon the earth”—an allegorical spirit of hunger. The joke has a point beneath it: Ichabod is always hungry, and the story is largely about his appetite for the Van Tassel farm’s produce, and its heiress. Famine walking the earth is exactly what he is, from the farmers’ point of view.

5

Scarecrow eloped from a cornfield

The last simile is the one everyone remembers, and “eloped” makes it: not a scarecrow blown down, but one that has run off, as if with a lover. The word plants Ichabod’s ambition—to carry off Katrina Van Tassel—and foretells his end, fleeing through the night from a pumpkin-headed rider. A scarecrow is a thing that frightens; Irving’s is a thing that gets frightened, and runs.

What the wording changes

A caricature that contains its own story.

Irving’s comic method is to describe a man as a set of objects. Shovels, a weather-cock, a spirit of famine, a scarecrow: Ichabod Crane is never shown from inside, and the reader is never asked to feel with him. This is deliberate. “The Legend of Sleepy Hollow” is a tale about a community expelling an outsider—a lean, bookish, ambitious Yankee among comfortable Dutch farmers—and the narrator’s genial cruelty toward Ichabod is the community’s. We are invited to laugh at the schoolmaster before the story gives Brom Bones a reason to scare him out of the valley, and by the time the horseman rides, we are on the valley’s side.

Each simile also carries a piece of plot. The weather-cock predicts a mind turned by every ghost story; the genius of famine predicts the appetite that draws him to the Van Tassel table; the eloped scarecrow predicts both the courtship and the flight. Irving was writing sketches, not novels, and the sketch-writer’s art is to make a portrait do the work of a chapter. This paragraph is the whole legend in miniature—a hungry, credulous, angular man who will be frightened off by a trick—delivered as a description of his hands and head.

The story’s historical place is worth remembering. The Sketch Book appeared in 1819–20, when American literature barely existed as a category, and it was the first American book to be read with respect in Britain. Irving achieved that partly by borrowing European forms—the German ghost tale, the English essayist’s persona—and setting them in the Hudson valley, with Dutch farmers and a Yankee schoolmaster instead of barons and monks. Ichabod’s portrait is where the transplant takes: a comic type as old as Chaucer’s clerks, drawn in a landscape that had never been drawn before.

Take one question away

Irving makes us laugh at Ichabod before anyone in the story mistreats him. Is the portrait affectionate comedy, or the narrator taking the valley’s side against the outsider?

Before reading

List the things.

Write down every object Ichabod is compared to: shovels, a weather-cock, a spirit, a scarecrow. Then ask what each object does—digs, turns, starves, frightens—and match it to something Ichabod does in the story.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Redraw the portrait.

  1. Sketch Ichabod from the paragraph alone, feature by feature.
  2. Then write the same man described sympathetically, from inside.
  3. Compare: what does the comic version know that the sympathetic one cannot say?
  4. Decide whose eyes the narrator is using—his own, or the Dutch farmers’.
Essay bridge

The outsider in the valley.

For an essay on the story, connect this portrait to Ichabod’s courtship of Katrina, Brom Bones’s rivalry, and the ambiguous ending in which the schoolmaster simply vanishes. Ask whether the legend is a ghost story, a comedy of a community ridding itself of a stranger, or a story that lets the reader choose.
