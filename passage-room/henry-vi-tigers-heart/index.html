Passage Room/Henry VI, Part 3/Tiger’s heart

Close reading 72 · Henry VI, Part 3

“O tiger’s heart wrapped in a woman’s hide!”

A captured duke, crowned with paper and handed a cloth dipped in his murdered son’s blood, curses the queen who did it. The line at the speech’s heart is the earliest surviving reference to Shakespeare as a writer—a rival quoted it in 1592 to call him an upstart crow.

William ShakespeareAct 1, Scene 4

Probably written around 1591, among the earliest of the plays. Richard, Duke of York, taken at Wakefield, speaks to Queen Margaret after she has shown him the blood of his youngest son Rutland. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: notice the speech switch from cataloguing her faults to weeping, and where. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Henry VI, Part 31.4

YorkShe-wolf of France, but worse than wolves of France1,
Whose tongue more poisons than the adder’s tooth!
How ill-beseeming is it in thy sex
To triumph like an Amazonian trull
5Upon their woes whom Fortune captivates!
…
’Tis beauty that doth oft make women proud;
But God he knows thy share thereof is small.
’Tis virtue that doth make them most admired;
10The contrary doth make thee wondered at.
’Tis government that makes them seem divine;
The want thereof makes thee abominable.
Thou art as opposite to every good
As the Antipodes are unto us,
15Or as the south to the Septentrion.
O tiger’s heart wrapped in a woman’s hide!2
How couldst thou drain the life-blood of the child,
To bid the father wipe his eyes withal3,
And yet be seen to bear a woman’s face?
20Women are soft, mild, pitiful, and flexible;
Thou stern, obdurate, flinty, rough, remorseless.4
Bid’st thou me rage? Why, now thou hast thy wish:
Wouldst have me weep? Why, now thou hast thy will;
For raging wind blows up incessant showers,
25And when the rage allays, the rain begins.5
These tears are my sweet Rutland’s obsequies6,
And every drop cries vengeance for his death
’Gainst thee, fell Clifford, and thee, false Frenchwoman.

Henry VI, Part 3, Act 1, Scene 4 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · seven lines omitted at the ellipsis · lines numbered from the start of the speech

1

The she-wolf

Margaret of Anjou is French, a queen consort who has taken command of her husband’s war, and York’s first blow is a beast-image with a national sneer inside it: a she-wolf, “but worse than wolves of France”. The insult is gendered and xenophobic together, and the play’s Margaret earns some of it—she has just had a child killed and mocked the father with a paper crown. Shakespeare gives the tyrant’s victim the tyrant’s own vocabulary.

2

The line with a history

In 1592 the playwright Robert Greene, dying and bitter, warned his fellow university wits against “an upstart crow, beautified with our feathers, that with his Tiger’s heart wrapped in a player’s hide” thinks himself the only Shake-scene in the country. The parody of this line is the first surviving mention of Shakespeare as a dramatist—proof that the speech was famous enough, within a year or two of its writing, to be quoted with a twist. The actor from Stratford had arrived, and a rival’s envy is the record.

3

The handkerchief

Margaret has given York a napkin dipped in the blood of Rutland, his youngest son, and told him to dry his tears with it. York’s question, how could you drain a child’s blood ‘to bid the father wipe his eyes withal’, names the exact cruelty: the killing, and then using it as a prop. The scene is a small theatre of torture, with a paper crown and a bloody cloth for properties, and the play’s wars will be fought in this key from now on.

4

Two lists

York sets what women are—“soft, mild, pitiful, and flexible”—against what Margaret is: “stern, obdurate, flinty, rough, remorseless”. Four adjectives against five, a line each, one ideal and one indictment. The rhetoric is early Shakespeare at its most schematic, and it is meant to be: the young playwright is showing his command of the balanced line, and the audience is meant to feel the second list land like blows.

5

Wind, then rain

The speech turns. Margaret has dared him to rage and to weep; he does both, and explains the sequence with weather—the wind of rage blows up the showers, and when the wind drops, the rain falls. It is a simile for grief that has stayed true: fury first, then tears. York’s eloquence, which has been all attack, becomes all sorrow in two lines.

6

Tears as funeral rites

“These tears are my sweet Rutland’s obsequies”: the boy will get no funeral, so his father’s weeping must serve. And every drop “cries vengeance”—the tears are a curse as well as a rite. Northumberland, watching, admits he can hardly keep from tears himself. The speech has moved a hostile witness; the queen it is addressed to has York stabbed a few lines later.

What the wording changes

Early Shakespeare, and the first time anyone noticed him.

The three parts of Henry VI are among Shakespeare’s earliest plays, and this scene is the moment the early style comes into its own. The rhetoric is formal—balanced antitheses, catalogued adjectives, a simile carefully explained—and the situation is horrifying: a paper crown, a bloody handkerchief, a father mocked before his murder. The young playwright fits the two together, and the effect is that York’s speech becomes a set-piece of grief, built so deliberately that its form is part of its dignity. This is not yet the flexible verse of the great tragedies; it is verse that stands and delivers, and the audience of 1591 loved it.

The evidence of that love is Greene’s parody. When a dying rival needs a line to sneer at, he chooses this one—and the sneer only works if the line is on everyone’s lips. “Tiger’s heart wrapped in a player’s hide” is the earliest notice of Shakespeare in print, and it is an attack: an actor presuming to write, a “Johannes factotum” who thinks himself the only “Shake-scene”. Every biography of Shakespeare begins here, with a jealous colleague quoting Margaret’s tiger heart back at the man who wrote it.

The scene also establishes Margaret, who will grow across four plays into the great curser of the first tetralogy and, in Richard III, a prophetess of doom. York’s catalogue of her unwomanliness is the play’s own assessment as much as his, and modern readers rightly notice how much of the horror is that a woman is doing this. Yet the play does not simply endorse the misogyny. Margaret is fighting for her son’s crown as York is fighting for his; the paper crown she mocks him with is the one he claimed. The tiger’s heart is a fair description, and the scene makes clear that everyone at Wakefield has one.

Take one question away

York curses Margaret for being unwomanly while she does what every man in the play does. Is the speech an indictment of her, or of the war that made her?

Before reading

Find the turn.

The speech attacks for twenty-one lines and then weeps. Mark the exact line where the voice changes, and the simile that explains the change. Early Shakespeare signposts his transitions; this one is a weather forecast.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Greene’s parody.

  1. Read Greene’s 1592 sentence about the “upstart crow” and find its source in this speech.
  2. Ask what the parody tells us about the play’s reputation.
  3. Ask what it tells us about Greene.
  4. Write a one-line parody of your own that would only work if the original were famous.
Essay bridge

Margaret across four plays.

For an essay on the first tetralogy, follow Margaret from the French bride of Part 1 through this scene to her prophecies in Richard III. Ask how the plays use her—as villain, as victim of a man’s war, as the one voice that remembers everything—and whether York’s tiger heart is her whole character or the part the men can see.
