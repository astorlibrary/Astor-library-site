Close reading 82 · Timon of Athens

“Thus much of this will make / Black white, foul fair, wrong right”

A ruined man digging in the woods for roots strikes gold, and instead of rejoicing delivers the most savage account of what money does ever put on a stage. Karl Marx copied it into his notebooks.

William Shakespeare (with Thomas Middleton)Act 4, Scene 3

Probably written around 1606, and now generally thought a collaboration with Middleton. Timon, bankrupted by his own generosity and abandoned by the friends he fed, has left Athens to live in a cave. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: count the reversals—the speech is a list of things gold turns into their opposites. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Timon of Athens, Act 4, Scene 3 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered within the extract

1

No idle votarist

Timon is digging for roots to eat and finds gold. His first reaction is refusal: “No, gods, I am no idle votarist”—not a man who prays for one thing and takes another. He asked for roots and means it. The speech that follows is not the joy of a treasure-finder but the fury of a man who has been handed, at the bottom of his ruin, the very substance that caused it.

2

The inversions

“Thus much”—a handful—will make “black white, foul fair, wrong right, / Base noble, old young, coward valiant”. Six antitheses in two lines, each a pair of moral or physical opposites that gold can swap. The rhythm is a machine: the line beats out reversal after reversal, and the audience hears money as a device for making every word mean its contrary. It is the most compressed account of corruption in Shakespeare.

3

The yellow slave

Gold is a “slave”—it serves whoever holds it—and what it will do for its master is “knit and break religions”, bless the cursed, make leprosy “adored”, seat thieves on the bench beside senators. The catalogue moves through church, body and law: no institution is exempt. Timon’s bitterness has a precise sociology; each line names a sphere where he watched money override worth.

4

The widow re-wed

Gold “makes the wappened widow wed again”—wappened is worn out, exhausted—and embalms a diseased body “to th’ April day again”, restoring spring to what the spittle-house (the hospital) would vomit at. The image is grotesque and exact: money as cosmetic, as embalming fluid, as the thing that makes the unmarriageable marry. Timon’s disgust is bodily, as his generosity once was.

5

The common whore of mankind

The earth that yields gold is “thou common whore of mankind”, setting nations at odds. Timon turns from cursing gold to cursing the ground it came from, and the misogyny of the image—the earth as prostitute—is of a piece with the play’s whole disgust at exchange. Yet he keeps some of the gold. He will use it to pay Alcibiades to destroy Athens: the yellow slave has a new master, and its right nature, he decides, is ruin.

What the wording changes

A speech on money that outlived its play.

Timon of Athens is among the least performed of Shakespeare’s tragedies—unfinished in places, probably collaborative, its hero a man who gives everything away and then hates everyone. But this speech has a life outside the theatre. Karl Marx quoted it at length in the 1844 manuscripts and again in Capital, as the sharpest account of money’s power to turn qualities into their opposites: gold makes the ugly beautiful, the old young, the coward brave, because it is “the universal whore” that couples with anything. Marx found in Timon the poet of what he called the “alienating” power of money, and the passage has been part of the critique of capitalism ever since.

The speech’s structure is the argument. It is built almost entirely of inversions—black white, foul fair—because that is what Timon has learned money does: it does not add value but exchanges one value for another, so that a thief with gold is a senator and a leper with gold is loved. Timon’s early generosity assumed that money expressed friendship; his ruin taught him that it replaced it. The bitterness is the bitterness of a man who discovers that the thing he thought was a sign of love was the whole of it.

The scene’s irony is that finding the gold changes nothing in Timon except his instrument. He does not go back to Athens; he uses the treasure to fund Alcibiades’ army against it, and to pay whores and bandits to spread disease. Gold, which made him beloved, will now make him destructive, and the play refuses to let him learn anything gentler. He dies in his cave, leaving an epitaph that curses the reader. It is the darkest ending in Shakespeare, and this speech—the yellow slave, the earth as whore—is where its darkness is given a theory.

Take one question away

Timon curses gold for making black white—and keeps some to buy the ruin of Athens. Has he seen through money, or only changed what he wants it for?

Before reading

Count the opposites.

List every pair of reversed terms in the speech, starting with black/white. Then ask what they have in common: are they moral, physical, social? The catalogue is Timon’s theory of money, and the theory is that money is a machine for swapping.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Test it on the play.

  1. Take one of Timon’s claims (thieves become senators; the cursed are blessed).
  2. Find the moment in Acts 1–3 where the play showed it happening.
  3. Ask whether Timon’s generosity in Act 1 was itself a version of the same swapping.
  4. Decide whether the speech is insight or self-pity—or both.
