Passage Room/A Christmas Carol/Scrooge and the prisons

Close reading 09 · A Christmas Carol

“Are there no prisons?”

Two visitors ask Scrooge to help people in immediate need. He answers with the names of institutions. Dickens lets the short questions reveal a whole habit of thought: distance, bookkeeping and a refusal to see poverty as a claim made by one person upon another.

Charles DickensStave One

Published in December 1843. The exchange takes place in Scrooge’s counting-house before the first spirit appears.

How to read this page: notice who speaks in statements and who answers in questions. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to follow the distance Scrooge places between himself and the poor.

A Christmas CarolStave One

“At this festive season of the year, Mr. Scrooge,” said the gentleman, taking up a pen, “it is more than usually desirable1 that we should make some slight provision for the Poor and destitute, who suffer greatly at the present time. Many thousands are in want of common necessaries; hundreds of thousands are in want of common comforts2, sir.”

“Are there no prisons?3” asked Scrooge.

“Plenty of prisons,” said the gentleman, laying down the pen again. […]

“Oh! I was afraid, from what you said at first, that something had occurred to stop them in their useful course4,” said Scrooge. “I’m very glad to hear it.” […]

“If they would rather die,” said Scrooge, “they had better do it, and decrease the surplus population5.”

A Christmas Carol, Stave One · public-domain text · ellipses mark two shortened exchanges

1

The season sharpens the claim

“Usually” makes poverty continuing rather than exceptional; “more than” makes winter and Christmas an added pressure. The appeal joins present suffering to a season that celebrates generosity.

2

Not luxuries

“Common” is repeated. Food, warmth and ordinary comfort are described as minimum needs shared by many, while the rising numbers make deprivation impossible to dismiss as a rare case.

3

A question that avoids the question

The collectors ask what Scrooge will give. He does not answer. His question moves responsibility away from a meeting between people and towards institutions he already helps fund through taxation.

4

Cold administrative language

Scrooge calls prisons, workhouses, the Treadmill and the Poor Law “useful”. “Course” makes their operation sound orderly and impersonal; the people subjected to that course disappear from his sentence.

5

A person becomes a statistic

“Surplus population” treats lives as an excess quantity. Dickens later makes the Ghost of Christmas Present return Scrooge’s own phrases to him, forcing him to hear their cruelty when Tiny Tim is at risk.

What the wording changes

The visitors ask for provision. Scrooge offers a system.

The scene is built from interruptions. The collector’s long sentences name “the Poor and destitute”, their want and a practical response. Scrooge’s short questions keep breaking that account apart. He can recognise categories—prison, workhouse, law—without meeting the human claim put before him.

The counting-house matters. Pens, lists and money belong to ordinary business, but the visitor also takes up a pen to record an act of giving. Scrooge refuses even a name on that list. His later claim that he wants to be “left alone” depends upon treating society as a set of payments already settled.

Dickens does not leave the exchange behind. When the Ghost asks whether Ignorance and Want have any refuge, it throws “Are there no prisons?” back at Scrooge. Repetition becomes judgment: the words have not changed, but the person forced to hear them has.
