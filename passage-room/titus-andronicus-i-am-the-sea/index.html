Passage Room/Titus Andronicus/I am the sea

Close reading 83 · Titus Andronicus

“I am the sea. Hark how her sighs doth flow!”

Told to let reason govern his grief, a father who has just seen his mutilated daughter answers that grief has no reason and he has no limits. Shakespeare’s earliest tragedy finds, for a moment, the language its horrors need.

William ShakespeareAct 3, Scene 1

Probably written around 1591–92, and hugely popular in its day. Titus has just been shown Lavinia, raped and mutilated; his brother Marcus urges restraint. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

Content note

The play stages rape, mutilation and murder, and this scene follows directly on Lavinia’s injuries. The reading treats the speech as what it is: a father’s response to atrocity.

How to read this page: notice how Titus turns Marcus’s word “reason” over and refuses it. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Titus Andronicus3.1

TitusIs not my sorrow deep, having no bottom?
Then be my passions bottomless with them.1

MarcusBut yet let reason govern thy lament.

TitusIf there were reason for these miseries,
Then into limits could I bind my woes.2
When heaven doth weep, doth not the earth o’erflow?
If the winds rage, doth not the sea wax mad,
Threatening the welkin with his big-swol’n face?
And wilt thou have a reason for this coil?
I am the sea. Hark how her sighs doth flow!
She is the weeping welkin, I the earth.3
Then must my sea be moved with her sighs;
Then must my earth with her continual tears
Become a deluge, overflowed and drowned;
For why my bowels cannot hide her woes,
But like a drunkard must I vomit them.4
Then give me leave, for losers will have leave
To ease their stomachs with their bitter tongues.

Enter a Messenger with two heads and a hand.

MessengerWorthy Andronicus, ill art thou repaid
For that good hand thou sent’st the emperor.
Here are the heads of thy two noble sons,
And here’s thy hand, in scorn to thee sent back.5

Titus Andronicus, Act 3, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

Bottomless

Titus asks whether his sorrow has a bottom, and answers his own question: it has none, so his passions must have none either. The logic is that of a man matching his feeling to his loss, and refusing to be smaller than it. “Bottomless” is the speech’s key: everything that follows describes what happens when the container breaks.

2

No reason, no limits

Marcus says “let reason govern thy lament”; Titus replies that if there were reason—cause, sense—for the miseries, he could bind them “into limits”. The pun is exact: reason as rationality and reason as justification are the same word, and neither exists. Grief that has no explanation cannot be given a shape. The young Shakespeare found in a play of horrors an argument about why horror cannot be reasoned with.

3

The weather of grief

Heaven weeps and the earth floods; winds rage and the sea goes mad. Then the identification: “I am the sea”—Lavinia is the weeping sky, Titus the earth and the sea beneath it, and her sighs move his tides. The image makes father and daughter one weather-system, and the “big-swol’n face” of the sea is his own. It is the play’s first great passage of lyric grief, and it comes after two acts of spectacle.

4

The drunkard’s vomit

The elevated weather-images crash into a body: his “bowels cannot hide her woes”, and “like a drunkard must I vomit them”. The shift is deliberate and characteristic of the play, which keeps setting grand rhetoric beside physical disgust. Grief is not only a flood in the heavens; it is nausea, and the man must be sick. “Losers will have leave” to purge—the proverb makes the lament a right.

5

Two heads and a hand

The stage direction is among the most notorious in Shakespeare. Titus has cut off his own hand and sent it to the emperor as ransom for his sons; a messenger returns it with their heads. The speech about bottomless grief is answered by a new bottom. From this point Titus stops weeping and begins to laugh, and the play turns from lament to revenge. The scene has shown that grief has no reason; what follows shows that revenge has no limit either.

What the wording changes

An early play finds its voice in the middle of its horrors.

Titus Andronicus was a hit in the 1590s and an embarrassment for three centuries afterwards—too bloody, too crude, surely not really Shakespeare’s. Twentieth-century productions restored it, and this scene is much of the reason. The first two acts are a machine of atrocity: a captured queen’s son sacrificed, her revenge plotted, Lavinia raped and mutilated, Titus’s sons framed for murder. Act 3 is where the machine stops and the play looks at what it has done. Titus’s speech to Marcus is the moment a revenge tragedy discovers that its victims have interiors.

The rhetoric is early Shakespeare—balanced questions, cosmic comparisons, a proverb to close—but the emotion is not schematic. The pun on “reason” is a genuine thought about grief: that it cannot be limited because it cannot be explained. And the descent from sea and sky to bowels and vomit is the play’s signature move, refusing to let suffering stay beautiful. Shakespeare would refine this in Lear, where Lear’s storm speech does the same work with more control; here the young playwright is discovering that the body has a claim on the language of grief.

The messenger’s entrance then does something the mature tragedies rarely risk: it answers a great speech with a grotesque prop. Two heads and a hand arrive, and Titus laughs. The laughter has been called the play’s most modern moment—the point at which suffering exceeds what lament can hold and becomes something else. Whether that something is madness, revenge or a kind of dreadful clarity, the speech about bottomless sorrow has prepared it: a man who has said there are no limits is about to demonstrate that he meant it.

Take one question away

Marcus asks for reason; Titus says grief has none. Is the speech a failure of restraint, or the play’s argument that restraint is the wrong demand to make of the wronged?

Before reading

Follow the water.

Mark every image of water: weeping heaven, overflowing earth, mad sea, sighs that flow, deluge. Then mark the one bodily image at the end. The speech moves from the elements to the stomach; ask what that descent does.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Reason and reason.

  1. Take Marcus’s line: what does he mean by “reason”?
  2. Take Titus’s reply: what does he mean by it?
  3. Ask whether the pun is a quibble or the argument.
  4. Stage the messenger’s entrance: how long before Titus laughs, and why?
Essay bridge

From Titus to Lear.

For an essay on Shakespeare’s development, set “I am the sea” beside Lear’s “Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks”. Both fathers turn grief into weather; ask what the fifteen years between them changed in how Shakespeare let a man’s suffering speak.
