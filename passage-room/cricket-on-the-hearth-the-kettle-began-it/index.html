Passage Room/The Cricket on the Hearth/The kettle began it

Close reading 90 · The Cricket on the Hearth

“The kettle began it!”

Dickens opens a Christmas book by picking a fight with one of his own characters about which household object started singing first. It is a joke, a piece of narrative theory, and the warmest first sentence he ever wrote.

Charles DickensChirp the First · the opening

Published in December 1845, the third of the five Christmas books, and the most popular of them on stage in Dickens’s lifetime. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: hear the narrator’s voice as a person in the room, insisting. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Cricket on the HearthChirp the First

The kettle began it! Don’t tell me what Mrs. Peerybingle said. I know better.1 Mrs. Peerybingle may leave it on record to the end of time that she couldn’t say which of them began it; but I say the kettle did. I ought to know, I hope? The kettle began it, full five minutes by the little waxy-faced Dutch clock in the corner, before the Cricket uttered a chirp.2

As if the clock hadn’t finished striking, and the convulsive little Hay-maker at the top of it, jerking away right and left with a scythe in front of a Moorish Palace, hadn’t mowed down half an acre of imaginary grass3 before the Cricket joined in at all!

Why, I am not naturally positive. Every one knows that I wouldn’t set my own opinion against the opinion of Mrs. Peerybingle, unless I were quite sure, on any account whatever. Nothing should induce me. But, this is a question of fact.4 And the fact is, that the kettle began it at least five minutes before the Cricket gave any sign of being in existence. Contradict me, and I’ll say ten.

Let me narrate exactly how it happened. I should have proceeded to do so, in my very first word, but for this plain consideration—if I am to tell a story I must begin at the beginning; and how is it possible to begin at the beginning without beginning at the kettle?5

The Cricket on the Hearth, Chirp the First · public-domain text

1

A quarrel in the first line

The book opens mid-argument. Someone, Mrs Peerybingle, the carrier’s young wife, has apparently said she cannot tell whether the kettle or the cricket began; the narrator overrules her before we know who she is. ‘I know better’ is comic self-assertion, and it sets up the voice: a narrator inside his story, a guest by the fire with opinions.

2

Five minutes by the Dutch clock

The evidence is precise and domestic: five minutes, measured by “the little waxy-faced Dutch clock in the corner”. Dickens’s comedy of exactness—timing a kettle’s song against a cricket’s chirp—makes the cottage a world with its own chronology, and the clock its official record. The reader learns the room by the instruments in it before any person appears.

3

The Hay-maker

The clock has an automaton on top, a little figure with a scythe who jerks “right and left” in front of a painted Moorish palace and has “mowed down half an acre of imaginary grass” while the cricket kept silent. The detail is pure Dickens: an object given a life and a labour, so that the room is full of small workers. The imaginary acre is the narrator’s wit and the Hay-maker’s whole career.

4

A question of fact

“But, this is a question of fact.” The narrator has just protested that he is “not naturally positive” and would never contradict Mrs Peerybingle—and then does, on grounds of fact, and threatens to raise his claim from five minutes to ten if disputed. The mock-legal solemnity about a kettle is the joke, and beneath it is Dickens’s abiding belief that small things are worth being exact about.

5

Beginning at the beginning

The paragraph ends with narrative theory disguised as a domestic joke: a story must begin at the beginning, and the beginning is the kettle. Dickens is explaining why he has spent a page on a kettle—because the story begins where the household’s sounds begin—and making the explanation part of the story. The narrator’s pedantry is the hearth’s hospitality: nothing here is too small to be told.

What the wording changes

A narrator who lives in the house.

The Christmas books gave Dickens a form he used differently each year, and The Cricket on the Hearth is the domestic one—no Scrooge, no ghosts of social conscience, but a carrier’s cottage, a young wife, a suspected infidelity and a cricket whose chirp is the spirit of the home. The opening declares the scale. Where A Christmas Carol begins with a death and The Chimes with a church tower, this book begins with a kettle, and with a narrator so involved in the household that he will argue with its mistress about the order of its noises.

The voice is the book’s invention. Dickens’s narrators are usually large—omniscient, ironic, capable of surveying London from above. This one is small and near, a presence at the fireside who “ought to know”, who protests his modesty while insisting on his facts, who threatens to raise five minutes to ten. He keeps the reader company more than he guides, and the comedy of his insistence is the comedy of a man who loves the room he is describing. The kettle and cricket will become, over the next pages, a duet; the narrator’s quarrel about who started it is his way of joining in.

The book was Dickens’s most performed Christmas story—there were seventeen stage versions in London in the first year—and this opening explains its appeal. It is theatre already: a set (the cottage), sound effects (kettle, cricket, clock), a chorus (the narrator) and a mystery held back. Dickens spends a page on the sounds of a room before a human being speaks, and by the time John Peerybingle comes in from the cold the reader has been living in his house for five minutes. By the Dutch clock, of course.

Take one question away

The narrator argues with a character about a kettle. What kind of storyteller does that make him, and what kind of book does it promise?

Before reading

List the sounds.

Note every sound in the passage: the kettle, the cricket, the clock striking, the Hay-maker jerking. The opening is a soundscape before it is a scene. Ask what a reader knows about a house from its noises.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

The narrator’s character.

  1. List everything the narrator claims about himself: not positive, would never contradict, ought to know.
  2. List everything he actually does.
  3. Ask what the gap tells you about him.
  4. Rewrite the first paragraph in a neutral omniscient voice and say what is lost.
Essay bridge

Five Christmas openings.

For an essay on the Christmas books, compare the first sentences of all five—“Marley was dead”, the bells of The Chimes, the kettle, the Battle of Life’s field, the “Everybody said so” of The Haunted Man. Ask how each opening sets the scale and voice of its book, and why the kettle is the gentlest.
