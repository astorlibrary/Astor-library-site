“Is this a dagger”

Macbeth is alone, waiting for Lady Macbeth’s bell. He knows the room he means to enter and the weapon he means to use. The vision makes that intention look as though it has stepped outside him.

William ShakespeareAct 2, Scene 1

Probably written around 1606. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text and has been checked against the Folger text.

How to read this page: read the speech once without stopping. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Macbeth2.1

Is this a dagger which I see before me,
The handle toward my hand1? Come, let me clutch thee2:—
I have thee not, and yet I see thee still.
Art thou not, fatal vision, sensible
To feeling as to sight? or art thou but
A dagger of the mind, a false creation3,
Proceeding from the heat-oppressed brain?

I see thee yet, in form as palpable
As this which now I draw.
Thou marshall’st me the way that I was going4;
And such an instrument I was to use.
Mine eyes are made the fools o’ the other senses,
Or else worth all the rest: I see thee still;
And on thy blade and dudgeon, gouts of blood,
Which was not so before5.—There’s no such thing.

Macbeth, Act 2, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

An object arranged for taking

The blade does not point at Macbeth; the handle faces his hand. Before the vision moves anywhere, its position makes it look offered, as if the imagined object has been designed around the action he is considering.

2

A question becomes a command

Macbeth has not answered whether the dagger is real before he orders it to come. “Let me” can sound like permission as well as intention. The attempt to touch is a test, but its grammar also begins a relation with the weapon.

3

The mind makes what it calls false

“Creation” gives the brain productive power. Macbeth can identify the dagger as mental and unreal without making it powerless: the false object remains visible, detailed and addressed as “thou”.

4

Led along an existing route

“Marshall’st” makes the dagger a guide, but “was going” returns us to Macbeth’s prior intention. The vision does not choose the destination. It appears on the path to a murder already planned.

5

The future stains the image

“Gouts” are drops; “dudgeon” is the dagger’s hilt. Blood spreads across both blade and handle only after Macbeth draws his real weapon. What has not yet happened enters his sight as though it were already evidence.

What the wording changes

The vision leads towards a place Macbeth was already going

The speech begins with an object under examination. Macbeth tests sight against touch, asks whether the vision is “sensible” and proposes a physical cause in the “heat-oppressed brain”. Yet the experiment does not clear the image. The more carefully he reasons, the more particular the dagger becomes.

Grammar helps that change. “This” becomes “thee”, and then “Thou”. An uncertain object becomes the subject of an active verb: it marshals. At the same time, Macbeth admits that both route and instrument belonged to his intention before the apparition appeared. The dagger can exert pressure without being allowed to carry all the responsibility.

Blood arrives last. Macbeth knows it was not present before, so perception changes within the speech. The imagined consequence has coloured the imagined weapon. When he says “There’s no such thing”, he may dismiss the dagger as an object, but he cannot dismiss the “bloody business” that has informed it.

Take one question away

Does the dagger tempt Macbeth towards murder, or allow him to see the intention that he has been trying to place elsewhere?

Before reading

Track the sentence forms

Underline every question once, each statement twice and the command with a box. Without explaining the dagger, decide where Macbeth sounds least uncertain. What has changed in the sentence at that point?

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Stage three visions

  1. Read the passage as fear.
  2. Read it as invitation.
  3. Read it as self-deception.
  4. After each, observers name the gesture, pace and exact phrase that made the interpretation possible.
  5. Choose the reading that best explains “the way that I was going”.
Essay bridge

Keep influence and choice together

For an essay on the supernatural or responsibility, place this speech between Macbeth’s “I am settled” in 1.7 and his movement towards the bell at the end of 2.1. The question is not only whether the dagger is real, but what Macbeth does after naming several reasons to doubt it.
