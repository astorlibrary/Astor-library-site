Passage Room/Henry VIII/Wolsey’s farewell

Close reading 80 · Henry VIII

“Farewell? A long farewell to all my greatness!”

The most powerful man in England after the king has just been stripped of office. Alone for the first time in the play, he describes his own career as a plant, a swimmer and a fallen angel—and then tells his servant he has never been happier.

William Shakespeare (with John Fletcher)Act 3, Scene 2

Written around 1613, probably in collaboration with Fletcher; a cannon fired during a performance burned the Globe to the ground. Cardinal Wolsey speaks after the lords have taken the great seal from him. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: watch the images change scale—from a garden to the sea to heaven. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Henry VIII, Act 3, Scene 2 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered from the start of the soliloquy · Cromwell’s first lines omitted

1

The word turned over

The lords have just said “fare you well” with a sneer. Wolsey picks the word up, looks at it—“Farewell?”—and turns it into the first word of his own elegy: “A long farewell to all my greatness.” The soliloquy begins by taking an enemy’s dismissal and making it a valediction. It is the last exercise of a politician’s skill: he cannot keep the power, but he can keep the last word.

2

Three days

“This is the state of man”: leaves today, blossom tomorrow, and on the third day “a frost, a killing frost”. A career compressed into a plant’s spring. The repetition of “frost” is the speech’s most quoted effect—the second one lands harder than the first, like a second night of cold. And “good easy man” is Wolsey pitying himself in the third person, which is how he will manage his fall: by watching it.

3

Boys on bladders

The second image is comic and cruel: children learning to swim on inflated bladders, out of their depth in “a sea of glory”. Wolsey reduces his whole ascent—butcher’s son to Lord Chancellor—to a boy paddling too far out on a float. “High-blown pride” then bursts the bladder. The great man’s self-image, for one moment, is of someone who never learned to swim.

4

The renunciation

“Vain pomp and glory of this world, I hate ye!” is the conversion of a worldly man, and the play wants us to hear both its sincerity and its convenience. He hates the pomp the moment it is taken from him. Yet “I feel my heart new opened” is not sarcasm; the speech is genuinely moving because it lets a man discover, too late, what he might have valued instead.

5

Like Lucifer

The third image is the largest: the courtier who falls from a prince’s favour “falls like Lucifer, / Never to hope again”. Wolsey, a cardinal, reaches for the fall of the brightest angel—pride punished by God—and applies it to himself. The comparison is grand, damning and slightly self-flattering all at once, which is the cardinal’s character in a line.

6

Never so happy

Cromwell, amazed, asks how he is. “Why, well. / Never so truly happy”—and the reason: “I know myself now.” The claim is the tragic pattern in miniature: self-knowledge arrives with the loss of everything that prevented it. Whether Wolsey is at peace or performing peace, the play lets us decide; the history it drew on records that he died on the road to London to face trial, saying he wished he had served God as diligently as the king.

What the wording changes

The fall of a great man, staged as a change of weather.

Henry VIII is a play of falls—Buckingham’s, Wolsey’s, Katherine’s—and this is the finest, partly because Wolsey has been the play’s least sympathetic figure until the moment he speaks alone. For two acts he has been the manipulator: arranging Buckingham’s execution, engineering the divorce, taxing the commons. Then his private letters to Rome fall into the king’s hands, the lords take back the great seal, and Shakespeare gives him the stage. The soliloquy’s effect is a reversal of sympathy so complete that audiences have always found Wolsey’s fall more moving than any of his victims’. That is the speech’s art, and its moral problem.

The three images—plant, swimmer, angel—are chosen for their scale. The plant is small, domestic, a matter of gardens and frosts; the swimmer is a child in a sea; Lucifer falls from heaven. Wolsey’s self-description grows as it goes, so that a career that began as a spring blossom ends as the cosmos’s greatest fall. The inflation is telling. Even in renunciation, the cardinal cannot describe his ruin without making it magnificent, and the play’s intelligence is to let the magnificence be both genuine grief and the last flourish of the pride it mourns.

The scene is usually attributed to Shakespeare in the collaboration with Fletcher, and it reads like late Shakespeare: the flexible verse, the mixed metaphors sliding into one another, the sudden plainness of “I know myself now”. The play as a whole is a pageant, ending in the christening of Elizabeth I and a prophecy of her reign; it was performed before her successor’s court. Wolsey’s farewell is the moment the pageant stops for a human face—a man who had everything, watching it go, and finding the words for it that no one else in the play can match.

Take one question away

Wolsey says he has never been so happy as in his ruin. Do we believe him—and does it matter whether he believes himself?

Before reading

Size the images.

List the three comparisons—plant, swimmer, Lucifer—and rank them by scale. Ask why Wolsey’s self-portrait grows larger as his fortunes shrink. The order is an argument about what pride does when it loses its object.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

The reversal of sympathy.

  1. List Wolsey’s actions in Acts 1–2 that the play condemns.
  2. Read the soliloquy and note where you begin to pity him.
  3. Ask whether the pity is earned by what he says or by the fact that he is alone.
  4. Decide whether Shakespeare has been fair to Wolsey’s victims.
Essay bridge

Falls in the late plays.

For an essay on Shakespeare’s last works, set Wolsey’s farewell beside Prospero’s renunciation of his magic and Leontes’ sixteen years of penance. Each is a powerful man surrendering power; ask how the late plays treat renunciation, and whether Wolsey’s is the one that comes too late to be redeemed.
