Close reading 29 · The Great Gatsby

“So we beat on”

On his last night in the East, Nick Carraway sits on Gatsby’s beach and watches the moon rise until the houses disappear. What he sees in their place is the oldest American sight there is—and the reason Gatsby’s dream was doomed before he dreamed it.

F. Scott FitzgeraldChapter IX · the final page

Published by Scribner’s in April 1925. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: watch the pronouns—the passage moves from “I” to “he” to “we”. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Great GatsbyChapter IX

And as the moon rose higher the inessential houses began to melt away until gradually I became aware of the old island here that flowered once for Dutch sailors’ eyes—a fresh, green breast of the new world1. Its vanished trees, the trees that had made way for Gatsby’s house, had once pandered in whispers to the last and greatest of all human dreams; for a transitory enchanted moment man must have held his breath in the presence of this continent2, compelled into an aesthetic contemplation he neither understood nor desired, face to face for the last time in history with something commensurate to his capacity for wonder3.

And as I sat there brooding on the old, unknown world, I thought of Gatsby’s wonder when he first picked out the green light at the end of Daisy’s dock. He had come a long way to this blue lawn, and his dream must have seemed so close that he could hardly fail to grasp it. He did not know that it was already behind him4, somewhere back in that vast obscurity beyond the city, where the dark fields of the republic rolled on under the night.

Gatsby believed in the green light, the orgiastic future that year by year recedes before us5. It eluded us then, but that’s no matter—tomorrow we will run faster, stretch out our arms further … And one fine morning—6

So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past.7

The Great Gatsby, Chapter IX · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

The continent as a body

With the houses imagined away, Nick sees Long Island as the Dutch sailors of the 1600s saw it: fresh, green, a “breast”—the new world as nurture, promise, a body offering itself to the arriving eye. The image gives America’s discovery the shape of desire, which is exactly what allows the paragraph to fuse it, a few lines later, with one man’s longing across a bay.

2

The held breath

‘Transitory’ admits it straight away: the enchanted moment was already passing as it happened. The sailors are “compelled” into contemplation they “neither understood nor desired”—wonder arriving unwanted, before commerce resumed. Fitzgerald’s history is elegy from the first glance: the trees that pandered to the dream were cut down for houses like Gatsby’s.

3

“For the last time in history”

The sentence’s largest claim: the fresh continent was the final object big enough to match the human “capacity for wonder”. Everything since—the novel implies—has been wonder in search of objects too small for it. Gatsby’s tragedy is that his capacity was continental and its object was Daisy: nothing left in the world was big enough for what he could dream.

4

The future located in the past

The paragraph’s quiet paradox: the dream ahead of Gatsby was “already behind him”. What he reached for across the water was a restored past, Louisville in 1917, the month he was someone she could love. The geography agrees: the dream lies “beyond the city”, back in the dark fields of the republic, back west where all the book’s characters came from.

5

A future that recedes—and a disputed word

The future is “orgiastic”—ecstatic, orgastic in Fitzgerald’s own manuscript and the first printing; his editor Maxwell Perkins queried it, Fitzgerald insisted, and later editors have divided over the word ever since. Either way the syntax does the damage: this future “recedes before us”, moving away at exactly the speed we approach it, like a horizon.

6

The sentence that never finishes

“It eluded us then, but that’s no matter”—the voice of optimism itself, budgeting failure. Tomorrow: faster, further. Then the dash. “And one fine morning—” breaks off because there is nothing true to put after it; the fine morning never arrives, in the novel or the sentence. Fitzgerald lets American hope speak its own idiom and then runs it off the edge of the page.

7

Rowing

The final image is muscular and futile: rowers straining forward while the current carries them back. ‘Beat’ is the oarsman’s stroke; ‘borne back ceaselessly’ is the water’s answer. The alliteration—beat, boats, borne back—makes the sentence itself pull like strokes of an oar. And the pronoun is “we”: Nick has stopped talking about Gatsby. The epitaph is for everyone, first person plural, present tense, still rowing.

What the wording changes

A personal elegy widens into a national one.

The movement of the passage is a series of enlargements. It begins with one man on a beach at night; the moonlight dissolves the real houses; three centuries vanish; and Gatsby’s private longing for Daisy is suddenly set beside the Dutch sailors’ first sight of the continent—two versions, the passage insists, of the same gesture. By the last line the subject is “we”. Fitzgerald wrote a novel about one summer on Long Island and closed it with a sentence about the structure of hope itself.

The equation works because both dreams share a flaw the imagery exposes. The sailors’ wonder was aimed at a continent that their arrival would immediately begin to spoil—the trees whisper and are cut down within the same sentence. Gatsby’s wonder was aimed at a past that his pursuit could only counterfeit—the mansion, the shirts, the parties, all built to repeat a month in 1917. In both cases the dream is destroyed by the machinery of reaching for it. That is the ending’s real sadness, more than Daisy’s unworthiness: the book never mocks the wonder, only the things it has been reduced to.

Nick’s “we” has one more effect worth noticing. Throughout the novel he has kept a fastidious distance—judging, qualifying, going back west. The last sentence surrenders the distance: he includes himself among the rowers. The judgment of Gatsby (“they’re a rotten crowd… You’re worth the whole damn bunch put together”) has already been delivered; the ending’s work is to remove the observer’s exemption. No one in the boat is steering from shore.

Take one question away

Is the last line a condemnation of the dream, a consolation for dreamers, or a description that will be neither?

Before reading

Follow the green.

Before this page, collect the novel’s green light appearances: Gatsby’s trembling reach in Chapter I, the moment in Chapter V when, with Daisy beside him, it becomes “again a green light on a dock” and Gatsby’s “count of enchanted objects” falls by one, and this final page, where it becomes the “green breast” of a continent. One object, three sizes.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Weigh the pronouns.

  1. Mark every “I”, “he” and “we” in the passage, in order.
  2. Identify the exact sentence where “he” becomes “us” and “we”.
  3. Rewrite the final sentence with “they” instead of “we”. What dies?
  4. Debate: does Nick earn the right to that “we”, or is it one more of his evasions?
Essay bridge

“Can’t repeat the past?”

For an essay on time in the novel, set this ending against Gatsby’s protest in Chapter VI—“Can’t repeat the past? Why of course you can!”—and the ending’s verdict that we are all borne back into it. Gatsby was wrong about repeating the past and the narrator says none of us ever leaves it; the essay lives in that difference.
