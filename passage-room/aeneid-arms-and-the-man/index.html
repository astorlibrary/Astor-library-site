Close reading 60 · The Aeneid

“Arms, and the man I sing”

Rome’s national epic opens by announcing its subject in three words, its hero in one, and then—before a single event—asking the Muse why a just man should be persecuted by heaven. The question hangs over all twelve books.

Virgil · translated by John DrydenBook I, lines 1–18

Composed in the last decade of Virgil’s life (he died in 19 BC, leaving the poem unfinished). Dryden’s heroic-couplet translation of 1697 was for two centuries the English Aeneid. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text of Dryden.

How to read this page: read the first sentence to its full stop—it is ten lines long and contains the whole poem. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The AeneidBook I

Arms, and the man I sing1, who, forc’d by fate,
And haughty Juno’s unrelenting hate2,
Expell’d and exil’d, left the Trojan shore.
Long labours, both by sea and land, he bore,
5And in the doubtful war, before he won
The Latian realm, and built the destin’d town3;
His banish’d gods restor’d to rites divine,
And settled sure succession in his line,
From whence the race of Alban fathers come,
10And the long glories of majestic Rome.4
O Muse! the causes and the crimes relate5;
What goddess was provok’d, and whence her hate;
For what offence the Queen of Heav’n began
To persecute so brave, so just a man;
15Involv’d his anxious life in endless cares,
Expos’d to wants, and hurried into wars!
Can heav’nly minds such high resentment show,
Or exercise their spite in human woe?6

The Aeneid, Book I · Dryden’s translation (1697) · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered within the extract

1

Arma virumque cano

Virgil’s first three Latin words—arms and the man I sing—announce the poem’s double inheritance: the war of Homer’s Iliad (arms) and the wandering hero of his Odyssey (the man). Virgil is declaring that his poem will do both in one. Dryden keeps the order exactly, so the English begins, as the Latin does, with weapons. The hero is not named for thirty lines; he is simply “the man”—a type before he is a person.

2

A goddess’s grudge

The engine of the plot is named in line 2: Juno’s hate, “unrelenting” and “haughty”. Aeneas has done nothing to her; she hates Troy for old slights and fears the Rome its survivors will found. The hero is persecuted not for a fault but for a future. Virgil sets the poem in motion with divine spite, and the question of whether the gods are just will not be laid to rest.

3

“Destin’d”

The town Aeneas will build is “destin’d”: fate has already decided the ending. This is the Aeneid’s peculiar shape—an epic whose outcome is guaranteed from the first lines, so that the interest lies not in whether Rome will rise but in what it costs. Aeneas is “forc’d by fate”, not driven by desire; his heroism is endurance of a script.

4

The sentence arrives at Rome

The first sentence travels from Troy’s shore through sea, war, gods and succession to land on its true subject: “the long glories of majestic Rome”. Virgil wrote under Augustus, in the first years of the empire, and the poem is among other things an origin-myth for the new order. Dryden, writing after England’s own civil wars, felt the parallel keenly; his “settled sure succession” is a very seventeenth-century phrase.

5

Causes and crimes

The invocation to the Muse comes second, not first—Virgil states his subject in his own voice before asking for help. And what he asks is not for the story but for its causes: “What goddess was provok’d, and whence her hate”. Dryden’s alliterating “causes and the crimes” sharpens it into an indictment. The epic opens as an inquiry into divine motive.

6

The question that starts the poem

Virgil’s tantaene animis caelestibus irae?—“can such anger dwell in heavenly minds?”—is the most famous question in Latin poetry, and Dryden expands it into a couplet: can gods feel such resentment, or “exercise their spite in human woe”? It is not rhetorical. The poem genuinely does not know, and its most painful episodes—Dido’s death, the killing of Turnus—keep returning to it. An epic of empire begins by doubting the justice of heaven.

What the wording changes

An empire’s epic that opens with a doubt.

The Aeneid was commissioned, in effect, by the new Roman state: Augustus wanted a national poem, and Virgil gave him one whose hero founds the line that leads to Augustus himself. Everything in the opening sentence serves that purpose—fate, destiny, succession, “the long glories of majestic Rome”. And yet the passage ends not on glory but on a question about divine cruelty, asked on behalf of a “brave” and “just” man who suffers without cause. The poem’s two voices—the public celebrant of Rome and the private mourner of what Rome costs—are both audible in its first eighteen lines. Readers have argued for centuries about which is the real Virgil; the honest answer is that the opening refuses to choose.

Dryden’s version shapes how English readers hear this. His heroic couplets give the Latin a balanced, public sound—every two lines closing on a rhyme, like a sentence in a formal speech—and his vocabulary is that of Restoration politics: succession, settled, realm. Virgil’s hexameters are more fluid and more melancholy. But Dryden catches the essential movement exactly: a long sentence of destiny, then a sudden turn to ask why any of it had to hurt. His couplet on heavenly spite is one of the great moments of English translation, expanding a five-word Latin question into a two-line accusation without losing its grief.

The opening also teaches how to read the hero. Aeneas is “the man” before he is a name, “forc’d by fate” before he acts, and defined by what he “bore”—labours, war, exile—rather than what he wanted. Where Achilles rages and Odysseus schemes, Aeneas endures, and Virgil’s word for him throughout is pius: dutiful, faithful to gods and family and mission. The opening lines make clear that this duty is not chosen but imposed, and that the poem will ask, again and again, whether a destiny worth so much suffering is a gift or a sentence.

Take one question away

The poem promises “the long glories of majestic Rome” and, eight lines later, asks whether gods can be spiteful. Which of these is the Aeneid about?

Before reading

Map the first sentence.

Follow the ten-line sentence from “Arms” to “Rome”: shore, sea, land, war, town, gods, succession, Alban fathers, Rome. It is the poem’s itinerary in miniature. Mark where the hero acts and where he is acted upon—“forc’d”, “expell’d”, “exil’d”.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Translate the question.

  1. Virgil’s Latin: tantaene animis caelestibus irae?—roughly, “such great angers in heavenly minds?”
  2. Compare Dryden’s two-line version. What has he added?
  3. Write your own one-line English version.
  4. Discuss whether the question is rhetorical, and what the answer would mean for the rest of the poem.
Essay bridge

Two voices.

For an essay on the poem’s politics, follow the tension of these opening lines—imperial promise against divine injustice—through Dido’s curse in Book IV and the killing of Turnus in Book XII. Ask whether Virgil celebrates Rome, mourns its cost, or wrote a poem in which both are true at once.
