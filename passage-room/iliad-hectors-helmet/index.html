Passage Room/The Iliad/Hector’s helmet

Close reading 47 · The Iliad

“scared… at the horse-hair plume”

Between battles, Hector stops at the Scaean gates to see his wife and baby son. The child cries at the sight of his father’s helmet, both parents laugh, and Hector takes the helmet off to hold him.

Homer · translated by Samuel ButlerBook VI

The poem comes from ancient Greek oral tradition. Hector, prince of Troy, has come from the battlefield to see his wife Andromache and their infant son. Butler’s 1898 prose translation uses the Roman name Jove for Zeus.

How to read this page: keep the armour and the child in view together; the scene depends on the clash between them. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The IliadBook VI

And Hector answered, “Wife, I too have thought upon all this, but with what face should I look upon the Trojans… if I shirked battle like a coward?1 I cannot do so: I know nothing save to fight bravely in the forefront of the Trojan host.”

He stretched his arms towards his child, but the boy cried and nestled in his nurse’s bosom, scared at the sight of his father’s armour, and at the horse-hair plume that nodded fiercely from his helmet.2

His father and mother laughed to see him3, but Hector took the helmet from his head and laid it all gleaming upon the ground.4 Then he took his darling child, kissed him, and dandled him in his arms, praying over him the while to Jove and to all the gods.

“Jove,” he cried, “grant that this my child may be even as myself, chief among the Trojans… Then may one say of him as he comes from battle, ‘The son is far better than the father.’5”

The Iliad, Book VI · Samuel Butler translation · public-domain text · ellipses mark shortened stretches

1

Shame before the Trojans

Hector fights because he cannot bear to be seen not fighting: “with what face should I look upon the Trojans”. The Homeric hero’s deepest motive is aidōs, shame before the eyes of others, and honour won in their sight. Andromache has just begged him to stay on the wall; he refuses, and his reason is what the Trojans would say. He already knows, and has just said, that Troy will fall and she will be enslaved. He goes anyway, because he could not live with the shame of staying.

2

The child sees the truth

The baby is frightened of his father’s armour, the bronze and the horse-hair plume nodding from the helmet. He cannot see the man inside the warrior, and shrinks from the warrior. What makes Hector a hero is what frightens his son, and the war comes between them in the shape of a helmet.

3

Laughter at the edge of grief

“His father and mother laughed”: a moment of ordinary family tenderness in a scene both parents know is a farewell. Andromache has foretold her widowhood; the reader knows Hector will die and Troy will burn. The laughter is hard to bear because it is happy, and doom surrounds it on every side.

4

The helmet on the ground

To hold his son, Hector takes off the helmet and lays it “all gleaming upon the ground”. He can reach the child only by disarming, and he puts the war down for as long as the embrace lasts. The helmet lies there “gleaming”, beautiful and ready, and we know he will pick it up again.

5

A prayer the poem will not grant

Hector prays that his son may grow up “chief among the Trojans”, a warrior of whom men say “the son is far better than the father”. Every listener knew the prayer was void: Troy falls, and the child Astyanax is thrown from the city walls by the Greeks. Homer lets a father hope aloud for a future the tradition has already cancelled, and the listener hears the prayer knowing how it will be answered.

What the wording changes

The farewell in Book VI

The Iliad is a poem of rage and slaughter. It begins with “the anger of Achilles”, and its battle books record spear-thrusts in close detail. Yet one of its best-known scenes is this one, in which nothing is killed and no one is brave in the ordinary sense. Homer sets it at the city gate, among the people who must leave and the people left behind. By putting the poem’s tenderest scene inside its grimmest subject, he shows what the fighting will destroy: this marriage and this child.

The scene works because the characters half-know what the audience knows completely. Andromache has foreseen her widowhood; Hector has said that Troy will fall and that she will be carried off a slave; the tradition has already killed both father and son. So the laughter, the kiss and the prayer for the child’s future all come after the doom has been spoken. Homer does not need to tell us it is a farewell. He lets the family behave as if it might not be, and leaves the grief to the listener, who knows what happens next.

The helmet carries the scene. It is Hector’s glory and his doom in one object: the mark of the hero the Trojans depend on, and the thing his own son cannot bear to look at. He must take it off to hold his child, and it lies gleaming on the ground until he takes it up again. Heroism in the Iliad is real, necessary and beautiful, and it stands between a man and the people he is dying to protect.

Take one question away

Hector knows Troy will fall and still takes up the helmet again. Is that heroism, or a shame he cannot escape? Does the poem let us tell them apart?

Before reading

Track the armour.

Follow the helmet through the passage: on his head, frightening the child, laid on the ground and, just after the passage ends, taken up again for battle. It is the scene’s one prop. Note every time it moves.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

What the baby knows.

  1. Ask why the child fears the armour but not the man.
  2. List everything the reader knows that the family does not fully face (Hector’s death, Troy’s fall, the child’s fate).
  3. Mark the exact moment the scene is happiest, and measure it against that knowledge.
  4. Decide what Homer gains by putting tenderness inside the doom, and not after it.
Essay bridge

Shame and the hero.

For an essay on heroism, set Hector’s “with what face should I look upon the Trojans” beside Achilles’ rage and withdrawal earlier in the poem. Both heroes are governed by honour before others; ask how the poem judges a code that sends a good man knowingly to his death and leaves the child he has just prayed over an orphan.
