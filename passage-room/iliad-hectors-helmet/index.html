Passage Room/The Iliad/Hector’s helmet

Close reading 47 · The Iliad

“scared… at the horse-hair plume”

The greatest war poem ever written stops, for one scene, at a city gate: a warrior reaches for his baby son, and the child screams at his helmet. Everything the poem knows about war is folded into that cry.

Homer · translated by Samuel ButlerBook VI

The poem comes from ancient Greek oral tradition. Hector, prince of Troy, has come from the battlefield to see his wife Andromache and their infant son. Butler’s 1898 prose translation uses the Roman name Jove for Zeus.

How to read this page: hold the armour and the child in the same eye—the scene is built on their collision. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The IliadBook VI

And Hector answered, “Wife, I too have thought upon all this, but with what face should I look upon the Trojans… if I shirked battle like a coward?1 I cannot do so: I know nothing save to fight bravely in the forefront of the Trojan host.”

He stretched his arms towards his child, but the boy cried and nestled in his nurse’s bosom, scared at the sight of his father’s armour, and at the horse-hair plume that nodded fiercely from his helmet.2

His father and mother laughed to see him3, but Hector took the helmet from his head and laid it all gleaming upon the ground.4 Then he took his darling child, kissed him, and dandled him in his arms, praying over him the while to Jove and to all the gods.

“Jove,” he cried, “grant that this my child may be even as myself, chief among the Trojans… Then may one say of him as he comes from battle, ‘The son is far better than the father.’5”

The Iliad, Book VI · Samuel Butler translation · public-domain text · ellipses mark shortened stretches

1

Shame, not bloodlust

Hector fights not because he wants to but because he cannot bear to be seen not fighting—“with what face should I look upon the Trojans?” The Homeric hero’s deepest motive is aidōs, shame before the eyes of others, and honour won in their sight. Andromache has just begged him to stay on the walls; he refuses, and the reason is social, not savage. He already knows, and has just said, that Troy will fall and she will be enslaved. He goes anyway, because the alternative is a shame he cannot live inside.

2

The child sees the truth

The baby is terrified not of his father but of his father’s armour—the bronze, the fierce nodding plume. The infant cannot tell Hector the man from Hector the warrior, and recoils from the second. In one image the poem says what pages of argument could not: the thing that makes Hector a hero is the thing that frightens his own son, and war stands physically between a father and his child.

3

Laughter at the edge of grief

“His father and mother laughed”—a single beat of ordinary family tenderness, and it lands amid a scene both parents know is a farewell. Andromache has foretold her widowhood; the reader knows Hector will die and Troy will burn. The laughter is unbearable precisely because it is happy, a moment of domestic sweetness the epic frames with doom on every side.

4

He sets the war down

To hold his son, Hector must take off the helmet and lay it “all gleaming upon the ground”. The gesture is the scene’s heart: the warrior can only reach the child by disarming, by putting the war down for the length of an embrace. That the helmet lies there “gleaming”—beautiful, waiting—reminds us he will pick it up again. The tenderness is granted on a strict loan.

5

A prayer the poem will not grant

Hector prays that his son may grow up “chief among the Trojans”, a warrior of whom men say “the son is far better than the father”. Every listener knew the prayer was void: Troy falls, and the child Astyanax is thrown from the city walls by the Greeks. Homer lets a father hope aloud for a future the tradition has already cancelled. The dramatic irony is total, and it turns the prayer from sentiment into tragedy.

What the wording changes

The war poem’s deepest moment has no fighting in it.

The Iliad is a poem of rage and slaughter—its first word is “wrath”, and its battle books catalogue spear-thrusts with terrible precision. Yet its most quoted scene is this one, in which nothing is killed and no one is brave in the ordinary sense. Homer knew that war is measured most sharply not on the field but at the gate, in what it costs the people who must leave and the people left behind. By setting the poem’s tenderest scene inside its grimmest subject, he makes the reader feel the whole weight of what the fighting will destroy—not abstract Troy, but this marriage, this baby, this laughing afternoon.

The scene works because it is saturated with a knowledge the characters half-share and the audience holds completely. Andromache has recited her own coming captivity; Hector has admitted Troy is doomed; the tradition has already killed both father and son. So the laughter, the kiss, the prayer for the child’s future are all spoken over a grave the poem has dug in advance. Homer does not need to tell us it is a farewell. He lets the family behave as if it might not be, and trusts the listener’s foreknowledge to supply the grief. This is dramatic irony used not for suspense but for sorrow.

The helmet is the scene’s master-stroke. It is Hector’s glory and Hector’s doom in one object—the mark of the hero the Trojans depend on, and the thing his own son cannot bear to look at. That he must remove it to hold his child, and that it lies gleaming on the ground waiting to be worn again, says everything the poem thinks about heroism: that it is real, and necessary, and beautiful, and that it stands between a man and the people he is dying to protect. Three thousand years later the image has not been improved on.

Take one question away

Hector knows Troy will fall and still puts the helmet back on. Is his choice heroism, or a shame he cannot escape—and does the poem let us tell them apart?

Before reading

Track the armour.

Follow the helmet through the passage: on his head, frightening the child, laid on the ground, implied back on for battle. It is the scene’s one prop, and it carries the whole meaning. Note every time it moves.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

What the baby knows.

  1. Ask why the child fears the armour but not the man.
  2. List everything the reader knows that the family does not fully face (Hector’s death, Troy’s fall, the child’s fate).
  3. Mark the exact moment the scene is happiest, and measure it against that knowledge.
  4. Decide what Homer gains by placing tenderness inside doom rather than after it.
Essay bridge

Shame and the hero.

For an essay on heroism, set Hector’s “with what face should I look upon the Trojans” beside Achilles’ rage and withdrawal earlier in the poem. Both heroes are governed by honour before others; ask how the poem judges a code that sends a good man knowingly to his death—and orphans the child he has just prayed over.
