Passage Room/Wuthering Heights/I am Heathcliff

Close reading 24 · Wuthering Heights

“Nelly, I am Heathcliff!”

Catherine has just agreed to marry Edgar Linton. Explaining herself to a housekeeper who does not want to listen, she produces the strangest account of love in Victorian fiction—one that has nothing to do with pleasure, and everything to do with existence.

Emily BrontëChapter 9

Published in 1847 as by Ellis Bell. Catherine speaks to Nelly Dean in the kitchen at Wuthering Heights; the italics are the novel’s own emphasis.

How to read this page: keep one fact in view throughout—Heathcliff is listening, but leaves before the end. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Wuthering HeightsChapter 9

“I cannot express it; but surely you and everybody have a notion that there is or should be an existence of yours beyond you1. What were the use of my creation, if I were entirely contained here? … If all else perished, and he remained, I should still continue to be; and if all else remained, and he were annihilated, the universe would turn to a mighty stranger2: I should not seem a part of it. My love for Linton is like the foliage in the woods: time will change it3, I’m well aware, as winter changes the trees. My love for Heathcliff resembles the eternal rocks beneath: a source of little visible delight, but necessary.4 Nelly, I am Heathcliff!5 He’s always, always in my mind: not as a pleasure, any more than I am always a pleasure to myself, but as my own being.6 So don’t talk of our separation again: it is impracticable; and—”

Wuthering Heights, Chapter 9 · public-domain text · one sentence shortened; Nelly’s interruptions omitted

1

A self stored elsewhere

Catherine begins with a claim she says everyone secretly shares: that part of your existence is kept outside you, in another. It is nearer metaphysics than romance—she is arguing about what a person is, not what she feels—and her opening admission, “I cannot express it”, is honest. The whole speech is an attempt to express something the available language of love was not built for.

2

The thought-experiment

She tests the claim with annihilation: subtract everything but Heathcliff, and she persists; subtract Heathcliff, and the universe becomes “a mighty stranger”—still existing, but no longer hers to belong to. Estrangement from the cosmos, not grief, is the imagined loss. The novel will hold her to this: after her death Heathcliff lives on in exactly that estranged universe, begging her ghost to haunt him.

3

A simile with an expiry date

Foliage is genuinely lovely—the comparison does not mock Edgar. But leaves are seasonal by definition, and Catherine says so herself: “time will change it, I’m well aware”. She chooses a simile that admits its own decay while she is in the act of accepting the marriage. Few characters in fiction describe their own future so accurately while proceeding anyway.

4

Rock, not flower

The counter-simile refuses every convention of love poetry: the rocks are underground, invisible, “a source of little visible delight”. What they offer is the load-bearing fact—necessary. Emily Brontë grew up on the moors above Haworth, where the geology genuinely does outlast the seasons; the image roots the love in landscape rather than sentiment, permanence rather than joy.

5

The simile collapses

“Is like”… “resembles”… and then no comparison at all: “I am Heathcliff”. The progression is grammatical and it is the argument—each figure of speech brings the two terms closer until the copula fuses them. The novel’s italics land on “am”: not love, not union, but identity. After this sentence, talk of choosing between men is beside the point she is making—which is, of course, exactly what makes her marrying Edgar catastrophic.

6

Being, not pleasure

Twice she rules out enjoyment: Heathcliff is in her mind “not as a pleasure”, any more than she is a pleasure to herself. The comparison is startlingly bleak self-knowledge. This love promises no happiness and is not defended as happiness; it is defended as the condition of her existing at all. Readers who find the pair unlikeable are not misreading—the novel never claims they are good, only that they are inseparable.

What the wording changes

The wrong listener hears half; the right one leaves too soon.

The speech’s tragedy is structural. Heathcliff, hidden in the kitchen, hears Catherine say that marrying him would degrade her—and slips out before she says everything above. He acts for three years, and arguably for the rest of the novel, on the half he heard. The reader holds the whole; the person the speech is about holds the worst fragment. Emily Brontë built the novel’s pivot out of an exit made at the wrong moment.

The listener who stays is no better suited. Nelly Dean is practical, pious and unimpressed—she calls the speech nonsense and wicked in the same breath. That matters because Nelly is also the novel’s narrator: everything we read arrives through her retelling to Lockwood years later. The most metaphysical declaration in Victorian fiction reaches us via a housekeeper who disapproved of it, inside a story told to a bored southern tenant. The framing does not diminish the speech; it protects it, letting Brontë state something extreme while keeping ordinary judgment audibly in the room.

And Catherine still marries Linton. The speech changes nothing in the plot—that is its coldest feature. She has distinguished the necessary love from the seasonal one with total clarity, and proceeds to build her life on the foliage. Whether that is self-betrayal, social realism (marriage to Heathcliff being genuinely impossible for her), or the belief that being Heathcliff makes marrying him irrelevant, is the question the rest of the novel spends two generations answering.

Take one question away

Catherine says she is Heathcliff—and then marries someone else. Does the speech explain the marriage, excuse it, or condemn it?

Before reading

Watch the comparisons tighten.

Mark the three stages: “is like the foliage”, “resembles the eternal rocks”, “I am Heathcliff”. Simile, weaker simile-verb, identity. Ask what is gained and lost at each step—and why the last one needs italics.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Hear it as Heathcliff did.

  1. Read the fuller scene and stop where Heathcliff leaves—just after “it would degrade me to marry Heathcliff now”.
  2. Write the two-line summary he would carry away.
  3. Now write the summary a listener would take from the whole speech.
  4. Trace one action later in the novel that follows from the difference.
Essay bridge

Two 1847 heroines.

Jane Eyre claims equality of soul with the man she loves; Catherine Earnshaw claims identity with hers. For an essay on the Brontës, compare the two claims: one grounds a marriage on independence, the other makes marriage almost irrelevant. Consider which novel is the more radical, and in what direction.
