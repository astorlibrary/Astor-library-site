Passage Room/Uncle Tom’s Cabin/Eliza crosses the ice

Close reading 11 · Uncle Tom’s Cabin

“One wild cry and flying leap”

Eliza runs because her child has been sold. Stowe makes the Ohio crossing spectacular, but keeps its cost visible in broken ice, lost shoes and blood. The scene turns a commercial decision made elsewhere into immediate danger borne by a mother and child.

Harriet Beecher StoweChapter VII

Published in book form in 1852. Eliza is escaping from Kentucky after learning that the trader Haley means to take Harry.

How to read this page: follow the verbs. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to see how the passage moves between the pursuer’s view, the narrator’s religious language and Eliza’s injured body.

Uncle Tom’s CabinChapter VII

A thousand lives seemed to be concentrated in that one moment1 to Eliza. Her room opened by a side door to the river. She caught her child, and sprang down the steps towards it. The trader caught a full glimpse of her just as she was disappearing down the bank; and throwing himself from his horse […] he was after her like a hound after a deer2.

Right on behind they came; and, nerved with strength such as God gives only to the desperate3, with one wild cry and flying leap, she vaulted sheer over the turbid current by the shore, on to the raft of ice beyond.

The huge green fragment of ice on which she alighted pitched and creaked as her weight came on it, but she staid there not a moment. With wild cries and desperate energy she leaped to another and still another cake; stumbling—leaping—slipping—springing upwards again!4 Her shoes are gone—her stockings cut from her feet—while blood marked every step5; but she saw nothing, felt nothing, till dimly, as in a dream, she saw the Ohio side, and a man helping her up the bank.

Uncle Tom’s Cabin, Chapter VII, “The Mother’s Struggle” · public-domain text · one shortened phrase

1

One moment holds a future

The sentence enlarges an instant because sale threatens more than separation now: it threatens the whole life Harry may be made to live. Eliza’s decision is compressed into urgent action.

2

The chase strips away the trader’s claim

Haley has treated Harry as property bought through a contract. The simile shows the violence beneath that legal transaction: a hunter pursues a vulnerable living body as prey.

3

Power born from desperation

“Nerved” makes strength bodily, while the appeal to God places Eliza’s action within Stowe’s Christian argument. Her extraordinary force is not evidence that the danger is bearable; it measures how extreme the danger is.

4

Grammar loses its footing

The participles and dashes arrive faster than a settled sentence. “Stumbling” and “slipping” prevent the crossing from becoming one clean heroic leap; survival has to be remade on each moving piece of ice.

5

The body records the crossing

Eliza cannot yet feel the cuts, but the reader is made to see them. “Marked” gives the route a written trace: the cost of flight appears step by step on the ice.

What the wording changes

The scene begins with sale and ends in an injured body.

Eliza’s crossing is often remembered as a single leap. Stowe makes it a broken sequence. The ice pitches; each landing fails to become safe; movement has to continue. The succession of verbs keeps the reader inside a danger that no picture of one triumphant jump can contain.

The chapter is titled “The Mother’s Struggle”, and Stowe repeatedly addresses family feeling. That appeal helped the novel reach readers who might otherwise keep slavery at a political distance. It also deserves examination: Eliza’s maternal devotion is made extraordinary, though the wrong lies in a system that makes any mother prove her love through such danger.

Ohio is across the river, but not outside the reach of law. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 strengthened procedures for recapturing people who had escaped into free states. The far bank is therefore a stage in Eliza’s flight towards Canada, not a simple end to it.

Take one question away

Does the scene’s extraordinary heroism reveal Eliza’s freedom—or reveal the extremity of a system that makes such heroism necessary?
