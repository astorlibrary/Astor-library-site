Passage Room/Moby-Dick/Call me Ishmael

Close reading 12 · Moby-Dick

“Call me Ishmael.”

The opening sounds direct, but it does not say “My name is”. Ishmael offers a name, waves away exact time and speaks to us as if the conversation has already begun. The great whaling voyage starts in a voice trying to manage life on shore.

Herman MelvilleChapter 1 · Loomings

Published in 1851. This opening comes after the “Etymology” and “Extracts” that crowd the book with earlier words about whales.

How to read this page: listen for how casually Ishmael addresses you while describing a serious need. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to see what the voice tells and withholds.

Moby-DickChapter 1 · Loomings

Call me Ishmael.1 Some years ago—never mind how long precisely2—having little or no money in my purse, and nothing particular to interest me on shore, I thought I would sail about a little and see the watery part of the world3. It is a way I have of driving off the spleen and regulating the circulation.

Whenever I find myself growing grim about the mouth; whenever it is a damp, drizzly November in my soul4; whenever I find myself involuntarily pausing before coffin warehouses, and bringing up the rear of every funeral I meet […] then, I account it high time to get to sea5 as soon as I can.

Moby-Dick, Chapter 1, “Loomings” · 1851 public-domain text · one shortened stretch

1

An invitation, not a certificate

“Call me” tells the reader what to do. It creates immediate familiarity while leaving open whether Ishmael is a legal name, a chosen name or simply the name this narrator will use for his story.

2

Precision waved aside

The dash opens a qualification and the narrator dismisses it. He controls the terms of the account: exact date matters less than the recurring condition that sends him towards the sea.

3

The world made strange

“Watery part” is both plain and oddly large. The phrasing makes the ocean one region of a world that could be entered almost casually—though the novel will expose how little that word “little” can contain.

4

Weather moves inward

Alliteration makes “damp, drizzly” linger in the mouth. November is no longer a date outside Ishmael but an inner climate: cold, dim and returning.

5

The sea as a practical answer

“High time” turns feeling into a signal for action. Ishmael presents going to sea as his alternative to violence against himself or others, though the voyage will carry him towards mortal danger.

What the wording changes

A vast book begins with one person speaking to another.

The first sentence is famous partly because it is so short. After the title, dedications, etymology and pages of extracts, three words suddenly establish a human voice. Yet the simplicity is artful. “Call me” makes the reader a participant and gives Ishmael authority over how the relation will begin.

That voice moves between understatement and danger. He will “sail about a little”, as though a whaling voyage were a stroll. At the same time he describes funerals, “pistol and ball” and a need to escape his own darkening mood. Humour does not cancel the seriousness; it is one of the means by which Ishmael can speak about it.

The passage also sets a pattern for the novel’s scale. A private feeling opens onto oceans, cities, labour, friendship, whales, myth, science and metaphysics. The movement is expansive, but it begins with a specific act of self-preservation: when shore life closes in, Ishmael goes where the streets lead—to water.
