Passage Room/Doctor Faustus/The face that launched a thousand ships

Close reading 19 · Doctor Faustus

“Was this the face…?”

Days from damnation, Faustus asks the spirits to bring him Helen of Troy. What he speaks to her is the most famous praise of beauty in English drama—and every image in it is on fire.

Christopher MarloweScene 13 · the Helen speech

Marlowe died in 1593; the play survives in two versions, printed in 1604 and 1616. The wording below follows the 1604 quarto text on Project Gutenberg.

How to read this page: read the speech once for its music, which is real. Then select or focus a numbered phrase and watch what the images actually do.

Doctor FaustusHelen

FAUSTUSWas this the face that launch’d a thousand ships1,
And burnt the topless towers of Ilium2—
Sweet Helen, make me immortal with a kiss.3—
[Kisses her.]
Her lips suck forth my soul: see, where it flies!4—
Come, Helen, come, give me my soul again.
Here will I dwell, for heaven is in these lips,
And all is dross that is not Helena.
I will be Paris, and for love of thee,
Instead of Troy, shall Wertenberg be sack’d5;
And I will combat with weak Menelaus,
And wear thy colours on my plumed crest;
Yea, I will wound Achilles in the heel,
And then return to Helen for a kiss.
O, thou art fairer than the evening air
Clad in the beauty of a thousand stars;
Brighter art thou than flaming Jupiter
When he appear’d to hapless Semele6;
More lovely than the monarch of the sky
In wanton Arethusa’s azur’d arms;
And none but thou shalt be my paramour!

Doctor Faustus, 1604 text · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

Praise that counts the dead

The most celebrated compliment in English drama measures beauty in warships. A thousand ships means a decade of siege and slaughter: Helen’s face is praised by the size of the catastrophe it caused. And it opens as a question—“Was this the face?”—as if Faustus, the scholar to the last, needs the legend verified before he commits to it.

2

Towers without tops

“Topless” means so high their tops cannot be seen—and those measureless towers exist in the line only as burning. Ilium is Troy. From its first image the speech can only praise by destroying: the city stands in the verse for the length of one word before the fire takes it.

3

The wrong immortality

Faustus already has an immortal soul; its eternity is the whole subject of the play, and he has signed it away. Asking a kiss to “make me immortal” asks a body to supply what he has spent—and this body is not Helen. In the play’s theology the figure the devils have conjured is a spirit wearing her shape. The Old Man who enters moments later calls the danger by its name.

4

A metaphor turns literal

Lovers’ poetry had long played at souls passing in kisses. Here the play’s machinery makes the conceit horribly plausible: a demon’s kiss may really draw the soul out. ‘See, where it flies!’ is ecstatic and exact. Faustus watches his soul leave and asks for it back in the next line, as though it were a game. It is the play in miniature.

5

He offers to burn his own city

Wittenberg—spelt “Wertenberg” in the 1604 text—is Faustus’s university town. Casting himself as Paris, he volunteers it for Troy’s fate: sacked for love. The scholar’s fantasy has left knowledge behind; he now dreams of entering the legends he once studied, and he will give up everything he was to do it.

6

Every comparison ends in ruin

Semele asked to see Jupiter in his full glory and was burnt to ash by it; Arethusa was a nymph pursued into transformation. Faustus reaches for mythological compliments and finds only mortals destroyed by contact with the divine. The speech knows what he refuses to: brightness on this scale consumes whatever comes close.

What the wording changes

The most beautiful speech in the play is its clearest warning.

Marlowe’s contemporaries heard something new in his verse—Ben Jonson later wrote of “Marlowe’s mighty line”—and this speech shows what the phrase meant: long, driving sentences that gather momentum across the pentameter, each image outbidding the last. The music is genuinely seductive. That is its function. We are meant to feel the pull that Faustus feels, and then to notice what we were pulled towards.

Because every term of the praise is catastrophic. Ships launched for war, towers burnt, a city sacked, Semele in ashes: the speech cannot describe beauty without describing destruction, and it ends with Faustus promising to re-enact Troy’s fall with his own university for kindling. The imagery convicts him even as it exalts her. Nothing in the play argues against this speech; the speech argues against itself.

Its placement completes the judgment. Faustus asks for Helen to distract him from repentance—the Old Man has just urged him towards mercy, and an act of grace is still, apparently, possible. The kiss is his answer. In the 1604 text he goes from “heaven is in these lips” to his final soliloquy within a scene: the man who located heaven in an illusion must then watch the real one close against him, crying out for a single drop of the blood he once signed away.

Take one question away

The speech is gorgeous, and it damns him. Can we enjoy its beauty and still see what it costs him, or does enjoying it repeat Faustus’s mistake?

Before reading

Sort the images.

Underline every image of fire and light. Circle every classical name and note what happened to that figure in myth. The two lists converge: the speech’s light is almost always something burning, and its myths are almost always fatal.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Prosecute and defend.

  1. Split into two groups. One argues the speech shows Faustus at his most human; the other that it shows him at his most lost.
  2. Each side must quote three phrases as evidence.
  3. Then swap: find the strongest point against your own case inside the same lines.
  4. Finish by deciding what the silent Helen contributes by saying nothing.
Essay bridge

From this kiss to the last hour.

For an essay on damnation, desire or knowledge, set this speech against Faustus’s final soliloquy, where the imagery returns inverted: Christ’s blood streaming in the firmament, the clock instead of the kiss, and time—not beauty—doing the burning.
