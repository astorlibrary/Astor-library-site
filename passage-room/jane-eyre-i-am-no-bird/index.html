“I am no bird”

In the Thornfield garden, believing Rochester is about to marry another woman, Jane says what a poor governess was never supposed to say aloud: that her soul weighs the same as his.

Charlotte BrontëChapter 23

Published in 1847 as ‘edited’ by Currer Bell, a name that kept the author’s sex unknown. The passage below keeps the novel’s own dashes.

How to read this page: read Jane’s speech at speaking pace; the punctuation shows where she draws breath. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Jane EyreChapter 23

Jane Eyre: An Autobiography. Edited by Currer Bell

“Do you think I can stay to become nothing to you? Do you think I am an automaton?—a machine without feelings?1 and can bear to have my morsel of bread snatched from my lips, and my drop of living water dashed from my cup?2 Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little3, I am soulless and heartless? You think wrong!—I have as much soul as you,—and full as much heart!4 And if God had gifted me with some beauty and much wealth, I should have made it as hard for you to leave me, as it is now for me to leave you. I am not talking to you now through the medium of custom, conventionalities, nor even of mortal flesh;—it is my spirit that addresses your spirit; just as if both had passed through the grave, and we stood at God’s feet, equal,—as we are!5”

“I am no bird; and no net ensnares me6; I am a free human being with an independent will, which I now exert to leave you.”

Jane Eyre, Chapter 23 · public-domain text · the second speech answers Rochester a moment later, when he begs her to be still and she breaks free of his arms

1

The machine question

“Automaton” belongs to its industrial moment. Brontë wrote in the England of mills and machinery, and a governess was in effect a paid machine for producing educated children: wound up, useful, replaceable. Jane names the assumption so that she can reject it. The question is rhetorical, and the novel’s social argument is that it needed asking at all.

2

Bread and living water

The morsel and the drop sound like a poor woman’s rations, and they are. But “living water” is scripture, the water Christ offers the woman of Samaria. Jane describes her love as daily bread and as a sacrament: the little that keeps her alive, in body and in spirit. “Snatched” and “dashed” make Rochester’s expected marriage an act of violence against that survival.

3

Four small words

Poor, obscure, plain, little: the adjectives shrink as they go, from circumstances to social visibility to face to body. Jane concedes every one; the novel has insisted on her plainness from the start, against the convention of beautiful heroines. The concession is strategic. Having granted everything the world counts, she can ask what any of it has to do with soul.

4

The claim of equality

Jane measures souls by quantity: “as much soul as you”, and “full as much heart”. The dashes around the sentence mark it as the centre of the speech. The novel’s frankness offended some critics, and Brontë answered them in the preface to the second edition: “Conventionality is not morality.”

5

Equal before God

Jane places equality after death, at God’s feet, spirit addressing spirit “as if both had passed through the grave”. Earthly society gives her no ground on which to stand level with Rochester, so she reaches past it to the one court where rank counts for nothing. Then the last three words pull that future into the present tense: “as we are!” The theology becomes a demand.

6

Refusing the cage

Rochester has just compared her to “a wild frantic bird” struggling in desperation. Jane rejects the metaphor itself: birds are caught in nets, kept in cages, admired for being small. Her correction, “a free human being with an independent will”, is deliberately plain, almost legal phrasing. She will not be his image of her, even a tender one, and she uses the will she names in the same sentence, to leave.

What the wording changes

The garden speech

Rochester has engineered the scene: he has let Jane believe he will marry Blanche Ingram so as to provoke her feeling into speech. What the provocation produces is a confession of love and, more than that, a declaration of standing. Jane speaks “roused to something like passion”, and the passion organises itself into argument: rhetorical questions, conceded premises, a conclusion about equality. Even overwhelmed, she reasons.

The punctuation records a voice under pressure. Brontë’s dashes mark the gasps and swerves of a person saying, at last, sentences rehearsed in silence for months. Without the dashes the speech reads like an essay; with them, you hear her breathing. The whole novel works this way: an autobiography that keeps the reader inside the body of the teller, and addresses the reader directly (“Reader”), as spirit addresses spirit here.

The rest of the novel puts the speech to the test. Rochester proposes; Jane accepts; and the wedding is stopped in the church when the existence of his living wife is revealed. The principles Jane declares in the garden (equality before God, an independent will) are what she must then use against her own desire, when she refuses to stay as his mistress. The famous line of the last chapter, “Reader, I married him”, comes only when marriage can happen on the terms this speech set: as equals, her fortune inherited, his dependence real.

Take one question away

Jane claims equality of soul while granting inequality of everything else. Is that a radical argument, a religious one, or both?

Before reading

Mark the questions.

Underline the four questions Jane asks before her first plain statement. Each question contains an accusation: identify whom or what each accuses. Then find the exact sentence where questioning turns to declaring.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

The dash experiment.

  1. Rewrite the central sentence with standard punctuation: “You think wrong!—I have as much soul as you,—and full as much heart!”
  2. Read both versions aloud. What do the dashes add: speed, breath, anger, control?
  3. Find two other dashes in the passage and say what each one does.
  4. Decide together: is Jane losing control of her speech here, or taking control of it?
Essay bridge

From the red-room to the garden.

For an essay on Jane’s development, connect this speech backwards to the child locked in the red-room at Gateshead and her outburst against Mrs Reed, and forwards to her refusal of St John Rivers’s loveless proposal. Three times Jane has to decide whether to let someone else define her; trace what changes in how she answers.
