Close reading 49 · The Red Badge of Courage

“pasted in the sky like a wafer”

A soldier watches his friend die by the roadside, shakes his fist at the battlefield, and gropes for a curse. The novel answers with one flat sentence about the sun.

Stephen CraneChapter IX

Published in 1895 by a writer who had never seen combat; Crane reconstructed war from imagination and veterans’ accounts. Henry Fleming, “the youth”, watches the tall soldier Jim Conklin die. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: watch how the prose describes a death, then a rage, and answers both with the weather. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Red Badge of CourageChapter IX

He was invaded by a creeping strangeness that slowly enveloped him. For a moment the tremor of his legs caused him to dance a sort of hideous hornpipe.1 His arms beat wildly about his head in expression of implike enthusiasm.

His tall figure stretched itself to its full height. There was a slight rending sound. Then it began to swing forward, slow and straight, in the manner of a falling tree.2

He now sprang to his feet and, going closer, gazed upon the pastelike face. The mouth was open and the teeth showed in a laugh.3 … the side looked as if it had been chewed by wolves.4

The youth turned, with sudden, livid rage, toward the battlefield. He shook his fist. He seemed about to deliver a philippic.

“Hell—”5

The red sun was pasted in the sky like a wafer.6

The Red Badge of Courage, Chapter IX · public-domain text · one clause shortened

1

A grotesque death

Jim Conklin’s dying spasms are a “hideous hornpipe”, arms beating in “implike enthusiasm”. Crane drops every convention of the noble deathbed: there is no last word, and the body jerks like a puppet. War loses the dignity that nineteenth-century writing usually lent it, and a young man’s death becomes involuntary, undignified, almost obscene motion.

2

“A falling tree”

The simile is deliberately impersonal: Conklin falls like timber, with nothing of the hero or the martyr about him. Crane repeatedly compares men to objects and animals, draining away the human significance the reader expects. A tree falls by physics, without meaning, and the comparison puts the death on the same footing as weather or gravity.

3

The corpse that seems to laugh

The dead face shows its teeth “in a laugh”: a rictus, an accident of the muscles, read by the living as mockery. Crane’s war is full of these false expressions, nature and the dead appearing to comment on human suffering while in fact meaning nothing. The laugh is in the onlooker’s horror; the corpse intends nothing.

4

“Chewed by wolves”

Crane gives the wound in Conklin’s side no military vocabulary, no bullet or shell: it looks as if it had been “chewed by wolves”. War becomes predation, the battlefield a place where men are eaten. Crane keeps swapping the vocabulary of glory for that of the slaughterhouse and the wild, as if organised human violence were, underneath, animals feeding.

5

The curse that cannot be finished

Henry, enraged, “seemed about to deliver a philippic”, a grand denunciation, and manages one broken word: “Hell—”. Language fails where the novel most needs it. No speech is adequate to the death, and no curse is large enough for the universe that permitted it. The dash swallows the rest, and Crane lets the inarticulacy stand.

6

The sentence that answers nothing

Into that silence drops the most famous line Crane wrote: “The red sun was pasted in the sky like a wafer.” It is a one-sentence paragraph, and it meets Henry’s rage with indifference. The sun is red, the colour of blood and of the badge in the title, but “pasted… like a wafer” makes it flat, cheap and stuck on, like the small red wafer once used to seal a letter. The sky Henry curses does not answer; the sun just hangs there, small and unmoved.

What the wording changes

The death of Jim Conklin

Crane wrote The Red Badge of Courage in his early twenties, having never seen a battle, and much of its force comes from what it leaves out: no cause, no strategy, no named battle, almost no officers with speeches. The war is experienced entirely through the confused senses of one frightened young soldier, and Crane strips away the framework (patriotism, glory, meaning) that had made war easy to narrate. What remains is sensation, terror, and a nature that watches without caring. The book is a founding text of American literary naturalism, and this passage shows its method as clearly as any.

The last sentence of the chapter withholds what Henry wants. He has just watched his friend die horribly and turned to curse the world; he and the reader want the universe to acknowledge the outrage with a storm, a darkening, some answering sign. Nineteenth-century fiction routinely supplied such weather, which Ruskin called the “pathetic fallacy”. Crane gives a sun that is merely there, flat and indifferent, “pasted… like a wafer”. The heavens do not grieve; they do not even notice. One calm sentence of indifference after a scene of horror hits harder than any storm.

The wafer may carry a further charge. To a nineteenth-century reader the word could also summon the communion host, so that the sun hangs like an emptied sacrament over a meaningless death. Readers have argued for decades over how much religious weight Crane intended, and the image can carry both: a gesture toward transcendence, and a scrap of red paper. Henry Fleming will find a kind of manhood by the novel’s end, but Crane never restores the meaning this sentence took away. War, in The Red Badge, happens under a sky that does not care, and this sentence says so and moves on.

Take one question away

Henry demands that the universe answer his friend’s death, and gets only the flat red sun. Is the sentence’s indifference the novel’s despair, or its honesty?

Before reading

Collect the comparisons.

List what Crane compares the dying man and the dead body to: a dancer, a falling tree, something chewed by wolves, a laughing face. None is heroic. Sort them into machine, animal and object. The pattern is the novel’s argument about war.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Rewrite the sky.

  1. Replace the final sentence with the “pathetic fallacy” version: a storm, a blood-red sunset weeping over the scene.
  2. Read both aloud after the “Hell—”. What does each say about the universe?
  3. Discuss why Crane sets the sentence as its own paragraph.
  4. Debate the “wafer”: a seal for a letter, a communion host, or both? What does each reading do to the sentence?
Essay bridge

Naturalism and the indifferent world.

For an essay on literary naturalism, set this sentence beside the ending of the novel, where Henry feels “a quiet manhood, nonassertive but of sturdy and strong blood… He was a man.” Ask whether the book endorses that self-assessment or undercuts it, and whether the indifferent sun of Chapter IX ever really sets.
