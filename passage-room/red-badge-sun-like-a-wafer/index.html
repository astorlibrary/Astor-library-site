Close reading 49 · The Red Badge of Courage

“pasted in the sky like a wafer”

A soldier watches his friend die by the roadside, shakes his fist at the battlefield, and gropes for a curse. What the novel gives him instead is one flat sentence about the sun—and it may be the most famous line in American war writing.

Stephen CraneChapter IX

Published in 1895 by a writer who had never seen combat; Crane reconstructed war from imagination and veterans’ accounts. Henry Fleming, “the youth”, watches the tall soldier Jim Conklin die. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: watch how the prose describes a death, then a rage, and answers both with the weather. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Red Badge of CourageChapter IX

He was invaded by a creeping strangeness that slowly enveloped him. For a moment the tremor of his legs caused him to dance a sort of hideous hornpipe.1 His arms beat wildly about his head in expression of implike enthusiasm.

His tall figure stretched itself to its full height. There was a slight rending sound. Then it began to swing forward, slow and straight, in the manner of a falling tree.2

He now sprang to his feet and, going closer, gazed upon the pastelike face. The mouth was open and the teeth showed in a laugh.3 … the side looked as if it had been chewed by wolves.4

The youth turned, with sudden, livid rage, toward the battlefield. He shook his fist. He seemed about to deliver a philippic.

“Hell—”5

The red sun was pasted in the sky like a wafer.6

The Red Badge of Courage, Chapter IX · public-domain text · one clause shortened

1

Death as grotesque, not noble

Jim Conklin’s dying spasms are a “hideous hornpipe”, arms beating in “implike enthusiasm”. Crane refuses every convention of the noble deathbed: the body does not compose itself for a last word but jerks like a puppet. This is war stripped of the dignity that nineteenth-century writing habitually lent it—a young man’s death rendered as involuntary, undignified, almost obscene motion.

2

“A falling tree”

The simile is deliberately impersonal—Conklin falls not like a hero or a martyr but like timber. Crane repeatedly compares men to objects and animals, draining the human significance the reader expects. A tree falls by physics, without meaning or witness. The comparison makes the death a natural event, indifferent, on the same footing as weather or gravity.

3

The corpse that seems to laugh

The dead face shows “teeth… in a laugh”—a rictus, the muscles’ accident, read by the living as mockery. The universe seems to grin at the survivors. Crane’s war is full of these false expressions, nature and the dead appearing to comment on human suffering while in fact meaning nothing. The laugh is the reader’s horror, not the corpse’s intent.

4

“Chewed by wolves”

The wound in Conklin’s side is not described in military terms—no bullet, no shell—but as if “chewed by wolves”. War is figured as predation, the battlefield as a place where men are eaten. Crane consistently swaps the vocabulary of glory for the vocabulary of the slaughterhouse and the wild, insisting that organised human violence is, underneath, only animals feeding.

5

The curse that cannot be finished

Henry, enraged, “seemed about to deliver a philippic”—a grand denunciation—and manages one broken word: “Hell—”. Language fails exactly where the novel most demands it. There is no speech adequate to the death, no curse large enough for the universe that permitted it. The dash swallows the rest. Crane lets the inarticulacy stand as the truest possible response.

6

The sentence that answers nothing

Into that silence drops the most famous line Crane wrote: “The red sun was pasted in the sky like a wafer.” It is a one-sentence paragraph, and it replies to Henry’s rage with pure indifference. The sun is red—blood, the badge of the title—but “pasted… like a wafer” makes it flat, cheap, stuck-on, like a communion wafer or a cardboard cut-out. The cosmos Henry curses does not answer; it just hangs there, small and unmoved. The whole of Crane’s bleak modern vision is compressed into that flatness.

What the wording changes

A war novel that refuses the war novel’s consolations.

Crane wrote The Red Badge of Courage at twenty-three, having never seen a battle, and its power comes partly from what it leaves out: no cause, no strategy, barely a named battle, almost no officers with speeches. The war is experienced entirely through the confused senses of one frightened young soldier, and Crane strips away the framework—patriotism, glory, meaning—that had made war narratable. What remains is sensation, terror, and a nature that watches without caring. The book is a founding text of American literary naturalism, and this passage is its purest distillation.

The famous last sentence works by refusal. Henry has just watched his friend die horribly and turned to curse the world; the reader, and Henry, want the universe to acknowledge the outrage—a storm, a darkening, some answering sign. Nineteenth-century fiction routinely supplied such “pathetic fallacy”, weather that mirrors human feeling. Crane gives the opposite: a sun that is merely there, flat and indifferent, “pasted… like a wafer”. The image denies the death any cosmic significance. The heavens do not grieve; they do not even notice. That indifference, delivered in one calm sentence after a scene of horror, is more devastating than any storm could be.

The wafer carries a further charge. To a nineteenth-century reader the word summons the communion host—the sacred wafer of the Eucharist—so that the sun hangs like a debased or emptied sacrament over a meaningless death. Critics have argued for decades over how much religious weight Crane intended, and the ambiguity is the point: the image gestures toward transcendence and offers only cardboard. Henry Fleming will go on to find a kind of manhood by the novel’s end, but Crane never restores the meaning this sentence took away. War, in The Red Badge, happens under a sky that does not care, and the greatest sentence in the book is the one that says so and moves on.

Take one question away

Henry demands that the universe answer his friend’s death, and gets only the flat red sun. Is the sentence’s indifference the novel’s despair—or its honesty?

Before reading

Collect the comparisons.

List what Crane compares the dying man and the dead body to: a dancer, a falling tree, something chewed by wolves, a laughing face. None is heroic. Sort them into machine, animal and object. The pattern is the novel’s argument about war.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Rewrite the sky.

  1. Replace the final sentence with the “pathetic fallacy” version—a storm, a blood-red sunset weeping over the scene.
  2. Read both aloud after the “Hell—”. What does each say about the universe?
  3. Discuss why Crane sets the sentence as its own paragraph.
  4. Debate the “wafer”: cheap object, communion host, or both—and what the choice does.
Essay bridge

Naturalism and the indifferent world.

For an essay on literary naturalism, set this sentence beside the ending of the novel, where Henry feels “a quiet manhood, nonassertive but of sturdy and strong blood… He was a man.” Ask whether the book endorses that self-assessment or undercuts it—and whether the indifferent sun of Chapter IX ever really sets.
