Close reading 53 · Anne of Green Gables

“the White Way of Delight”

A shy man is driving an orphan he did not ask for home to a farm that wanted a boy. On the way she renames everything she sees—and turns what looks like a child’s chatter into the book’s quiet argument about how to survive a hard life.

L. M. MontgomeryChapter II · Matthew Cuthbert is surprised

Published in 1908 in Prince Edward Island. Eleven-year-old Anne, sent by mistake instead of the boy the Cuthberts requested, is being driven to Green Gables by the taciturn Matthew. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: notice that Anne is not describing places but re-making them. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Anne of Green GablesChapter II

“Oh, it was wonderful—wonderful. It’s the first thing I ever saw that couldn’t be improved upon by imagination. It just satisfied me here”—she put one hand on her breast—“it made a queer funny ache and yet it was a pleasant ache.1”

“But they shouldn’t call that lovely place the Avenue. There is no meaning in a name like that. They should call it—let me see—the White Way of Delight.2 Isn’t that a nice imaginative name? When I don’t like the name of a place or a person I always imagine a new one3 and always think of them so.”

“That’s Barry’s pond,” said Matthew. “Oh, I don’t like that name, either. I shall call it—let me see—the Lake of Shining Waters.4 Yes, that is the right name for it. I know because of the thrill.5 When I hit on a name that suits exactly it gives me a thrill. Do things ever give you a thrill?6”

Anne of Green Gables, Chapter II · public-domain text

1

Beauty felt as a bodily ache

Anne locates joy physically—hand on her breast, “a queer funny ache and yet… a pleasant ache”. The oxymoron is exact childhood: intense beauty registers as something close to pain. And the Avenue is “the first thing I ever saw that couldn’t be improved upon by imagination”—a startling admission from a child who improves everything, and the book’s first hint of how much of her world she has had to make up because the real one gave her so little.

2

Renaming as re-making

“The Avenue” is dismissed—“there is no meaning in a name like that”—and replaced with “the White Way of Delight”. Anne does not merely admire the place; she takes possession of it by naming it, as Adam named the animals. Naming is her form of power, the one kind available to a penniless orphan: she cannot own anything, but she can rechristen the whole visible world and make it hers.

3

Imagination as a coping method

“When I don’t like the name of a place or a person I always imagine a new one.” Said lightly, this is the book’s survival strategy laid bare. Anne has come from orphanages and grudging households; imagination is how she has made an unbearable life bearable—renaming a plain girl “Rosalia DeVere”, turning a lane into a delight. Montgomery presents fancy not as escapism but as resilience, the mind refusing to accept the world’s poverty of meaning.

4

The name that outlives the joke

“The Lake of Shining Waters” is offered as another of Anne’s whims—and it sticks. Across the novel the pond keeps this name; the whole community, eventually, half-adopts Anne’s vision of Avonlea. The child’s private renaming quietly becomes shared reality. Montgomery’s gentle claim is that a strong enough imagination does not just console the imaginer; it reshapes how everyone sees.

5

“I know because of the thrill”

Anne’s test for a true name is a physical sensation—a “thrill” when it “suits exactly”. This is a genuine aesthetic theory in a child’s mouth: rightness is felt, not reasoned; the correct word announces itself in the body. It is also, quietly, a writer’s creed. Montgomery, naming her own places, is describing her own art through Anne—the search for the word that gives the thrill of exactness.

6

The question aimed at Matthew—and us

“Do things ever give you a thrill?” Anne turns to the silent old bachelor beside her and asks whether he, too, feels the world this intensely. The question is the hinge of the whole book: Matthew, who wanted a boy and got this talkative girl, is being invited into her way of seeing—and he will accept. Her imagination is contagious, and the novel’s emotional plot is the thawing of the reserved adults around her by exactly this kind of question.

What the wording changes

A children’s classic with a theory of survival in it.

Anne of Green Gables is loved as a warm, funny book about a redheaded orphan winning over a stern Island community, and it is that. But this early scene shows what gives the warmth its backbone. Anne’s ceaseless renaming—the Avenue into the White Way of Delight, Barry’s pond into the Lake of Shining Waters—reads as charming chatter, and is. It is also the visible working of a mind that has had to build its own beauty because the world handed it almost none. Montgomery lets us laugh at Anne’s extravagance while showing us its source: an orphan shuttled between households and asylums, who learned early that if she wanted delight she would have to imagine it.

The book’s quiet radicalism is to treat this imaginative power as a strength rather than a fault to be corrected. The adults around Anne—Marilla especially—keep trying to school the fancy out of her, to make her practical and plain-spoken. The novel sides, gently but firmly, with Anne. Her renaming is not a flight from reality but a way of meeting it; she sees the actual Avenue clearly (it “couldn’t be improved upon”) and gives it a name worthy of what she sees. Imagination, in Montgomery, is a mode of attention—a way of taking the world seriously enough to insist it mean something.

And the naming spreads, which is the book’s deepest optimism. “The Lake of Shining Waters” outlives the moment of invention and becomes what the pond is called; Matthew is won, then Marilla, then Avonlea. Anne does not adjust to her world so much as slowly convert it to hers. That a powerless orphan girl reshapes an entire community’s sense of its own landscape—simply by seeing it more vividly and refusing the dull official names—is the whole novel in miniature, and it is all here, on a buggy ride, before she has even reached the house that did not want her.

Take one question away

Anne renames the world because the real one gave her too little. Is her imagination an escape from her life—or the truest way she has of facing it?

Before reading

List the renamings.

Collect every renaming in the passage: the Avenue, Barry’s pond, and the earlier Hepzibah Jenkins into Rosalia DeVere. For each, note the plain name and Anne’s version. Ask what her names add that the originals lack.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

The thrill test.

  1. Anne says a true name gives “a thrill”. Rename a dull-sounding local place by her method.
  2. Test your name aloud—does it “suit exactly”? How do you know?
  3. Discuss whether Anne’s renaming changes the place or only her feeling about it.
  4. Find where, later in the book, one of her names is adopted by others, and say what that proves.
Essay bridge

Imagination against the practical.

For an essay on the novel, trace the conflict between Anne’s imagination and Marilla’s practicality from this scene onward. Ask whether the book asks Anne to grow out of her fancy or asks the adults to grow into it—and what its answer says about how Montgomery valued the imaginative life.
