Close reading 74 · King John

“Grief fills the room up of my absent child”

Two men tell a mother she is too fond of her sorrow. Her answer—that grief has moved into her son’s empty clothes—is the truest thing in a play about kings, and the reason the play is still read.

William ShakespeareAct 3, Scene 4

Probably written around 1595–96. Constance’s son Arthur, rival claimant to John’s throne, has been captured; she expects his death. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: notice that Constance answers two men who have not lost a child, and how. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

King John, Act 3, Scene 4 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered from the start of Constance’s reply

1

One line, and the argument is over

Cardinal Pandulph, a churchman, has told Constance her grief is excessive. Her reply is nine words: “He talks to me that never had a son.” The line does not argue; it disqualifies. The legate’s theology of moderation is answered with the one fact that makes it irrelevant. Shakespeare gives Constance the play’s sharpest weapon, which is not eloquence but standing.

2

Grief moves in

King Philip accuses her of loving grief as she loved the child. She takes the accusation literally and makes it true: grief “fills the room up” Arthur left. Every verb that follows is domestic—lies in his bed, walks with me, puts on his looks, repeats his words. Grief is not an emotion here but a lodger who has taken the boy’s place and does the things he did. The personification is so exact that it stops being a figure of speech.

3

The empty clothes

The image the speech is remembered for: grief “stuffs out his vacant garments with his form”. A child’s clothes, hanging empty, are filled again by sorrow wearing his shape. Anyone who has kept a dead person’s clothes knows the line is true; Shakespeare, who had lost his own son Hamnet at eleven in 1596, may have written it from knowledge. The image is small, physical and unbearable, and it does more than a lament could.

4

The question turned back

“Then have I reason to be fond of grief?” Philip called her fond; she has just shown that grief is all of her son that remains. Of course she is fond of it. The rhetorical question makes the men’s counsel look not wrong but obtuse, and her “Fare you well” dismisses them: had they suffered this, she says, they would comfort better. Constance is the only expert in the room.

5

The unbound hair

She has earlier bound her hair; now she lets it down, matching “disorder in my wit” with disorder in her appearance. The gesture is conventional—loose hair was the stage sign of madness or mourning—but she names it as a choice, and the choice is defiance: she will not keep “form” on her head while her mind has none. The play’s politics are all about form—titles, claims, ceremonies—and Constance lets hers go.

6

The cry

The composed argument breaks into a list of what the boy was: life, joy, food, all the world, widow-comfort, cure. Six nouns, no verbs, a mother naming everything at once. The speech has moved from repartee through image to raw address, and it ends with Philip fearing “some outrage” and following her out. Nobody in the play answers her. There is nothing to say.

What the wording changes

The mother outlasts the kings.

King John is among the least performed of Shakespeare’s histories, a play of shifting alliances, papal legates and a hero who dies of poison offstage. Its most famous passages belong not to John but to the people his politics crush: the Bastard Faulconbridge with his speech on “commodity”, and Constance, mother of the boy whose claim to the throne makes him a prisoner and then a corpse. In the nineteenth century, when the play was staged more often, actresses made Constance the reason to see it, and this scene was their showpiece. It still is, in the sense that matters: it is the part of the play people quote.

The speech is built as a reply, and that shapes everything. Two men counsel moderation from positions of comfort—a cardinal with his doctrine, a king with his platitude—and Constance answers each with a fact they cannot possess. The structure is nearly a courtroom’s: objection, dismissal, evidence, conclusion. But the evidence is the empty clothes, and once that image arrives the argument is no longer about whether grief is excessive but about whether the men have any standing to speak. Shakespeare lets her win completely, and then lets her go to pieces, because winning the argument does not bring the boy back.

The date invites a biographical reading that the play cannot confirm. Hamnet Shakespeare died in August 1596, aged eleven; King John is usually dated to around the same year. Whether the speech came before or after, and whether it was written from grief or from craft, cannot be known, and the poetry does not need the story. What can be said is that the image of grief in a child’s vacant garments is not a rhetorical figure a playwright reaches for lightly. It is the kind of thing a person notices, and Shakespeare noticed it.

Take one question away

Constance is told her grief is excessive, and proves it is all she has. Can there be such a thing as too much grief—and who has the right to say so?

Before reading

Count the verbs of grief.

List everything grief does in lines 1–5: fills, lies, walks, puts on, repeats, remembers, stuffs out. Grief has a body and a routine. Ask what Shakespeare gains by making sorrow a person rather than a feeling.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Answer the counsellors.

  1. Take Pandulph’s line and Philip’s line. Say what each man thinks he is offering.
  2. Read Constance’s two replies and identify what each one denies the speaker.
  3. Decide whether the speech is an argument, a rebuke, or a breakdown—or all three in order.
  4. Stage the unbinding of the hair: as defiance, as collapse, as both.
Essay bridge

Commodity and Constance.

For an essay on the play’s values, set this speech against the Bastard’s cynical hymn to “commodity”—self-interest—in Act 2. One voice says everyone in the play acts for gain; the other loses everything and gains nothing. Ask which voice the play finally trusts, and what Arthur’s death does to the answer.
