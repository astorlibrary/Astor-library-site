Close reading 63 · Pamela

“Don’t wonder to see the paper so blotted”

The first English bestseller opens with a fifteen-year-old servant writing home. Her letter reports a death, a promise and a master’s kindness—and every reassurance in it is, for the reader who knows what follows, a warning.

Samuel RichardsonLetter I

Published in 1740 and an instant sensation; the novel is told entirely in Pamela’s letters and journal. The wording below follows the public-domain text. Pamela Andrews, a lady’s maid, writes to her parents after her mistress’s death.

How to read this page: read it as the letter it is—written fast, to parents, by a girl trying to make bad news sound like good. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

PamelaLetter I

Letter I · Dear Father and Mother,

I have great trouble, and some comfort, to acquaint you with.1 The trouble is, that my good lady died of the illness I mentioned to you, and left us all much grieved for the loss of her… Much I feared, that as I was taken by her ladyship to wait upon her person, I should be quite destitute again, and forced to return to you and my poor mother, who have enough to do to maintain yourselves; and, as my lady’s goodness had put me to write and cast accounts, and made me a little expert at my needle, and otherwise qualified above my degree2, it was not every family that could have found a place that your poor Pamela was fit for: but God… put it into my good lady’s heart, on her death-bed, just an hour before she expired, to recommend to my young master all her servants, one by one; and when it came to my turn to be recommended, (for I was sobbing and crying at her pillow) she could only say, My dear son!—and so broke off a little; and then recovering—Remember my poor Pamela3—And these were some of her last words! O how my eyes run—Don’t wonder to see the paper so blotted.4

Well, but God’s will must be done!—And so comes the comfort, that I shall not be obliged to return back to be a clog upon my dear parents! For my master said, I will take care of you all, my good maidens; and for you, Pamela, (and took me by the hand5; yes, he took my hand before them all,) for my dear mother’s sake, I will be a friend to you, and you shall take care of my linen.

Pamela, or Virtue Rewarded, Letter I · public-domain text · ellipses mark shortened stretches

1

Trouble, and some comfort

The letter announces its own structure in the first line: bad news, then good. Pamela is managing her parents’ feelings before she has told them anything—a habit of reassurance that the whole novel will test, since the “comfort” she reports here is the beginning of her danger. Richardson lets a naive letter-writer organise her news in a way the reader will learn to read against.

2

“Above my degree”

Pamela can write, keep accounts and sew finely—accomplishments her mistress gave her, and which have made her, as she says, hard to place. The phrase names the novel’s social engine. A servant “qualified above my degree” belongs nowhere: too educated for the kitchen, too poor for the parlour. Her letters are the proof of that education, and they are also what will eventually make a gentleman marry her.

3

The deathbed charge

The dying lady recommends her servants “one by one”, and for Pamela can manage only a broken sentence: “My dear son!—… Remember my poor Pamela.” The words are meant as protection; the son will remember her in ways his mother did not intend. Richardson plants the novel’s central ambiguity in a deathbed blessing, and Pamela, reporting it tearfully, cannot hear the double meaning.

4

The blotted page

“O how my eyes run—Don’t wonder to see the paper so blotted.” The letter reports its own tear-stains. This is the epistolary novel’s signature move—the document as physical object, written in the moment of feeling, marked by the body of the writer. Richardson called it “writing to the moment”. The reader is asked to imagine the actual sheet, wet in places, and Pamela becomes real by the damage to her paper.

5

He took her hand—before them all

The master promises to be “a friend” and, as he says it, takes Pamela’s hand. She reports the gesture twice—in the sentence and again in a parenthesis, “yes, he took my hand before them all”—as if unsure what it meant and unable to leave it alone. The reader, unlike Pamela, can already guess. The offer of “his linen” to look after is the first step in a pursuit that will fill the next three hundred pages.

What the wording changes

A novel invented from a letter that cannot see itself.

Pamela began as a manual. Richardson, a printer, had been commissioned to write a book of model letters for people who could not compose their own; one of the situations he imagined—a servant girl writing to her parents about a master’s advances—grew into a story, and the story became the first English novel to be a public sensation. Villages reportedly rang church bells when Pamela married; the book sold in editions, was pirated, parodied and dramatised within months. Its method is entirely here in Letter I: a young woman writing in the present tense of her own experience, to readers (her parents, and us) who can see more than she can.

That gap is the source of both the book’s power and its notoriety. Pamela reports her master’s hand on hers with an innocence the text half contradicts by repeating it; she calls his interest ‘comfort’; she does not know what the reader knows. Richardson intended a moral tale—“Virtue Rewarded”—in which a servant’s resistance wins her a marriage, and he was astonished when readers took a less pious view. Henry Fielding answered within a year with Shamela, a parody in which the innocent letter-writer is a schemer who knows exactly what her blotted paper is doing. The argument has gone on ever since, and it starts on this page: is Pamela artless, or is artlessness her art?

What is not in dispute is the technique. “Writing to the moment”—Richardson’s phrase—gives fiction a new immediacy: no narrator, no hindsight, only a mind on paper in the middle of events it cannot yet understand. The blotted page, the parenthesis about the hand, the trouble reframed as comfort, are all the marks of a consciousness caught in the act. Later novelists would learn to do this without letters; Richardson did it with them, and the first letter of Pamela is where the English novel learned to think in the first person, in real time, about a danger it could not yet name.

Take one question away

Pamela reports the hand on hers twice and calls it comfort. Does she not understand it—or does the letter understand more than its writer admits?

Before reading

Read as the parents.

Before the notes, read the letter as Pamela’s mother or father would. Mark what would reassure you and what would worry you. Richardson’s art is in the gap between what the writer means and what the reader sees; find where the gap opens.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

The parenthesis.

  1. Isolate “(and took me by the hand; yes, he took my hand before them all,)”.
  2. Remove it and read the sentence. What is lost?
  3. Ask why Pamela says it twice, and why “before them all” matters to her.
  4. Decide: naive, knowing, or both—and defend the choice with the punctuation.
Essay bridge

Pamela and Shamela.

For an essay on the epistolary novel, read Letter I beside the opening of Fielding’s Shamela, which rewrites it as calculation. Ask what the parody exposes in Richardson’s method, and what it cannot touch, since a letter written ‘to the moment’ can be sincere and self-serving together.
