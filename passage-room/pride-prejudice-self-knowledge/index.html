Close reading 05 · Pride and Prejudice

“I never knew myself”

Elizabeth’s revelation is often remembered as a sudden flash of self-knowledge. Austen makes it slower and more demanding: she unfolds the letter again, tests its claims and discovers where her own judgement has failed.

Jane AustenChapter 36

First published in 1813. This scene follows Darcy’s rejected proposal and the letter in which he answers Elizabeth’s accusations.

How to read this page: follow what Elizabeth physically does with the letter. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to see how rereading becomes self-correction.

Pride and PrejudiceChapter 36

In this perturbed state of mind1, with thoughts that could rest on nothing, she walked on; but it would not do; in half a minute the letter was unfolded again2, and collecting herself as well as she could, she again began the mortifying perusal of all that related to Wickham, and commanded herself3 so far as to examine the meaning of every sentence.

But when she read and re-read with the closest attention4 […] again was she forced to hesitate.

“How despicably I have acted!” she cried; “I, who have prided myself on my discernment! […] Pleased with the preference of one, and offended by the neglect of the other […] I have courted prepossession and ignorance, and driven reason away, where either were concerned. Till this moment I never knew myself.5”

Pride and Prejudice, Chapter 36 · public-domain text · ellipses mark two shortened stretches

1

Thought unsettles the body

“Perturbed” means disturbed or agitated. Elizabeth cannot simply walk the letter off: her physical movement cannot settle what its evidence has begun to disturb.

2

Back to the object

Austen makes revision tangible. The letter is folded away and opened again; Elizabeth’s change begins with returning to words she had decided to reject.

3

Judgement takes discipline

Elizabeth must “command” herself to attend to each sentence. Clearer reading is not an effortless triumph of intelligence but work performed against anger and preference.

4

The repeated act

“Read and re-read” slows the scene. She compares accounts, recalls exact words and weighs probability. Self-knowledge is built through checking evidence.

5

A sudden sentence, slowly earned

“Till this moment” sounds like revelation. Yet Austen has shown the difficult process behind it: Elizabeth recognises not only a mistaken opinion, but the vanity that helped produce it.

What the wording changes

The turning point is an act of reading.

Darcy’s letter does not compel belief merely because it contains his version of events. Elizabeth begins by reading too quickly to understand, then rejects it. Only when she returns with “closest attention” does the evidence start to alter what she thinks.

Austen separates being clever from judging well. Elizabeth has “prided” and “valued” herself on discernment, but her confidence has made pleasing evidence feel true. Wickham’s attention gratified her; Darcy’s first slight offended her. “Prepossession” names the belief formed in advance that reason was later made to serve.

The scene does not make Elizabeth suddenly infallible, nor does it excuse Darcy’s earlier conduct. It changes the novel because she can now treat her own interpretation as something that may require revision. The reader, who has largely seen events from near her viewpoint, has to do the same.

Take one question away

Is Elizabeth’s greatest discovery the truth about Darcy and Wickham—or the discovery that intelligence does not protect her from wanting a story to be true?
