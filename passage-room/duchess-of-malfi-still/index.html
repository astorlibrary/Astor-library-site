Close reading 36 · The Duchess of Malfi

“I am Duchess of Malfi still.”

Imprisoned by her brothers, surrounded by staged madmen, the Duchess faces a tormentor disguised as a tomb-maker who tells her what her body is worth. Her answer is six words long, and four centuries have not finished arguing about them.

John WebsterAct 4, Scene 2

First performed around 1613–14 and printed in 1623. Bosola, her brothers’ hired spy, comes to her prison disguised. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read it as a duel—two philosophies of what a person is, exchanged across a cell. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Duchess of Malfi4.2

BOSOLA. Thou art a box of worm-seed1, at best but a salvatory of green mummy. What’s this flesh? a little crudded milk, fantastical puff-paste. Our bodies are weaker than those paper-prisons boys use to keep flies in; more contemptible, since ours is to preserve earth-worms. Didst thou ever see a lark in a cage? Such is the soul in the body2: this world is like her little turf of grass, and the heaven o’er our heads like her looking-glass, only gives us a miserable knowledge of the small compass of our prison.

DUCHESS. Am not I thy duchess?3

BOSOLA. Thou art some great woman, sure, for riot begins to sit on thy forehead (clad in gray hairs) twenty years sooner than on a merry milk-maid’s. Thou sleepest worse than if a mouse should be forced to take up her lodging in a cat’s ear.

DUCHESS. I am Duchess of Malfi still.4

BOSOLA. That makes thy sleep so broken:
Glories, like glow-worms, afar off shine bright,
But, look’d to near, have neither heat nor light.5

DUCHESS. Thou art very plain.

BOSOLA. My trade is to flatter the dead, not the living; I am a tomb-maker.6

The Duchess of Malfi, Act 4, Scene 2 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · one sentence shortened

1

The body priced at nothing

Bosola’s catalogue comes from the old contempt-of-the-world tradition: the body as a seed-box for worms, a “salvatory” (ointment-box) of “green mummy”—unripe corpse-flesh, since powdered mummy was sold as medicine. Curdled milk, puff-paste: kitchen stuffs. The speech is designed to strip a duchess to her perishable materials before killing her—spiritual preparation, or torture wearing its vocabulary.

2

The caged lark

Amid the disgust, Webster gives Bosola an image of unexpected beauty: the soul as a lark in a cage, the world its little turf of grass, heaven its mirror—showing just enough to measure the prison. The tenderness is the unsettling thing; Bosola half-believes in the soul he is bullying. Throughout the play he is the tragedy’s most divided man, executioner and mourner in one body.

3

Rank, tested

“Am not I thy duchess?” is not vanity; it is a test of what survives. Bosola answers with the body again—grey hairs arriving twenty years early, sleep worse than a mouse lodged in a cat’s ear. Time and fear, he says, have already unmade the title. The question sets up the great line by failing: asking for confirmation invites denial.

4

Six words

So she stops asking and states: “I am Duchess of Malfi still.” The sentence claims what nothing in the cell supports—identity intact against imprisonment, widowhood, approaching murder. “Still” carries the whole weight: continuing, unmoved, in spite of everything. Yet the claim is also, precisely, to a title—the public office her brothers control—and Bosola will instantly read it as vanity. Triumph and self-deception have shared these six words for four hundred years, and the line is great because neither reading can evict the other.

5

The glow-worm couplet

Bosola answers in rhyme—one of Webster’s sententiae, detachable moral couplets his tragedies are studded with: glories shine like glow-worms at a distance, but held close have “neither heat nor light”. The couplet form itself argues—neat, closed, quotable—against her open declaration. It is a fine epigram, and the play does not endorse it: within a scene, Bosola is the one undone by what he has helped extinguish.

6

The theatre of execution

“My trade is to flatter the dead, not the living; I am a tomb-maker.” The disguise announces the murder as ceremony: coffin, cords and bell follow, presented like properties in a masque. Her brothers have scripted her death as spectacle; the Duchess’s response—here and to the end—is to refuse the script’s terror while accepting its fact. “Let me be a little merry”, she says moments later, asking what fashion her tomb will take.

What the wording changes

Two accounts of a person, and a death that judges between them.

The exchange is a formal debate conducted in a torture chamber. Bosola’s position is the old ascetic one: the self is flesh, flesh is worm-food, and titles are optical illusions of distance. The Duchess’s position is that a person is what she has been and chosen—ruler, wife, mother—and that no cell revokes it. Webster gives Bosola the better images and the Duchess the better sentence, which is the play’s way of scoring the contest.

What follows tests her claim past breaking point. She kneels to be strangled—“heaven-gates are not so highly arch’d / As princes’ palaces”, she says: one must kneel to enter—and her last thoughts are practical and maternal: her little boy’s cold medicine, her daughter’s prayers. The woman who insisted on her title dies attending to nursery details; rank and flesh both fall away, and what remains is care. It is the quietest refutation of Bosola’s worm-seed philosophy the stage affords.

And it is Bosola the death converts. Holding her body he finds the pity his employers paid him to suppress, and the play’s last act is his revenge on them. The scene’s question—what survives of a person under absolute power—gets its answer not in the debate but in its effect: her six words outlive her within the play itself, doing to Bosola exactly what glories, by his own couplet, should not be able to do at close range.

Take one question away

Is “I am Duchess of Malfi still” a claim about her title, her self, or her soul—and does Bosola’s sneer answer any of the three?

Before reading

Inventory the images.

List Bosola’s materials: worm-seed, mummy, curdled milk, puff-paste, paper-prisons, the lark, the turf, the looking-glass. Sort them into images of disgust and images of beauty. The mixture is the man—keep the list for the essay on his divided role.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Weigh the six words.

  1. Say the line aloud four ways: as defiance, as delusion, as simple fact, as a prayer.
  2. For each delivery, decide what “still” means and where the stress falls.
  3. Read Bosola’s glow-worm reply after each version. Which delivery does his couplet answer best—and which does it entirely miss?
  4. Vote on the reading the scene supports, citing one detail either side.
Essay bridge

“Flesh and blood, sir.”

For an essay on identity and rank, set this scene against the wooing scene (1.1), where the Duchess, proposing to her steward Antonio, insists “This is flesh and blood, sir; / ’Tis not the figure cut in alabaster / Kneels at my husband’s tomb.” Early, she claims the living body against the monument; in prison, she claims the title against the body’s decay. Trace what “Duchess of Malfi” means at each end of the play.
