Passage Room/The Rape of Lucrece/Time’s glory

Close reading 73 · The Rape of Lucrece

“Time’s glory is to calm contending kings”

Alone after the crime, before dawn, Lucrece has no one to accuse but abstractions. She turns on Time, and for three stanzas the poem lists what Time is for—a catalogue so complete that it becomes an argument about why it failed her.

William ShakespeareLines 939–959

Published in 1594, the year after Venus and Adonis, and dedicated to the Earl of Southampton. Lucrece, raped by Tarquin in her husband’s absence, rails at Night, Opportunity and Time before she resolves to die. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

Content note

The poem’s subject is rape and its aftermath. This passage comes from Lucrece’s long lament after the assault, and the reading treats it as what it is: the speech of a woman with no one to hear her.

How to read this page: read the three stanzas as one sentence of accusation—the catalogue is the charge. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Rape of Lucrece, lines 939–959 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered within the extract

1

Time as judge

The catalogue opens with Time’s highest functions: to end wars, expose lies, reveal truth. These are the offices of a just ruler, and Lucrece begins by granting Time its glory. The strategy is forensic—she is building the case for what Time should have done, so that its failure to stop Tarquin will stand out as dereliction. The praise is a prosecution.

2

To wrong the wronger

Time’s justice works by turning wrong back on the wrongdoer “till he render right”—retribution as a slow mechanism. It is the one office Lucrece most needs performed, and the poem’s ending will perform it: Tarquin is banished and the Roman monarchy falls. But that is centuries of Time away from the woman speaking, and she knows it.

3

Worm-holes and blotted books

The second stanza turns from justice to decay: worm-holes in monuments, oblivion fed with ruin, old books blotted and their contents altered. Shakespeare, who would fight Time in the Sonnets, here lists its slow work on stone, memory and text. ‘Alter their contents’ is a poet’s fear: that Time corrupts the records of bodies as well as the bodies. Lucrece is about to become a record herself.

4

Fortune’s wheel

The stanza ends on the medieval emblem of mutability: Time turns “the giddy round of Fortune’s wheel”, raising and casting down. The rhyme royal stanza—seven lines, closing on a couplet—gives each catalogue a firm end, and Shakespeare uses the couplets for the largest images: golden towers smeared with dust, the wheel turning, stones worn away.

5

The child a man, the man a child

The third stanza gives Time its gentler and stranger offices: to age a girl into a grandmother, to grow the child and shrink the man, to tame monsters, to fool the clever, to reward the ploughman. It is a whole cycle of life in seven lines, evenhanded and unhurried. The evenhandedness is the point—Time does all this, for everyone, and did nothing for her.

6

Stones and water-drops

The catalogue closes on the proverb that patience wears stone: Time wastes “huge stones with little water-drops”. It is Time’s most patient power, and the image cuts two ways. Time will erode Tarquin’s wrong eventually, drop by drop. But Lucrece cannot wait for erosion; in the next stanza she asks why Time did not spare ‘one poor retiring minute’ to prevent the crime. The slow justice of water on stone is exactly what a woman in the hours after violence cannot use.

What the wording changes

A woman with no listener argues with an abstraction.

The Rape of Lucrece is a narrative poem of nearly two thousand lines, and most of its second half is Lucrece alone, speaking. Her husband is away at the siege; her household is asleep; the man who assaulted her has fled. She has, literally, no one to tell, and so she addresses Night, Opportunity and Time in turn, in long formal apostrophes. Modern readers sometimes find these stretches slow. They are the poem’s subject. Shakespeare is showing what happens to a mind after violence when there is no court, no confidant and no language yet for what was done—it turns on the universe and puts it on trial.

The Time stanzas are the most controlled of these speeches, and their control is expressive. Lucrece does not rage; she itemises. Twenty-one lines, each beginning “To…”, list Time’s duties with a completeness that becomes an accusation: this is everything you do, and you did none of it for me. The rhetorical figure is anaphora, the same opening word hammered line after line, and the effect is of someone holding herself together by making a list. The stanzas that follow break the composure—“Why work’st thou mischief in thy pilgrimage?”—but here the grief is disciplined into inventory.

The poem was hugely popular in its day and went through many editions; it made Shakespeare’s name as a poet before the plays made it as a dramatist. Its handling of Lucrece has been argued over ever since—whether the long laments give her a voice or drown her in rhetoric, whether the suicide the poem moves toward is presented as heroism or as the only exit a patriarchal Rome allowed. The Time stanzas sit at the heart of that argument. They are magnificent, and they are what a woman says when the only thing that will hear her is an idea.

Take one question away

Lucrece praises Time for twenty-one lines in order to accuse it. Is the catalogue a way of holding grief together, or a sign that she has been left with nothing but rhetoric?

Before reading

Hear the anaphora.

Count the lines that begin “To”. Read the three stanzas aloud without pausing at the stanza breaks. The list is one breath held for twenty-one lines; ask what that does to the reader’s sense of the speaker’s control.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Sort Time’s offices.

  1. Divide the catalogue into justice (calming kings, wronging the wronger), decay (worm-holes, dust) and cycle (child to man, crops).
  2. Ask which category Lucrece needed and which she got.
  3. Find the one office that will, eventually, avenge her.
  4. Discuss why she lists the gentle ones too.
Essay bridge

Time in the poems and the Sonnets.

For an essay on Shakespeare’s Time, set these stanzas beside Sonnet 64 (“When I have seen by Time’s fell hand defaced”) and Sonnet 19 (“Devouring Time”). The catalogue of decay is shared; ask what changes when the speaker is a poet fighting for a beloved’s memory, and not a woman fighting for justice.
