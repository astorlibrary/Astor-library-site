Passage Room/Henry IV, Part 1/What is honour?

Close reading 67 · Henry IV, Part 1

“What is honour? A word.”

Minutes before a battle in which the play’s young heroes will fight for honour, its fat old knight takes the word apart in the form of a Sunday-school catechism—and the play lets him win the argument and lose the war.

William ShakespeareAct 5, Scene 1

Probably written around 1596–97. Falstaff speaks alone on the field of Shrewsbury after Prince Hal has told him he “owest God a death”. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: hear the questions and answers as a schoolroom drill—that is the joke. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Henry IV, Part 15.1

Falstaff’Tis not due yet. I would be loath to pay him before his day.1 What need I be so forward with him that calls not on me? Well, ’tis no matter; honour pricks me on. Yea, but how if honour prick me off when I come on?2 How then? Can honour set to a leg? No. Or an arm? No. Or take away the grief of a wound? No. Honour hath no skill in surgery then? No.3 What is honour? A word. What is that word honour? Air. A trim reckoning!4 Who hath it? He that died a Wednesday.5 Doth he feel it? No. Doth he hear it? No. ’Tis insensible then? Yea, to the dead. But will it not live with the living? No. Why? Detraction will not suffer it. Therefore I’ll none of it. Honour is a mere scutcheon—and so ends my catechism.6

Henry IV, Part 1, Act 5, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

A debt not yet due

Hal has just said, “thou owest God a death”—a proverb, and a pun on debt. Falstaff takes it literally as a bill and declines to settle early: “I would be loath to pay him before his day.” The speech begins by turning the language of chivalric duty into the language of a debtor dodging a creditor, and never quite leaves it.

2

Pricked on, pricked off

Honour “pricks” him on—spurs him—but might, he worries, “prick me off”: strike his name from the roll, as a sheriff pricked names on a list, or simply kill him. The pun turns a motivating virtue into a bureaucratic deletion. Falstaff’s fear is exact and practical, which is why the grand word cannot survive contact with it.

3

Honour as surgeon

The test is medical: can honour reset a leg, an arm, take away a wound’s grief? No, no, no. “Honour hath no skill in surgery then?” The drill of question and answer is the catechism form—the way children were taught the articles of faith—applied to the one value the play’s aristocrats hold sacred. Every “No” is a small mutiny against the chivalric creed, delivered in the rhythm of Sunday instruction.

4

A word; air

The centre of the speech is a two-step reduction. What is honour? “A word.” What is that word? “Air”—breath, nothing. “A trim reckoning!” is his sarcastic audit: a neat sum, adding up to zero. Falstaff, who has spent the play running up bills he never pays, knows a bad balance sheet when he sees one, and honour’s books do not balance.

5

He that died a Wednesday

Who has honour? A dead man, and the day is comically specific—“a Wednesday”, as if honour were a weekday appointment you kept by being killed. The dead man cannot feel or hear it; it is “insensible”. And the living cannot keep it either, because “detraction”—slander, envy—will not let them. Honour, the argument runs, exists only for those who can no longer have it.

6

A painted shield

A scutcheon is a heraldic shield, painted with a coat of arms and carried at a funeral. Honour, Falstaff concludes, is “a mere scutcheon”—a decoration hung over a corpse. Then the joke about form: “and so ends my catechism”. He has named what he was doing all along. The children’s drill in the articles of faith has been used to unlearn one.

What the wording changes

The play lets the coward be right.

Shakespeare places the catechism at the worst possible moment for the chivalric ideal to hear it: on the eve of Shrewsbury, between Hal’s reconciliation with his father and Hotspur’s death in pursuit of exactly the honour Falstaff is dismantling. The structure of Henry IV, Part 1 sets three men in a triangle—Hotspur, who lives for honour; Falstaff, who lives despite it; and Hal, who must choose—and this speech is Falstaff’s case, argued to the audience with no one on stage to answer it. That the case is delivered in prose, in the form of a schoolroom drill, is part of its force: it is common sense against verse, the body against the emblem.

The catechism form matters. Every Elizabethan child learned religion by rote question and answer—“What is thy name?” “Who gave thee this name?”—and the audience would have recognised the rhythm at once. To use it for honour is to treat the aristocratic code as a creed, and to demonstrate, question by question, that the creed has no content: it cannot heal, cannot be felt, cannot be kept. Falstaff’s reasoning is airtight in its own terms. What it leaves out is what the play then shows—that Hal will win his honour at Shrewsbury, and that the audience will cheer.

That doubleness is the point. Falstaff is not refuted; he is outlived. Within the scene he plays dead to survive Douglas, then stabs Hotspur’s corpse and claims the kill—honour as a scutcheon, literally picked up off the field. The play lets him be funny, right and disgraceful in the same ten minutes, and never resolves which. By Part 2 the balance will tip, and Hal will banish him. But here, on the field, the fat knight’s arithmetic stands: honour is a word, the word is air, and the man who died a Wednesday cannot hear it.

Take one question away

Falstaff’s argument is unanswerable and the play still crowns Hal for the thing it dismisses. Does Shakespeare want us to reject honour, keep it, or know exactly what it costs?

Before reading

Count the questions.

Mark every question and every “No”. The speech is a drill, and the drill is the meaning: a value that cannot answer a child’s catechism. Say it aloud in a schoolroom sing-song and notice how much of the comedy is rhythm.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Answer Falstaff.

  1. Take the role of Hotspur and answer the catechism in verse.
  2. Take the role of Hal, who must fight in ten minutes, and answer it.
  3. Find the step in Falstaff’s logic you would attack first.
  4. Decide whether the argument is easier to beat than to ignore.
Essay bridge

Three men and a word.

For an essay on honour in the play, set the catechism beside Hotspur’s “By heaven, methinks it were an easy leap / To pluck bright honour from the pale-fac’d moon” and Hal’s eulogy over Hotspur’s body. Three definitions, one word; ask which the play finally endorses, and what Falstaff’s survival does to the answer.
