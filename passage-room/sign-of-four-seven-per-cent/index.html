Close reading 75 · The Sign of the Four

“Which is it to-day, morphine or cocaine?”

The second Holmes novel opens not with a client but with a syringe. Watson, a doctor, finally protests; Holmes, a mind with nothing to do, explains. The exchange fixed the character for a century—and it is Watson’s voice, not Holmes’s, that carries the scene.

Arthur Conan DoyleChapter 1 · The Science of Deduction

Published in Lippincott’s Monthly Magazine in February 1890—the same magazine and editor that commissioned The Picture of Dorian Gray over the same dinner. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: notice how long Watson waits before he speaks, and why. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Sign of the FourChapter 1

Again and again I had registered a vow that I should deliver my soul upon the subject, but there was that in the cool, nonchalant air of my companion which made him the last man with whom one would care to take anything approaching to a liberty.1 … Yet upon that afternoon, whether it was the Beaune which I had taken with my lunch, or the additional exasperation produced by the extreme deliberation of his manner, I suddenly felt that I could hold out no longer.

“Which is it to-day?” I asked,—“morphine or cocaine?”2

He raised his eyes languidly from the old black-letter volume which he had opened. “It is cocaine,” he said,—“a seven-per-cent. solution. Would you care to try it?3”

“No, indeed,” I answered, brusquely. “My constitution has not got over the Afghan campaign yet.4 I cannot afford to throw any extra strain upon it.”

He smiled at my vehemence. “Perhaps you are right, Watson,” he said. “I suppose that its influence is physically a bad one. I find it, however, so transcendently stimulating and clarifying to the mind that its secondary action is a matter of small moment.5”

“But consider!” I said, earnestly. “Count the cost! Your brain may, as you say, be roused and excited, but it is a pathological and morbid process6, which involves increased tissue-change and may at last leave a permanent weakness.”

The Sign of the Four, Chapter 1 · public-domain text · one stretch shortened

1

The last man to take a liberty with

Watson has watched the syringe three times a day “for many months” and said nothing. The reason is not indifference but Holmes’s manner—“cool, nonchalant”, forbidding intimacy. The novel opens by establishing the distance between the two men, and the friendship as something Watson must find the courage to act on. The Beaune at lunch helps. It is a very human beginning for a book about a reasoning machine.

2

Morphine or cocaine?

The question is blunt and clinical, a doctor’s question, and the “to-day” tells us it is habitual. Both drugs were legal and available from chemists in 1890; cocaine had been isolated in the 1850s and was being promoted as a tonic. Doyle, a physician himself, knew the pharmacology, and the famous opening is medically exact rather than sensational. Its shock for the first readers was moral, not legal.

3

A seven-per-cent solution

The precision is Holmes: he answers a question about his habit with a concentration. And then the offer—“Would you care to try it?”—casual, hospitable, faintly contemptuous of Watson’s scruple. The phrase became so famous that a twentieth-century pastiche took it as a title. It is the first thing Holmes says in the novel, and it tells us he is bored, precise and unrepentant.

4

The Afghan campaign

Watson refuses on medical grounds that are also biography: he was wounded at Maiwand in 1880 and invalided home, as A Study in Scarlet established. The line places the stories in their imperial moment—a Victorian veteran of a colonial war, sharing rooms with a consulting detective. Watson’s body carries the empire’s cost; Holmes’s mind carries its restlessness.

5

Transcendently stimulating

Holmes concedes the drug is physically bad and dismisses the concession: its “secondary action”—the damage—is “a matter of small moment” beside the clarity it gives. The value system is stated plainly. Mind is everything; body is the vehicle it is allowed to wear out. The sentence explains the whole character, including why he needs cases: the drug is what he uses when there is no problem to solve.

6

The doctor speaks

“A pathological and morbid process”, “increased tissue-change”, “permanent weakness”—Watson answers as a physician, in the vocabulary of the 1880s medical press, and Doyle’s own training shows. But the earnestness is friendship too: “Count the cost!” is not a diagnosis but a plea. The narrator who is often called Holmes’s admiring foil is here his doctor, his conscience and his only friend, and the scene lets him be all three.

What the wording changes

The detective as a mind with nothing to detect.

The Holmes of popular memory is the Holmes of this scene: the syringe, the languid eyes, the seven-per-cent solution. Doyle put it at the very front of the second novel deliberately, before any mystery, so that the reader meets the detective in his off-hours—and discovers that the off-hours are the problem. Holmes takes cocaine, he explains a page later, against “the dull routine of existence”; his mind “rebels at stagnation”. The drug is not a vice added to the character but a symptom of what the character is: an intelligence that must be fed problems or it will feed on itself. Every case that follows, in this novel and the fifty-odd stories after it, is in part a cure.

The scene is also where Watson becomes more than a narrator. In A Study in Scarlet he was mostly an observer, cataloguing Holmes’s peculiarities; here he confronts him, and the confrontation is written as a doctor’s intervention and a friend’s. Doyle gives Watson the medical language and the moral standing, and gives Holmes a reply that is charming, precise and unanswerable—which is the pattern of their whole relationship. Watson is right and Holmes does not care, and the reader, who came for Holmes, finds that it is Watson who makes the pages bearable.

The publishing history adds a footnote worth knowing. The novel was commissioned by the American editor J. M. Stoddart at a dinner in London in August 1889 for Lippincott’s Magazine; the other guest was Oscar Wilde, who left with a commission for The Picture of Dorian Gray. Two of the most famous books of the decade—one about a mind that lives for stimulus, one about a face that never pays for it—came out of the same meal. Both, in their first pages, put a drug on the table.

Take one question away

Holmes says the damage is “of small moment” beside the clarity. Is the cocaine a flaw in the great detective, or the clearest statement of what he is?

Before reading

Mark the two vocabularies.

Underline Watson’s medical words (pathological, morbid, tissue-change) and Holmes’s (transcendently, clarifying, secondary action). Two men describe one syringe in two languages. The scene is a disagreement about what a body is for.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Why here?

  1. Ask why Doyle opens a detective novel with no crime, no client, and a drug.
  2. List what the reader learns about Holmes before the case arrives.
  3. Rewrite the opening to begin with the client’s knock, and say what is lost.
  4. Decide whether the scene makes Holmes more admirable or less.
Essay bridge

The reasoning machine and its fuel.

For an essay on the character, set this opening against Watson’s portrait of Holmes as “the most perfect reasoning and observing machine” in A Scandal in Bohemia. A machine does not need cocaine. Ask what the drug reveals that the machine metaphor conceals.
