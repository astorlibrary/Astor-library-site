Close reading 39 · Lyrical Ballads

“a sense sublime”

Revisiting the river Wye after five years, Wordsworth measures what has died in him against what has replaced it. The exchange he records—raptures traded for a presence that rolls through all things—became Romanticism’s central transaction.

William Wordsworth“Lines written a few miles above Tintern Abbey”

Composed in July 1798, on revisiting the banks of the Wye, and printed that year as the closing poem of Lyrical Ballads. The wording below follows the 1798 Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read the verse-paragraph as one continuous thought—its clauses build like breathing. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Lyrical BalladsTintern Abbey

—I cannot paint
What then I was. The sounding cataract
Haunted me like a passion:1 the tall rock,
The mountain, and the deep and gloomy wood,
5Their colours and their forms, were then to me
An appetite: a feeling and a love,
That had no need of a remoter charm,
By thought supplied, or any interest
Unborrowed from the eye.—That time is past,
10And all its aching joys are now no more,
And all its dizzy raptures.2 Not for this
Faint I, nor mourn nor murmur: other gifts
Have followed, for such loss, I would believe,
Abundant recompence.3 For I have learned
15To look on nature, not as in the hour
Of thoughtless youth, but hearing oftentimes
The still, sad music of humanity4,
Not harsh nor grating, though of ample power
To chasten and subdue. And I have felt
20A presence that disturbs me with the joy
Of elevated thoughts; a sense sublime
Of something far more deeply interfused5,
Whose dwelling is the light of setting suns,
And the round ocean, and the living air,
25And the blue sky, and in the mind of man,
A motion and a spirit, that impels
All thinking things, all objects of all thought,
And rolls through all things. Therefore am I still
A lover of the meadows and the woods,
30And mountains; and of all that we behold
From this green earth; of all the mighty world
Of eye and ear, both what they half-create,
And what perceive6; well pleased to recognize
In nature and the language of the sense,
35The anchor of my purest thoughts, the nurse,
The guide, the guardian of my heart, and soul
Of all my moral being.

Lyrical Ballads (1798), “Lines written a few miles above Tintern Abbey” · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered within the extract

1

Nature felt as appetite

The younger self did not contemplate waterfalls; he was “haunted” by them, “like a passion”. Rock, mountain and wood were “an appetite”—hunger, not taste—needing no “remoter charm, / By thought supplied”: no ideas, no interpretation, nothing the eye did not deliver raw. The lines define a way of feeling precisely so the poem can announce its extinction.

2

An elegy for intensity

“That time is past”—flatly, without cushioning. What died is named in two oxymorons: “aching joys”, “dizzy raptures”—pleasures so intense they hurt and unbalanced. Wordsworth mourns them exactly as one mourns youth: by cataloguing what made it unbearable and calling the catalogue loss. Few poems admit this openly—that growing wiser begins as growing number.

3

“I would believe”

The claim of “abundant recompence”—full compensation for the lost raptures—carries a hairline crack: “I would believe”. Not “I believe”; the subjunctive of a man persuading himself. The entire poem balances on this hedge. Read confidently, what follows is the recompense; read anxiously, it is the hoped-for recompense—and the poem’s closing turn to his sister, asked to hold his memories for him, suggests the anxiety was real.

4

The still, sad music

What replaced the cataract’s sound is quieter and human: hearing in nature “the still, sad music of humanity”. The line is deliberately paradoxical—still music, sad but “not harsh nor grating”, powerful enough “to chasten and subdue”. Where the boy heard water, the man hears everyone: suffering, labour, mortality, carried in the same landscape. It is the poem’s hinge from sensation to sympathy.

5

The presence

The famous credo: “a presence that disturbs me with the joy / Of elevated thoughts”—disturbance and joy fused—“a sense sublime / Of something far more deeply interfused”. Note how carefully unnamed the something is: not God, not Spirit, but “a motion and a spirit” dwelling in sunlight, ocean, air, sky “and in the mind of man”, rolling through all things. The catalogue ends inside the skull—mind listed among the elements—which is the doctrine in miniature: one current runs through world and thinker alike.

6

Half-create, and perceive

The quietest radical claim in the poem: the mighty world of eye and ear is one that our senses “half-create, / And what perceive”. Perception is partnership—the mind contributes half of what it finds. Wordsworth footnoted the debt himself, citing a line of Edward Young. Everything the poem has recorded—cataract, music, presence—is thereby signed as a collaboration between the Wye valley and the man looking at it; the question of which half is which keeps the poem alive.

What the wording changes

A transaction audited in public.

The poem’s full title is part of its meaning: “Lines written a few miles above Tintern Abbey, on revisiting the banks of the Wye during a tour, July 13, 1798”. Dated like a diary, placed like a survey—and the abbey itself never appears in the poem. The occasion is a re-visit: the same landscape five years after 1793, with the difference located entirely in the visitor. Out of that ordinary tourist experience Wordsworth built the Romantic poem of memory—the mind measuring itself against its own past on unchanged ground.

The passage is the audit at the poem’s centre: intensity lost, meaning gained, and the balance declared—almost—even. Its honesty is what keeps it from complacency. The oxymorons grieve; the “I would believe” wavers; and the poem does not end on the credo but on Dorothy, the “dear, dear Sister” in whose voice and “wild eyes” he catches his own former self. He asks her to remember this moment for him against the time when his hearing dulls further. The recompense, in other words, is not fully trusted; a second keeper is appointed.

Placed last in Lyrical Ballads, the poem was the volume’s destination. The collection had opened with Coleridge’s Mariner—supernatural ballad, punishment and spell—and closes here with blank verse in which the only spirit is one “deeply interfused” in ordinary light and air. Between the two poems sits the book’s whole revolution: the marvellous relocated from ghosts to consciousness, and a language, as the Preface would soon argue, of men speaking to men.

Take one question away

“I would believe” and the appeal to Dorothy both qualify the poem’s confidence. Is the recompense real, hoped for—or does the poem’s greatness lie in not knowing?

Before reading

Two soundtracks.

List the sounds of the younger self’s nature (the sounding cataract) and the older self’s (the still, sad music of humanity). One is loud, outer and thoughtless; one is quiet, inner and moral. The poem’s argument is the passage from the first hearing to the second.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Weigh the exchange.

  1. Column one: what was lost—appetite, aching joys, dizzy raptures, the eye’s self-sufficiency.
  2. Column two: what was gained—the sad music, the presence, elevated thoughts, the anchor of moral being.
  3. Mark every hedge in the gain column: would believe, oftentimes, well pleased.
  4. Debate: does the ledger balance? Cite the sister’s role as evidence either way.
Essay bridge

Against the Mariner.

For an essay on Lyrical Ballads as a joint experiment, set this passage against the becalmed stanzas of The Ancient Mariner: Coleridge’s spirit-world acts on a sinner from outside; Wordsworth’s presence is “interfused” and half-created from within. The two poems bracket the 1798 volume and divide the labour its Preface later described.
