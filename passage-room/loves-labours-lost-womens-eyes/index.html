Passage Room/Love’s Labour’s Lost/From women’s eyes

Close reading 86 · Love’s Labour’s Lost

“From women’s eyes this doctrine I derive”

Four young men swore to study for three years and see no women. All four have fallen in love. Berowne, the wittiest, is asked to prove that breaking the oath is lawful, and he does.

William ShakespeareAct 4, Scene 3

Probably written around 1594–95. The King of Navarre and his lords, each caught writing love-poems, ask Berowne to find them “some tricks, some quillets” to justify forswearing. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: admire the argument, then check whether any of it is true. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Love’s Labour’s Lost4.3

BerowneFrom women’s eyes this doctrine I derive.
They sparkle still the right Promethean fire1;
They are the books, the arts, the academes,
That show, contain, and nourish, all the world2;
5Else none at all in aught proves excellent.
Then fools you were these women to forswear,
Or, keeping what is sworn, you will prove fools.3
For wisdom’s sake, a word that all men love,
Or for love’s sake, a word that loves all men,
10Or for men’s sake, the authors of these women,
Or women’s sake, by whom we men are men,
Let us once lose our oaths to find ourselves,
Or else we lose ourselves to keep our oaths.4
It is religion to be thus forsworn,
15For charity itself fulfils the law,
And who can sever love from charity?5

KingSaint Cupid, then, and, soldiers, to the field!6

Love’s Labour’s Lost, Act 4, Scene 3 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered within the extract

1

Promethean fire

Prometheus stole fire from the gods and gave it to mankind; Berowne says women’s eyes still carry “the right Promethean fire”, the original spark of knowledge. The lords swore off women in order to study; Berowne’s trick is to argue that women are the study. The oath, properly understood, required them to fall in love.

2

Books, arts, academes

The King founded his court as “a little academe”; Berowne relocates the academy into women’s eyes, which are “the books, the arts, the academes” and “nourish all the world”. It is a beautiful inversion and a complete evasion: the men can keep their scholarly vocabulary and abandon its content. Shakespeare gives the sophistry his best verse so that the audience wants it to be true.

3

Fools either way

The chiasmus is the speech’s logic in miniature: fools to have sworn, or fools to keep the oath. Either way, folly; so the only wisdom is to break it. The argument is perfectly balanced and proves nothing, like most of what the play’s clever men say. Berowne’s wit is real, and the play will make him earn something better than wit.

4

Lose oaths, find ourselves

The sophistry peaks here: “lose our oaths to find ourselves, / Or else we lose ourselves to keep our oaths”. The symmetry sounds like wisdom, and it is a rationalisation dressed as a paradox. Yet part of it is true: the oath to shun women was a denial of nature, and the men were more themselves in breaking it. Berowne is wrong about the method and right about the diagnosis.

5

Religion to be forsworn

The final move borrows scripture: “charity itself fulfils the law” (Romans 13), and love is charity, so oath-breaking is holy. It is the kind of argument a clever undergraduate makes at midnight, and Shakespeare lets it land as a triumph. The men cheer. The women will be harder to persuade.

6

Saint Cupid

The King’s response makes the joke explicit: “Saint Cupid, then, and, soldiers, to the field!” The oath-breakers become crusaders for love, and the last act is their campaign, a masque of Muscovites in disguise, which the ladies of France rout completely. The speech won the argument among men and did not win a single woman.

What the wording changes

Berowne’s argument

Love’s Labour’s Lost is Shakespeare’s most verbal comedy, a play in which almost everyone talks too well, and Berowne’s speech is its rhetorical summit. Men who have all been caught breaking the same oath ask him to make the breach honourable, and he obliges with a long speech in which their vow of study becomes a vow of love, women’s eyes become libraries, and forswearing becomes religion. It is dazzling, and it is the kind of cleverness the rest of the play humbles. The lords are wits, and the play is about what wit cannot do.

What it cannot do is persuade the women. The Princess of France and her ladies meet the men’s campaign of wooing with mockery, swap favours to expose it, and, when the men finally speak plainly, are interrupted by a messenger: the Princess’s father is dead. The comedy ends without a wedding, the only one of Shakespeare’s comedies to do so. Each lady sets her suitor a year’s task, and Berowne’s is the sharpest: to spend twelve months jesting in a hospital, to see whether his wit can make the dying laugh. His speech about women’s eyes as academes gets its answer: go and learn something.

Read that way, the play is about sophistry and is not taken in by it. Berowne’s argument is beautiful because Shakespeare wanted the audience to feel the pull of eloquence and then watch it fail against grief and time. “Our wooing doth not end like an old play,” Berowne says as the play ends; “Jack hath not Jill.” The man who proved that oath-breaking was holy finds that the women wanted something the argument could not supply, and the comedy closes with two songs, of the cuckoo and the owl.

Take one question away

Berowne proves, brilliantly, that breaking the oath is holy. The women are unmoved. What does the play think eloquence is for?

Before reading

Find the chiasmus.

Mark every line that swaps its own terms: fools/fools, oaths/ourselves, wisdom/love. Berowne argues in mirrors. Ask whether a mirrored sentence can prove anything, or only sound as if it has.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Cross-examine Berowne.

  1. State his conclusion: breaking the oath is lawful.
  2. List each premise and test it. Are women’s eyes books?
  3. Ask what a lady of France would say to each step.
  4. Decide whether the play agrees with Berowne, disagrees, or enjoys him too much to decide.
Essay bridge

The comedy that ends without a wedding.

For an essay on the play’s design, connect this speech to the ending: the messenger, the year’s tasks, Berowne’s hospital, “Jack hath not Jill”. Ask how a play about the failure of wit uses its wittiest speech, and why Shakespeare denied the lords the marriages every other comedy grants.
