Close reading 30 · A Room of One’s Own

“money and a room of her own”

Asked to lecture on “women and fiction”, Woolf answers with what she calls one minor point—then explains that she will argue it by telling stories, through a speaker who does not exist.

Virginia WoolfChapter One

Published by the Hogarth Press in 1929, developed from lectures given at Newnham College and Girton College, Cambridge—then two of the very few colleges for women—in October 1928.

How to read this page: read the passage as a speech to a room of young women—that is what it was. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

A Room of One’s OwnChapter One

All I could do was to offer you an opinion upon one minor point1—a woman must have money and a room of her own if she is to write fiction2; and that, as you will see, leaves the great problem of the true nature of woman and the true nature of fiction unsolved.

At any rate, when a subject is highly controversial—and any question about sex is that—one cannot hope to tell the truth. One can only show how one came to hold whatever opinion one does hold.3 One can only give one’s audience the chance of drawing their own conclusions as they observe the limitations, the prejudices, the idiosyncrasies of the speaker. Fiction here is likely to contain more truth than fact. Therefore I propose, making use of all the liberties and licences of a novelist4, to tell you the story of the two days that preceded my coming here… I need not say that what I am about to describe has no existence; Oxbridge is an invention; so is Fernham; ‘I’ is only a convenient term for somebody who has no real being.5 Lies will flow from my lips, but there may perhaps be some truth mixed up with them6; it is for you to seek out this truth and to decide whether any part of it is worth keeping.

A Room of One’s Own, Chapter One · public-domain text · one connecting stretch shortened

1

“Minor”

The word is armour. Woolf was a woman addressing an ancient university that would not grant women full membership—Cambridge did not until 1948—and the modesty is tactical: a “minor point” slips past defences that a manifesto would raise. It is also a joke the whole book unpacks, since the minor point turns out to contain the economics of literature entire.

2

Materialism, stated plainly

Not genius, not encouragement, not sympathy: money, and a lockable door. Later chapters price it—five hundred pounds a year, the income Woolf’s fictional narrator inherits from an aunt. The claim was and remains provocative because it moves the discussion of women’s writing from talent (a question about women) to conditions (a question about what has been withheld from them). Genius, the book argues, dies undocumented in interrupted sitting-rooms.

3

Stepping down from the lecturer’s chair

A lecture is supposed to conclude; Woolf announces she has “shirked” that duty. On controversial subjects, she says, truth-telling is impossible—one can only display the making of an opinion, prejudices included, and let the audience judge. The method is the argument: where male authorities on women pronounce (the British Museum shelves full of them appear in Chapter Two), she will narrate, and trust her listeners.

4

An essay borrows fiction’s tools

“Fiction here is likely to contain more truth than fact” licenses everything that follows: invented colleges, an invented lunch and dinner (the famous partridges against the prunes), an invented sister for Shakespeare. Woolf takes the lecture—the driest of forms—and rebuilds it as narrative, because narrative can show what statistics assert: what a mind feels like when it is fed, funded and left alone, and when it is not.

5

The vanishing speaker

The quotation marks around ‘I’ are the passage’s most radical mark of punctuation. The speaker dissolves into “a convenient term”—call her, the next sentence offers, Mary Beton, Mary Seton, or any name you please. A woman’s ‘I’ had rarely been given authority in such rooms; Woolf does not try to claim it, and makes the speaker a fiction, so that the argument stands whoever speaks it.

6

Lies with truth mixed in

The lecturer promises to lie—and hands the audience the work of sieving. It is a startling contract, and a deeply respectful one: “it is for you to seek out this truth… If not, you will of course throw the whole of it into the waste-paper basket and forget all about it.” The book ends the same way it begins, transferring responsibility to the young women listening; its last pages ask them to write.

What the wording changes

The argument performs its own conditions.

The opening chapters dramatise exactly what the thesis claims. The narrator is turned off the grass at “Oxbridge” by a beadle; a library door is barred to her without a fellow’s introduction; she compares the sole and partridges of a men’s college lunch with the gravy soup and prunes served at “Fernham”. Each anecdote is invented and each is evidence: the difference between the two meals, Woolf suggests, is the difference between literatures. One cannot think well, love well, sleep well, if one has not dined well—the book’s materialism starts at the table.

The famous fiction of Chapter Three grows from this opening’s method. Judith Shakespeare, William’s equally gifted imaginary sister, is a “lie” in precisely the sense promised here—an invented life used to show why the historical record is so quiet: denied school, betrothed young, fled to London, turned from every stage door, dead by her own hand and buried at a cross-roads. No fact about a real Elizabethan woman could carry that argument, because the argument concerns the women about whom no facts were kept.

It is worth remembering who was in the room. Newnham and Girton students were, in 1928, studying at a university that would not give them degrees on equal terms; Woolf’s “you” throughout is those young women. The essay’s ironies, the mock-modesty, the speaker who cancels herself, the promised lies, show how to think freely without waiting for an institution to give permission. That she performs this in prose of complete financial and formal independence—published by a press she and Leonard Woolf owned—is the quiet proof of the thesis: she had the money and the room, and this book is what they bought.

Take one question away

Woolf claims that on controversial subjects one can only show how an opinion was formed, never prove it. Is that a limitation she accepts—or a higher standard she sets?

Before reading

Notice the setting of the claim.

The thesis arrives in the first paragraph, almost as an aside, flanked by apologies—“All I could do”, “one minor point”. Mark every gesture of modesty in the passage, then ask what each one is protecting.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Test the method.

  1. List what the passage promises: no conclusion, invented places, a fictional speaker, lies with truth mixed in.
  2. For each promise, find where a conventional lecture would do the opposite.
  3. Debate: does the fictional method make the argument easier to dismiss, or harder?
  4. Finish by drafting the one-sentence “minor point” you would defend about writing today, in Woolf’s syntax: a writer must have ___ and ___ if she is to ___.
Essay bridge

Judith Shakespeare’s evidence.

For an essay on the whole book, trace how this opening’s contract—fiction as the carrier of truth—pays off in the Judith Shakespeare chapter, and weigh Woolf’s closing charge to her audience: that Judith “lives in you and in me”, and will be born when women write.
