Passage Room/The Metamorphosis/A horrible vermin

Close reading 46 · The Metamorphosis

“transformed… into a horrible vermin”

In the first sentence a man wakes up as vermin, and the prose shows no surprise. Nobody in the story, least of all Gregor, treats it as a horror.

Franz KafkaPart I · the opening

Written in 1912, published in 1915. Kafka wrote in German; the passage below follows David Wyllie’s public-domain English translation, with the original noun discussed in the notes.

How to read this page: notice what the sentence spends its words on, and what it hurries past. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The MetamorphosisPart I

One morning, when Gregor Samsa woke from troubled dreams1, he found himself transformed in his bed into a horrible vermin.2 He lay on his armour-like back3, and if he lifted his head a little he could see his brown belly, slightly domed and divided by arches into stiff sections.

“What’s happened to me?” he thought. It wasn’t a dream.4 His room, a proper human room although a little too small, lay peacefully between its four familiar walls.

Gregor then turned to look out the window at the dull weather. … “How about if I sleep a little bit longer and forget all this nonsense5”, he thought, but that was something he was unable to do because he was used to sleeping on his right, and in his present state couldn’t get into that position.6

The Metamorphosis, Part I · David Wyllie translation · public-domain text · one clause shortened

1

Troubled dreams

“Woke from troubled dreams” closes off the easy way out. Gregor has just left his dreams behind; he is awake, in a world where the impossible is simply the case. Kafka rules out the “it was all a dream” reading in the first clause, so the reader has nowhere to put the event except reality.

2

The untranslatable creature

Kafka’s German is “ein ungeheueres Ungeziefer”. Ungeziefer is an old word for unclean animals unfit for sacrifice, and it names no species: no beetle, no cockroach, only vermin. When the story was first published, Kafka asked his publisher not to have the insect itself drawn on the cover. Translators have tried “insect”, “bug”, “vermin”; each narrows what Kafka kept vague. The thing Gregor becomes is defined only as something loathsome and unhouseable, which is also how his family comes to see him.

3

Precision where we expect panic

Having announced a metamorphosis, the prose turns entomological: armour-like back, domed brown belly, arched stiff sections. Kafka describes the new body with the calm exactness of a naturalist’s field note. The mismatch between the enormity of the event and the flatness of the description sets the tone of the whole story: horror reported in the register of an inventory.

4

The wrong-sized question

Waking as an insect, Gregor asks “What’s happened to me?”, and the narrator answers, dryly, “It wasn’t a dream.” The question is far too small for the event: the mild puzzlement of a man who has misplaced his keys. The story is a black comedy, and much of the comedy lies in this mismatch of scale, a mind meeting a catastrophe with the equipment of an ordinary morning.

5

“All this nonsense”

Gregor’s response to being transformed is to consider going back to sleep and forgetting it. He calls it “nonsense”, an inconvenience. Everyone, including Gregor, goes on to treat the transformation as a domestic problem. No one asks how or why. Because nobody marvels at it, the miracle stops looking like a miracle and starts looking like a diagnosis.

6

The first worry is a small one

His new condition registers first as a sleeping-position problem: he likes to lie on his right and now cannot. Within a page his worries will be the 5 o’clock train, his hated job, and the debt his family owes his employer. The enormous change is immediately absorbed into the anxieties of a put-upon commercial traveller. Kafka makes the point without fuss: Gregor was already living so reduced a life that turning into an insect barely changes his list of worries.

What the wording changes

Kafka’s flat voice

Most fantastic stories spend their openings persuading you that the impossible has occurred. Kafka states the transformation as settled fact in the first sentence, and the rest of the paragraph shows no astonishment: it describes the insect body with clinical calm and lets Gregor call the change “nonsense” and worry about his train. Because the prose does not react, the reader has to supply the horror the characters withhold, and comes to see the withholding as part of the horror.

We never learn a cause: no curse, no experiment, no punishment for some moral fault. Gregor simply is vermin now, and the family’s slow shift from shock to disgust to relief at his death follows the logic of a household adjusting to an inconvenience. Read one way, the change makes literal what Gregor already was: the family’s uncomplaining provider, worked to exhaustion and valued for his wage, a useful creature who becomes unbearable the moment he stops being useful. The insect body makes visible a status he already held.

That is why the untranslatable Ungeziefer matters. Because Kafka keeps it vague, the transformation is about more than a bug: it is about becoming the thing a family cannot love and cannot house, unclean, unnameable and in the way. The novella has been read as a parable of illness, of alienated labour, of the outsider, of Kafka’s own suffocating relation to his father. It can carry all of these because it specifies so little. The first sentence gives a fact and no explanation, and readers have been filling the silence for more than a century.

Take one question away

The transformation is never explained and never doubted. Does the story explain how Gregor became vermin, or show that he had become it long before?

Before reading

Weigh the sentence’s halves.

Split the first sentence at the comma. Count how many words announce the miracle and how many describe the ordinary (dreams, bed, morning). Kafka spends more care on the frame than the event. Ask what that ratio does to the tone.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Name the creature.

  1. List the English words translators have used for Ungeziefer: insect, bug, vermin, cockroach.
  2. For each, say what it adds or removes: species, size, disgust, specificity.
  3. Decide why Kafka forbade any picture of it on the cover.
  4. Rewrite the first sentence naming an exact animal, and describe what the story loses.
Essay bridge

The useful son.

For an essay on labour and family, trace Gregor’s worries from this opening (the train, the job, the family’s debt) through the family’s changing treatment of him to the relief that follows his death. Ask whether the metamorphosis creates his situation or only exposes the one he was already living.
