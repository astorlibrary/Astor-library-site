Passage Room/The Metamorphosis/A horrible vermin

Close reading 46 · The Metamorphosis

“transformed… into a horrible vermin”

The most famous opening in modern fiction performs a miracle and refuses to be amazed by it. The horror is already there in the first sentence; the strangeness is that no one—least of all Gregor—treats it as horror.

Franz KafkaPart I · the opening

Written in 1912, published in 1915. Kafka wrote in German; the passage below follows David Wyllie’s public-domain English translation, with the original noun discussed in the notes.

How to read this page: notice what the sentence spends its words on—and what it hurries past. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The MetamorphosisPart I

One morning, when Gregor Samsa woke from troubled dreams1, he found himself transformed in his bed into a horrible vermin.2 He lay on his armour-like back3, and if he lifted his head a little he could see his brown belly, slightly domed and divided by arches into stiff sections.

“What’s happened to me?” he thought. It wasn’t a dream.4 His room, a proper human room although a little too small, lay peacefully between its four familiar walls.

Gregor then turned to look out the window at the dull weather. … “How about if I sleep a little bit longer and forget all this nonsense5”, he thought, but that was something he was unable to do because he was used to sleeping on his right, and in his present state couldn’t get into that position.6

The Metamorphosis, Part I · David Wyllie translation · public-domain text · one clause shortened

1

The dreams are over before the horror begins

“Woke from troubled dreams” deliberately closes off the easy escape route. This is not a nightmare Gregor is inside; he has just left his dreams behind and entered a waking world in which the impossible is simply the case. Kafka forecloses the “it was all a dream” reading in the first clause, so the reader has nowhere to put the event except reality.

2

The untranslatable creature

Kafka’s German is “ein ungeheueres Ungeziefer”. Ungeziefer is an old word for unclean vermin unfit for sacrifice—deliberately unspecific: not “beetle”, not “cockroach”, just vermin. Kafka insisted the creature never be drawn on the cover, precisely because the horror is that it has no exact name. Translators have tried “insect”, “bug”, “vermin”; each narrows what Kafka kept vague. The thing Gregor becomes is defined only as something loathsome and unhouseable—which is also, the story will show, how his family comes to see him.

3

Precision where we expect panic

Having announced a metamorphosis, the prose turns entomological: armour-like back, domed brown belly, arched stiff sections. Kafka describes the new body with the calm exactness of a naturalist’s field note. The mismatch between the enormity of the event and the flatness of the description is the story’s whole tone—horror reported in the register of an inventory.

4

The wrong-sized question

Waking as an insect, Gregor asks “What’s happened to me?”—and the narrator answers, dryly, “It wasn’t a dream.” The question is far too small for the event; it is the mild puzzlement of a man who has misplaced his keys. Kafka’s comedy—and the story is, blackly, comic—lives in this scaling error, a mind meeting a catastrophe with the equipment of an ordinary morning.

5

“All this nonsense”

Gregor’s response to being transformed is to consider going back to sleep and forgetting it. He calls it “nonsense”—an inconvenience, not an apocalypse. This is the story’s deepest and strangest move: the transformation is treated by everyone, including its victim, as a domestic problem. No one asks how or why. The refusal of wonder is what makes the book uncanny; a miracle no one marvels at stops looking like a miracle and starts looking like a diagnosis.

6

The first worry is a small one

His new condition registers first as a sleeping-position problem: he likes to lie on his right and now cannot. Within a page his worries will be the 5 o’clock train, his hated job, and the debt his family owes his employer. The enormous change is immediately absorbed into the anxieties of a put-upon commercial traveller. Kafka’s point is quietly devastating—Gregor was already living a life so reduced that turning into an insect barely alters his list of concerns.

What the wording changes

A miracle told in the flattest possible voice.

Almost every fantastical story spends its opening persuading you that the impossible has occurred. Kafka does the reverse. The transformation is stated as settled fact in the first sentence, and the rest of the paragraph declines to be astonished by it—describing the insect body with clinical calm, letting Gregor treat the change as “nonsense” and worry about his train. The flatness is not a failure of drama; it is the drama. By refusing to react, the prose forces the reader to supply the horror the characters withhold, and to notice that the withholding is itself the horror.

The story’s genius is that the metamorphosis explains nothing and everything. We never learn a cause—no curse, no experiment, no moral transgression punished. Gregor simply is vermin now, and the family’s slow shift from shock to disgust to relief at his death proceeds with the logic of a household adjusting to an inconvenience. Read one way, the change literalises what Gregor already was: the family’s uncomplaining provider, worked to exhaustion, valued only for his wage—a useful creature who becomes, the moment he stops being useful, an unbearable one. The insect body only makes visible a status he already held.

That is why the untranslatable Ungeziefer matters so much. Kafka’s vagueness keeps the transformation from being merely a fantasy about a bug; it is about becoming the thing a family cannot love and cannot house—unclean, unnameable, in the way. The novella has been read as a parable of illness, of alienated labour, of the outsider, of Kafka’s own suffocating relation to his father. It sustains all of these because it specifies so little. The first sentence gives you a fact and no explanation, and eighty years of readers have been filling the silence ever since.

Take one question away

The transformation is never explained and never doubted. Does the story ask what turned Gregor into vermin—or reveal that he had already become it?

Before reading

Weigh the sentence’s halves.

Split the first sentence at the comma. Count how many words announce the miracle and how many describe the ordinary (dreams, bed, morning). Kafka spends more care on the frame than the event. Ask what that ratio does to the tone.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Name the creature.

  1. List the English words translators have used for Ungeziefer: insect, bug, vermin, cockroach.
  2. For each, say what it adds or removes—species, size, disgust, specificity.
  3. Decide why Kafka forbade any picture of it on the cover.
  4. Rewrite the first sentence naming an exact animal, and describe what the story loses.
Essay bridge

The useful son.

For an essay on labour and family, trace Gregor’s worries from this opening—the train, the job, the family’s debt—through the family’s changing treatment of him to the relief that follows his death. Ask whether the metamorphosis creates his situation or merely exposes the one he was already living.
