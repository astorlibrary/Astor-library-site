Close reading 79 · Henry IV, Part 2

“Uneasy lies the head that wears a crown”

At one in the morning, in his nightgown, the king who took the throne by force asks why sleep will visit a sailor-boy lashed to a mast in a storm and not him. The famous last line is the least interesting thing in the speech.

William ShakespeareAct 3, Scene 1

Probably written around 1597–98. Henry IV, sick and beset by rebellion, has sent for his lords in the middle of the night. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: follow the speech from the cottages to the ship to the palace—it is a tour of who sleeps. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Henry IV, Part 23.1

KingHow many thousands of my poorest subjects
Are at this hour asleep!1 O sleep, O gentle sleep,
Nature’s soft nurse, how have I frightened thee2,
That thou no more will weigh my eyelids down,
5And steep my senses in forgetfulness?
Why rather, sleep, liest thou in smoky cribs,
Upon uneasy pallets stretching thee,
And hush’d with buzzing night-flies to thy slumber,
Than in the perfum’d chambers of the great,
10Under the canopies of costly state,
And lull’d with sound of sweetest melody?
O thou dull god, why liest thou with the vile
In loathsome beds, and leav’st the kingly couch
A watch-case or a common ’larum-bell?3
15Wilt thou upon the high and giddy mast
Seal up the ship-boy’s eyes, and rock his brains
In cradle of the rude imperious surge4,
And in the visitation of the winds,
Who take the ruffian billows by the top,
20Curling their monstrous heads, and hanging them
With deafing clamour in the slippery clouds,
That with the hurly death itself awakes?
Canst thou, O partial sleep, give thy repose
To the wet sea-boy in an hour so rude;
25And in the calmest and most stillest night,
With all appliances and means to boot,
Deny it to a king?5 Then, happy low, lie down!
Uneasy lies the head that wears a crown.6

Henry IV, Part 2, Act 3, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered from the start of the apostrophe

1

The count begins with the poor

The king’s first thought is of his “poorest subjects”—thousands of them, all asleep. The speech is structured as an inventory of who sleeps and who does not, and it starts at the bottom. Henry envies the poor before he envies anyone; the crown’s cost is measured against a cottage.

2

Sleep as a nurse he has frightened

Sleep is “nature’s soft nurse”, and the king has scared her away—“how have I frightened thee”. The verb admits fault. He does not say sleep has left him; he says he has driven her off, and the play knows how: by deposing Richard II, a crime the whole sequence comes back to again and again. The insomnia is guilt wearing the costume of a complaint about office.

3

A watch-case

The royal bed has become “a watch-case or a common ’larum-bell”—a sentry-box, an alarm. The image is precise: the king lies in his bed as a watchman stands in his box, on duty all night, and the bed itself has become a thing that rings. Sleep prefers “loathsome beds” and “smoky cribs” to this. Comfort, the speech argues, is not the same as rest.

4

The ship-boy

The speech’s great set-piece: a boy at the masthead in a storm, rocked to sleep by waves that would wake death itself. Every line of the storm is violent—ruffian billows, monstrous heads, deafing clamour—and the boy sleeps through it. Shakespeare gives the storm eight lines and the sleep one, so that the reader feels the enormity of what sleep can overcome, and therefore the enormity of what keeps a king awake.

5

The unanswered question

“Deny it to a king?” The speech has been one long question addressed to sleep, and sleep does not reply. Henry lists his advantages—“all appliances and means”, the calmest night—and finds them useless. The question is rhetorical and also real: he does not understand, or will not say, why the crown weighs on him as it does.

6

The proverb at the end

The famous line is a couplet’s worth of summary, delivered like a motto: “Uneasy lies the head that wears a crown.” It has become a proverb, and it is the least particular line in the speech—everything before it was this king, this night, this storm; the last line is any king. Shakespeare lets Henry retreat from his own specificity into a general truth, which is what a guilty man does when the particular gets too close.

What the wording changes

A usurper’s insomnia, dressed as the burden of office.

The speech is usually quoted as a meditation on the loneliness of power, and it is; but its placement makes it something more. Henry IV took the crown from Richard II in the play that bears Richard’s name, and the two parts of Henry IV are the long aftermath—rebellions, a wayward heir, a kingdom that will not settle. When the king asks why he cannot sleep, the audience knows an answer he does not give. A few lines later, in the same scene, he remembers Richard’s prophecy that the men who helped depose him would turn on him too. The insomnia is political and it is moral, and by blaming sleep instead of himself the speech leaves both open.

The structure is a descending comparison: the poorest subjects, the smoky cottages, the ship-boy in the storm, and finally the king. At each step the sleeper has less and sleeps better, until the boy at the masthead, with nothing at all, sleeps through a tempest. The rhetoric is designed to make the crown look like the one thing in the kingdom that prevents rest—which flatters Henry (his burden is uniquely heavy) while indicting him (he chose to pick it up). Shakespeare gives the king’s self-pity real eloquence and lets the audience hear what the eloquence is for.

The speech also prepares the play’s ending. Henry will die in this play, in a chamber called Jerusalem, having never reached the Holy Land he promised to crusade in as penance; and Hal, taking the crown from his father’s pillow, will speak his own meditation on its weight. Father and son both discover that the crown keeps you awake. The difference is that Hal inherits it, and the play’s wager—tested in Henry V—is that inheritance sleeps better than usurpation.

Take one question away

Henry blames the crown for his sleeplessness and never names Richard II. Is the speech about the burden of kingship, or a guilty man finding a nobler reason for lying awake?

Before reading

Track the sleepers.

List everyone in the speech who sleeps: the poorest subjects, the vile in loathsome beds, the ship-boy. Note what each has. Then note what the king has. The speech is a ladder, and Henry is standing at the top with nothing.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Name the cause.

  1. List the reasons the speech gives for the king’s sleeplessness.
  2. List the reasons the play has given that the speech does not mention.
  3. Read the lines that follow, where Henry recalls Richard’s prophecy, and ask why they come after the speech and not in it.
  4. Decide whether the last line is wisdom or evasion.
Essay bridge

Two heads and one crown.

For an essay on kingship in the tetralogy, set this speech beside Hal’s address to the crown on his father’s pillow in Act 4 and Henry V’s “Upon the king” on the night before Agincourt. Three men, three sleepless nights; ask what changes when the crown is inherited and not seized.
