“Who has been taking my bed?”

Penelope does not recognise her husband because a goddess has made him handsome again. She recognises the knowledge contained in his angry account of a bed that cannot be moved.

Homer · translated by Samuel ButlerBook XXIII

The poem emerged from ancient Greek oral tradition. This page follows Butler’s 1900 prose translation, which uses the Roman names Ulysses and Minerva.

How to read this page: look for acts of making as well as signs of love. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to see why the bed proves identity.

The OdysseyBook XXIII

“Nevertheless, Euryclea, take his bed outside the bed chamber1 that he himself built.”

She said this to try him, but Ulysses was very angry and said, “Wife […] Who has been taking my bed2 from the place in which I left it? […] There is no man living […] who could move it from its place, for it is a marvellous curiosity which I made with my very own hands3.

“There was a young olive growing within the precincts of the house4 […] I built my room round this […] Then I cut off the top boughs of the olive tree and left the stump standing. […] I made it the centre-post of my bed.”

When she heard the sure proofs Ulysses now gave her5, she fairly broke down.

The Odyssey, Book XXIII · Samuel Butler translation · public-domain text · ellipses mark shortened stretches

1

A domestic command with a trap inside

Penelope’s instruction sounds practical, but it proposes the impossible. Her caution becomes a test built from knowledge only the couple shares.

2

Anger proves more than appearance

Ulysses does not calmly supply a password. He reacts as the bed’s maker and owner, exposing the depth of his knowledge before he knows he is being examined.

3

The returning warrior is also a craftsman

“My very own hands” grounds identity in work. Penelope recognises not a public hero from Troy but the man who planned and made their private room.

4

A living foundation

The bed is built around a rooted olive tree. Marriage, household and Ithacan soil become physically joined; moving the bed would mean cutting through that living centre.

5

Recognition requires proof

Butler’s plural “proofs” matters. Penelope does not surrender judgment to longing, altered appearance or another person’s assurance. She tests and then believes.

What the wording changes

Homecoming is proved by making.

Throughout the poem, identity can be disguised, narrated and falsely claimed. Penelope has survived by delaying and testing. Her final caution is not coldness but an intelligence equal to the returning hero’s: she invents a trial he cannot knowingly prepare for.

Ulysses’s reply moves through the bed’s construction step by step—tree, walls, tools, post, inlay, leather. The detail is proof because it belongs to work performed before the long absence and to a room kept from public view. Recognition rests on shared history made material.

The olive bed also changes what “home” means. Ulysses has travelled through fabulous places and reclaimed his palace by force, but the marriage is restored around something rooted, cultivated and immovable. Penelope’s test makes nostos, homecoming, more than arrival at an address: it is the recovery of a private life that only these two can fully know.

Take one question away

Why does the poem make a piece of skilled domestic work—not a scar, a victory or divine transformation—the final proof between husband and wife?
