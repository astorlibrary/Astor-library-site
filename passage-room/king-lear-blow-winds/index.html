Passage Room/King Lear/Blow, winds

Close reading 17 · King Lear

“Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks!”

Shut out of both his daughters’ houses, Lear stands in open country and gives orders to the weather. The commands cannot be obeyed—and inside them, for the first time, a king begins to describe himself accurately.

William ShakespeareAct 3, Scene 2

Probably written 1605–06 and performed at court in December 1606. The quarto of 1608 and the 1623 Folio differ throughout; the wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read both speeches aloud if you can—the sound is half the argument. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

King Lear3.2

LEAR.
Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks! Rage! blow!1
You cataracts and hurricanoes, spout
Till you have drench’d our steeples, drown’d the cocks!2
You sulphurous and thought-executing fires,
Vaunt-couriers to oak-cleaving thunderbolts3,
Singe my white head! And thou, all-shaking thunder,
Strike flat the thick rotundity o’ the world!
Crack nature’s moulds, all germens spill at once,
That make ingrateful man!4

FOOL.
O nuncle, court holy-water5 in a dry house is better than this rain-water out o’ door. Good nuncle, in; and ask thy daughters blessing: here’s a night pities neither wise men nor fools.

LEAR.
Rumble thy bellyful! Spit, fire! spout, rain!
Nor rain, wind, thunder, fire are my daughters;
I tax not you, you elements, with unkindness;
I never gave you kingdom, call’d you children6;
You owe me no subscription: then let fall
Your horrible pleasure. Here I stand your slave,
A poor, infirm, weak, and despis’d old man7:
But yet I call you servile ministers,
That will with two pernicious daughters join
Your high-engender’d battles ’gainst a head
So old and white as this! O! O! ’tis foul!

King Lear, Act 3, Scene 2 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

Orders no one can obey

Old maps drew the winds as faces with puffed cheeks, blowing across the sea; Lear commands those faces to blow until they burst. The speech is built of imperatives—blow, rage, spout, singe, strike, crack—the grammar of a king. But weather takes no orders, so the form of royal command survives here with nothing left that must obey it.

2

A second Flood

“Cataracts” are the floodgates of heaven; “hurricanoes” were waterspouts, a new and exotic word in English. Drowned steeples and weathercocks put whole towns underwater. Lear is not asking for shelter or revenge on two women: he is asking for something on the scale of Noah’s deluge.

3

Lightning quick as thinking

“Thought-executing” can mean acting as fast as thought—and, more darkly, executing it, killing thought itself. “Vaunt-couriers” (from French avant) are the scouts who ride ahead of an army: the lightning outrides the thunderbolt that splits oaks. Even raving, Lear observes the storm precisely—flash first, then the crack.

4

Cancel the species

“Germens” are seeds—the germs of all future life; nature’s “moulds” are the forms living things are cast in. Lear asks the storm to smash the casts and spill every seed at once, so that no more human beings can ever be made. The reason he gives is one word: ingratitude. Two daughters have failed him, so mankind should end.

5

The Fool’s cold arithmetic

“Court holy-water” was a proverbial phrase for flattery—the empty blessings sprinkled around palaces. The Fool’s advice is deliberately shameful: go back, flatter, beg your daughters’ blessing, because flattery indoors beats truth in the rain. His prose keeps undercutting Lear’s verse; the play lets us hear grandeur and survival argue.

6

The storm owes him nothing

“Tax” means accuse; “subscription” is sworn allegiance. Lear acquits the elements on strictly feudal grounds: he never gave them a kingdom, never called them children, so their cruelty is not treachery. He still cannot think outside the vocabulary of contract, obligation and gift—the very frame of mind that began the tragedy, when love was measured out in return for land.

7

The first true description

Four plain adjectives, none of them royal. Lear has been addressed all his life through titles; this is the first time he names himself as what the storm sees. The play will push the thought further in the next scene, when he meets a naked beggar and asks whether man is no more than this. Self-knowledge in Lear arrives not as comfort but as exposure.

What the wording changes

The commands fail; the failure teaches.

The speech is often played as pure spectacle, and it earns that: no other scene in Shakespeare asks an actor to outshout the sound effects. But its logic is worth following. Lear begins by ordering the storm about like an army, moves to acquitting it like a judge, and ends by accusing it of taking sides—“servile ministers” leagued with his daughters. Command, acquittal, accusation: three attempts to keep a relationship with a universe that has none with him.

Inside that failure something genuine happens. “Here I stand your slave, / A poor, infirm, weak, and despis’d old man” reverses every term of the play’s opening scene, where Lear sat enthroned, distributing a map of Britain in exchange for declarations of love. The storm completes what Goneril and Regan began: it strips the difference between a king and any other cold old man. Lear’s madness and his insight grow from the same root, and this scene is where they first become inseparable.

The Fool matters to the balance. His interruption is funny, practical and morally uncomfortable—he recommends the flattery that ruined Lear in the first place. Shakespeare keeps both voices on stage because neither is sufficient: the Fool’s prudence would have prevented the tragedy, and Lear’s excess is what makes the tragedy mean anything.

Take one question away

Lear asks the storm to destroy all mankind for his daughters’ ingratitude. Is this the old tyranny speaking—or the beginning of his cure?

Before reading

Count the commands.

Underline every imperative verb in Lear’s two speeches. Circle every word drawn from law, service or family—tax, subscription, slave, ministers, children, daughters. The two lists are the scene’s two halves: a king’s grammar, and the obligations he cannot stop reckoning.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Stage the impossible.

  1. In pairs, decide how the storm should be produced: sound design, lighting, silence?
  2. Choose one line the actor should whisper rather than shout, and justify it.
  3. Decide where the Fool stands, and whether the audience should laugh.
  4. Compare stagings: what does each choice claim about whether the storm is outside Lear or inside him?
Essay bridge

From the throne to the hovel.

For an essay on power, nature or madness, set this speech between two others: “Tell me, my daughters… which of you shall we say doth love us most?” in 1.1, and “Poor naked wretches” in 3.4, where Lear first prays for others. The storm scene is the hinge: command failing, pity not yet found.
