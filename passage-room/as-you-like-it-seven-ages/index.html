“All the world’s a stage”

The most quoted speech in the comedies is spoken by its most melancholy character—and the play answers it, in the same minute, by carrying an exhausted old man on stage and giving him supper.

William ShakespeareAct 2, Scene 7

Probably written around 1599. Jaques, the exiled Duke’s resident cynic, speaks in the Forest of Arden. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: notice that every age is described from the outside, with a satirist’s eye. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

As You Like It2.7

JaquesAll the world’s a stage,
And all the men and women merely players1;
They have their exits and their entrances,
And one man in his time plays many parts,
5His acts being seven ages. At first the infant,
Mewling and puking in the nurse’s arms2;
Then the whining schoolboy, with his satchel
And shining morning face, creeping like snail
Unwillingly to school. And then the lover,
10Sighing like furnace, with a woeful ballad
Made to his mistress’ eyebrow. Then a soldier,
Full of strange oaths and bearded like the pard,
Jealous in honour, sudden and quick in quarrel,
Seeking the bubble reputation
15Even in the cannon’s mouth3. And then the justice,
In fair round belly with good capon lined,
With eyes severe and beard of formal cut,
Full of wise saws and modern instances;
And so he plays his part. The sixth age shifts
20Into the lean and slippered pantaloon,
With spectacles on nose and pouch on side,
His youthful hose, well saved, a world too wide
For his shrunk shank4, and his big manly voice,
Turning again toward childish treble, pipes
25And whistles in his sound. Last scene of all,
That ends this strange eventful history,
Is second childishness and mere oblivion5,
Sans teeth, sans eyes, sans taste, sans everything.6

Enter Orlando bearing Adam.

As You Like It, Act 2, Scene 7 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered from the start of the speech

1

A commonplace, brilliantly done

The idea that the world is a theatre was proverbial long before Shakespeare—the Globe’s own motto is thought to have been a Latin version of it. Jaques’s contribution is “merely”: men and women are merely players, and the word drains the metaphor of dignity. This is not the theatre as glorious mirror of life but as a cast of puppets going through routines. The speech’s fame has softened a sneer.

2

The satirist’s eye

Every age is caught in its least flattering posture: the infant “mewling and puking”, the schoolboy “whining” and “creeping like snail”, the lover “sighing like furnace” over a ballad to an eyebrow. Jaques observes humanity as a collector of specimens, from the outside, with a taxonomist’s cold accuracy. The details are funny and exact, and there is no love in any of them.

3

The bubble in the cannon’s mouth

The soldier seeks “the bubble reputation / Even in the cannon’s mouth”—honour as a soap-bubble, pursued into the one place guaranteed to burst it. It is the speech’s most compressed judgment: the thing men die for is weightless and momentary, and they die for it anyway. Compare Falstaff’s “What is honour? A word”; Jaques says the same in an image.

4

The clothes outlast the man

The old man’s stockings, “well saved” from youth, are now “a world too wide / For his shrunk shank”. The detail is pitiless and observant: thrift has preserved the hose while time has shrunk the leg inside them. The “pantaloon” is a stock figure of Italian comedy—the foolish old man—so even age is a theatrical type, played from a script.

5

The circle closes

The last age is “second childishness”—the infant of line 5 returning, but without the promise. “Mere oblivion”: the word “mere” again, meaning utter, nothing but. The seven ages end where they began, in helplessness, and Jaques makes the shape of a life into a joke with no punchline.

6

Sans

The French word for “without”, hammered four times, strips the old man of teeth, eyes, taste and finally “everything”. The line’s rhythm is a subtraction sum, and it ends on nothing. Then the stage direction: Orlando enters carrying old Adam, faint with hunger but loyal, loved and about to be fed. The play answers Jaques’s bleak arithmetic with a body on stage that contradicts it—an old man who is not “sans everything” because someone is carrying him.

What the wording changes

A famous speech the play immediately disagrees with.

Quoted alone, the seven ages read as Shakespeare’s wisdom on the human span. In the scene, they are Jaques’s—and Jaques is the play’s designated melancholic, a man the other characters enjoy and gently mock, who weeps over a wounded deer and sulks at weddings. Shakespeare gives the cynic the best set-piece in the play and then, before the applause can settle, refutes it with an entrance. Orlando carries Adam in, an old servant who has followed his young master into exile out of pure devotion. He is nearly “sans everything” and he is the most loved man on stage. The Duke feeds him. The speech’s theory of age dissolves against the fact of kindness.

This is typical of As You Like It, a comedy made of points of view more than of plot. Almost nothing happens in Arden; instead people talk, and the play sets their ways of seeing against each other—Jaques’s satire, Touchstone’s clowning, Rosalind’s wit, the Duke’s stoic content. The seven ages are one lens, brilliant and partial. The play does not say Jaques is wrong about mewling infants and shrunk shanks; it says that his description leaves out everything that makes the ages bearable—which is other people.

The theatre metaphor has a further irony. Jaques calls all men “merely players”, and he is one, spoken by an actor on a stage before an audience that has paid to watch players pretend. The Globe’s audience would have felt the wink. But the metaphor cuts both ways: if life is a play, then it can have kindness written into it as easily as cynicism, and the playwright, a few lines later, chooses to write in a rescue. The speech is a great performance of despair; the scene it sits in is a demonstration of why despair is not the last word.

Take one question away

Jaques says life ends “sans everything”, and old Adam is carried in loved and fed. Which does the play believe—and can it believe both?

Before reading

Catch each age in its verb.

List the seven ages and, beside each, the single most unflattering word Jaques uses for it—puking, whining, sighing, and so on. The pattern shows his method: a life seen entirely from the outside, as comedy.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Stage the refutation.

  1. Read the speech aloud, then read the stage direction: “Enter Orlando bearing Adam.”
  2. Decide how long the pause should be before Orlando enters, and what the audience should feel in it.
  3. List what Adam has that Jaques’s seventh age lacks.
  4. Rewrite the last two lines as Adam might answer them.
Essay bridge

Ways of seeing Arden.

For an essay on the play’s structure, treat the speech as one of several competing philosophies—set it beside the Duke’s “Sweet are the uses of adversity”, Touchstone’s parodies of pastoral, and Rosalind’s deflations of romantic love. Ask which voice the play finally backs, or whether its comedy lies in not choosing.
