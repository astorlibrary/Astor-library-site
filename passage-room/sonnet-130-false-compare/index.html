“nothing like the sun”

For twelve lines the poem refuses every compliment the sonnet tradition kept in stock. The couplet explains why: the target was never the woman. It was the lying poetry written about women.

William ShakespeareSonnet 130

Published by Thomas Thorpe in 1609 among the sonnets concerning the so-called Dark Lady (127–154). The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read the whole sonnet first—its shape is the argument. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Sonnets130

My mistress’ eyes are nothing like the sun1;
Coral is far more red, than her lips red:
If snow be white, why then her breasts are dun;
If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head.2
I have seen roses damask’d, red and white3,
But no such roses see I in her cheeks;
And in some perfumes is there more delight
Than in the breath that from my mistress reeks.4
I love to hear her speak, yet well I know
That music hath a far more pleasing sound:
I grant I never saw a goddess go;
My mistress, when she walks, treads on the ground:5
    And yet by heaven, I think my love as rare,
    As any she belied with false compare.6

Shakespeare’s Sonnets, 130 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

The stock compliment, refused

By 1609 the eyes-as-suns comparison was worn smooth: English poets had spent two decades imitating Petrarch’s praises of Laura. The poem’s first line takes the most familiar item in that inventory and flatly denies it. Every quatrain that follows works through the rest of the stockroom—coral lips, snowy breasts, golden hair—checking each against an actual woman.

2

Wires were treasure

The comparison has aged into an insult it never was. Elizabethan “wires” meant the fine gold thread used in jewellery and court embroidery: poets praised hair as golden wires. The line accepts the convention’s own terms—very well, hairs are wires—then reports the colour honestly. Black hair, in the sonnets to this mistress, is its own kind of beauty; Sonnet 127 has already argued that “black” is now beauty’s heir.

3

A gardener’s precision

Damask roses were prized variegated blooms, streaked red and white—the exact two colours convention assigned to a lady’s blushing cheek. The speaker has “seen” them: the verb is observational, almost botanical. He knows the real flower and the real face, and declines to pretend the one grows on the other.

4

“Reeks” has fallen further than the line

In Shakespeare’s English reek meant to breathe out or rise like vapour—stronger than “is fragrant”, but not yet “stinks”. The line says: perfume smells nicer than her breath, which is human. Modern readers hear an insult the word has grown into. Even so, the choice is deliberately unglamorous; the poem wants the plainest true statement the metre will hold.

5

Goddesses glide; she walks

“Go” means walk. Classical goddesses were known by their gait—Venus in the Aeneid is recognised by her step as she moves. The speaker “grants”, in the manner of a man conceding a point in a debate, that he has never actually seen one. His mistress touches the ground when she moves. The couplet is one line away, and the poem has quietly made walking on the earth sound like a virtue.

6

The real accusation

“Any she” means any woman; to “belie” is to misrepresent. The couplet swears—“by heaven”, the poem’s one conventional flourish, now earned—that this love is as rare as any woman who has been lied about with false comparisons. The insult lands on other poets, not other women: their mistresses were real too, until the poetry falsified them.

What the wording changes

A love poem about honest description.

The convention Sonnet 130 dismantles is the blazon: an itemised praise of the beloved, feature by feature, each part matched to a treasure—suns, coral, snow, gold, roses, music. The form flattered the poet as much as the lady, displaying his wit through her inventory. Sonnet 130 keeps the inventory and swaps the verdicts, producing something close to comedy: fourteen lines that sound, until the couplet, like the world’s least promising valentine.

The couplet reorganises everything above it. If the comparisons were false, then declining them is not insult but fidelity—the poem loves a woman rather than a rhetorical exercise. Readers have long noticed that the joke was not entirely new: anti-Petrarchan poems existed across Europe, and Shakespeare’s own audiences knew parodies of overcooked praise. What distinguishes this one is its temperature. It is not savage; it is affectionate about the woman and merciless only about the genre.

A fair question keeps the poem interesting rather than merely charming: who benefits from its honesty? The speaker still runs the descriptions; the mistress still does not speak. Some readers hear tenderness grounded in reality; others hear a subtler performance in which the poet’s wit remains the true subject. The sonnet supports both—which is one reason it has outlived the fashion it mocked.

Take one question away

Does refusing to idealise someone honour them—or is honest description just a quieter way of keeping the describer in charge?

Before reading

Build the poem it answers.

Before reading the sonnet, list the standard compliments: eyes like the sun, lips like coral, breasts like snow, hair of gold wires, cheeks of roses, breath of perfume, a voice of music, the walk of a goddess. Sonnet 130 answers each in order. Read it as a reply.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Write the false version.

  1. Rewrite lines 1–12 as the conventional blazon they negate, keeping the same features.
  2. Read both aloud. Which sounds more loving? Which sounds more true?
  3. Find the single line in 130 where honesty and affection sit closest together.
  4. Debate whether the couplet rescues the poem, or whether the poem never needed rescuing.
Essay bridge

Two ways to outdo a comparison.

Sonnet 18 begins “Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day?” and answers by out-praising the summer; Sonnet 130 refuses the game entirely. For an essay on the sonnets’ art, set the two strategies side by side: both end by claiming the poem itself does what comparison cannot.
