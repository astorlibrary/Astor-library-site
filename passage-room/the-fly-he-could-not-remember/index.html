Close reading 66 · The Doves’ Nest and Other Stories

“For the life of him he could not remember”

A prosperous man, reminded of his son killed in the war six years ago, finds he cannot weep. He drops ink on a fly instead, and the story ends in a forgetting so complete that the reader has to carry the grief the character has lost.

Katherine MansfieldThe Fly · the ending

Written in 1922, the year before Mansfield’s death, and collected in The Doves’ Nest (1923). The boss’s only son died in the Great War. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: notice that the story never says what the fly means. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The FlyThe ending

He’s a plucky little devil, thought the boss, and he felt a real admiration for the fly’s courage.1 That was the way to tackle things; that was the right spirit. Never say die; it was only a question of…. But the fly had again finished its laborious task, and the boss had just time to refill his pen, to shake fair and square on the new-cleaned body yet another dark drop2.… All the same, there was something timid and weak about its efforts now, and the boss decided that this time should be the last, as he dipped the pen into the inkpot.

It was. The last blot on the soaked blotting-paper, and the draggled fly lay in it and did not stir.… “Come on,” said the boss. “Look sharp!” And he stirred it with his pen—in vain. Nothing happened or was likely to happen. The fly was dead.3

The boss lifted the corpse on the end of the paper-knife and flung it into the waste-paper basket. But such a grinding feeling of wretchedness seized him that he felt positively frightened.4 He started forward and pressed the bell for Macey.

“Bring me some fresh blotting-paper,” he said, sternly, “and look sharp about it.” And while the old dog padded away he fell to wondering what it was he had been thinking about before. What was it? It was…. He took out his handkerchief and passed it inside his collar. For the life of him he could not remember.5

The Doves’ Nest and Other Stories, “The Fly” · public-domain text · ellipses mark shortened stretches

1

Admiration from the man holding the pen

The boss admires the fly’s courage—“plucky little devil”, “the right spirit”, “never say die”—in the clichés of wartime encouragement, while he is the one dropping the ink. The language is that of the recruiting poster and the memorial speech. Mansfield lets the boss praise endurance in exactly the terms a nation used for its soldiers, and be, at the same moment, the source of the ordeal.

2

“Fair and square”

He shakes the drop “fair and square” onto the fly—the idiom of sportsmanship applied to torment. Each blot is a test set with mock-fairness, and each time the fly cleans itself the boss sets another. The rhythm of the passage is the rhythm of the experiment: relief, admiration, ink, again. Whether the boss is God, fate, the generals, or simply a man who cannot bear his own feeling, the story does not say. It only shows the hand and the pen.

3

Four words

“The fly was dead.” After the drawn-out ordeal, the flattest possible sentence. Mansfield’s prose has been close to the fly—its legs, its wings, its “laborious task”—and now withdraws to a report. The death is small and total, and the boss’s “Look sharp!” to a corpse is the story’s cruellest comedy: the voice of command addressed to something command has killed.

4

Wretchedness without a name

Flinging the fly away, the boss is seized by “such a grinding feeling of wretchedness” that he is “positively frightened”. He does not connect it to anything. Six years ago his son died; minutes ago he tried to weep and could not; now a fly is dead and he is frightened by a feeling he cannot place. The story has moved the grief from the son to the insect and back into the body, where it grinds without a name.

5

The forgetting

The last sentence is a blank: “For the life of him he could not remember.” What he was thinking about was his son. The story ends with the boss unable to recover the thought, and the reader unable to forget it. “For the life of him” is an idiom, and it is also exact—his life, his boasted vigour and success, is what stands between him and the memory. Mansfield lets ordinary speech carry the meaning her character has lost.

What the wording changes

A story about grief that ends by losing it.

“The Fly” is barely five pages long and has generated decades of argument about what the fly stands for. The story sets it up carefully: an old, failing friend visits the boss and mentions, in passing, seeing the graves of both their sons in Belgium; the boss, who has never been able to speak of his boy, sends the friend away and prepares to weep—and finds he cannot. Then the fly falls in the inkpot, and the rest is what you have read. Readers have made the fly the son, the boss himself, mankind under a cruel God, the soldiers under their generals; Mansfield, characteristically, endorses none of these and rules none out. The story is not an allegory with a key but an event with a shape, and the shape is what hurts.

The war is the story’s unspoken subject. Mansfield’s own younger brother, Leslie, was killed in 1915 in a grenade accident in France, and her writing after his death returned obsessively to loss and to New Zealand. “The Fly” was written in the last months of her life, when she was dying of tuberculosis, and it is among her most controlled pieces: no lyricism, no narrator’s sympathy, only the boss’s point of view and the boss’s idioms. The tenderness with which he addresses the fly—“You artful little b….”—is the tenderness he never managed for his son, and it kills what it is spent on.

The ending is the story’s masterstroke and its cruelty. Fiction about grief usually ends in recognition—the mourner feels, at last, what has been lost. Mansfield reverses it. The boss feels something grinding and frightening, then forgets what he was thinking about, calls sternly for fresh blotting-paper, and goes on. Grief has been displaced onto a fly, discharged as torment, and then erased; the son is not mourned but mislaid. What the reader is left holding is precisely the memory the character cannot find—and the story’s last line makes us the only ones in the room who remember the boy.

Take one question away

The boss cannot remember what he was thinking about. The reader can. What has the story done by handing the grief from the character to us?

Before reading

Collect the idioms.

List the ready-made phrases the boss thinks in: plucky little devil, the right spirit, never say die, fair and square, look sharp, for the life of him. They are the language of the war effort and the office. Ask what a man who thinks in slogans cannot think.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Refuse the key.

  1. Propose what the fly “means”: the son, the boss, the soldier, humanity.
  2. For each, find one detail in the passage that fits and one that does not.
  3. Ask whether the story is weaker or stronger for having no single answer.
  4. Write a one-sentence ending in which the boss does remember, and say what the story loses.
Essay bridge

The war in the office.

For an essay on Mansfield and the Great War, set “The Fly” beside her story “The Garden Party”, where a death is also kept at the edge of a comfortable life. Ask how both stories manage grief by displacement, and why Mansfield refused the consolations that war fiction usually offers.
