Close reading 25 · Great Expectations

“was Pip”

A small boy in a churchyard works out, one clause at a time, what the world is and where he fits in it. The sentence saves him for last—and the moment it ends, a convict rises from among the graves.

Charles DickensChapter 1

Serialised in 1860–61 in Dickens’s magazine All the Year Round. The narrator is the adult Pip, looking back at the child he was on the Kent marshes.

How to read this page: read the long sentence in one breath if you can—its structure is the point. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Great ExpectationsChapter 1

Ours was the marsh country, down by the river, within, as the river wound, twenty miles of the sea. My first most vivid and broad impression of the identity of things1 seems to me to have been gained on a memorable raw afternoon towards evening2. At such a time I found out for certain that this bleak place overgrown with nettles was the churchyard; and that Philip Pirrip, late of this parish, and also Georgiana wife of the above, were dead and buried3; and that Alexander, Bartholomew, Abraham, Tobias, and Roger, infant children of the aforesaid, were also dead and buried; and that the dark flat wilderness beyond the churchyard, intersected with dikes and mounds and gates, with scattered cattle feeding on it, was the marshes; and that the low leaden line beyond was the river; and that the distant savage lair from which the wind was rushing was the sea4; and that the small bundle of shivers growing afraid of it all and beginning to cry, was Pip.5

“Hold your noise!” cried a terrible voice6, as a man started up from among the graves at the side of the church porch. “Keep still, you little devil, or I’ll cut your throat!”

Great Expectations, Chapter 1 · public-domain text

1

A child’s philosophy lesson

“The identity of things” is a strikingly abstract phrase for a memory of being five years old—because it is not the child’s phrase. The adult Pip narrates, choosing words the boy could never have used, for an experience the boy alone had. That doubleness—child’s terror, adult’s syntax—runs through the whole novel and is audible in every clause here.

2

Memory setting its scene

“Seems to me to have been gained” is carefully hedged: the narrator admits he is reconstructing. Yet the afternoon itself is “memorable” and “raw”, felt on the skin. Dickens lets certainty and reconstruction sit in one sentence—which is what long memory actually feels like: vivid, and not quite verifiable.

3

Parents made of lettering

Pip knows his father and mother only as inscriptions—the paragraph before this one confesses that he deduced his father’s appearance from the shape of the letters on his tombstone. “Late of this parish” and “wife of the above” are the stone’s own legal formulas, absorbed into the boy’s sense of who his family were. An orphan’s genealogy, written in epitaph.

4

The sea as a beast

The catalogue moves outward in rings—churchyard, marshes, river—and each item is named flatly until the last, where description breaks into metaphor: the sea is a “distant savage lair”, an animal’s den the wind rushes out of. At the edge of the known world the child’s naming fails, and fear supplies the image. From that lair, in a sense, Magwitch is about to come.

5

Last item in the inventory: himself

The sentence’s final discovery is the discoverer. Pip appears in his own list in the third person, as “the small bundle of shivers”—an object among objects, smaller than cattle, defined by cold and fear, “beginning to cry”. Self-knowledge arrives grammatically: after everything else is identified, “was Pip”. It is one of the great sentence-endings in English fiction—identity as the leftover, the thing that remains when the world has been named.

6

Identity interrupted

The child exists for exactly one sentence before someone seizes him. The convict’s first words attack Pip’s crying—the very sign of self the long sentence ended on—and the threat inverts the churchyard’s stillness into violence. The timing is the novel’s design in miniature: Pip’s identity will never again be purely his own; Magwitch, rising from the graves, will secretly write the rest of it.

What the wording changes

One sentence builds a world; one voice takes it over.

The long sentence works by polysyndeton—the deliberate chain of “and that… and that… and that”. Each link is a separate act of finding out, and the repetition mimics how certainty accumulates for a child: this is the churchyard; those are the graves; that is the marsh; that is the river. Dickens could have written six sentences. Joined into one, they become a single expanding moment of consciousness—the afternoon the world clicked into place.

The order of the list is worth attention. It moves from the near and nameable to the far and frightening, and from the dead to the living: buried parents and brothers first, cattle in the middle distance, the wind’s lair at the horizon, and at the very end the one warm item, a crying boy. Pip is introduced as the world’s smallest and most afraid component—which is precisely the self-image his “great expectations” will later teach him to despise, and the novel will teach him to forgive.

Then the interruption. Fiction offers few sharper cuts than the white space between “was Pip” and “Hold your noise!”—self-recognition and seizure, back to back. The convict Pip meets here will turn out to be the hidden source of his fortune, the true author of the gentleman Pip becomes. Dickens plants that irony in the very grammar of the opening: no sooner does Pip conclude who he is than another voice starts dictating.

Take one question away

The adult narrator gives the child’s terror a beautifully controlled sentence. What does that control add—and what might it soften or betray?

Before reading

Map the rings.

List the items the sentence identifies in order: churchyard, parents, brothers, marshes, river, sea, Pip. Sketch them as rings around the boy. Notice the direction of travel—outward through the landscape, then suddenly back to the centre.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Break the sentence.

  1. Rewrite the long sentence as six short ones.
  2. Read both versions aloud. What disappears with the “and thats”?
  3. Move “was Pip” to the start of its clause (“and that Pip was the small bundle…”). What dies?
  4. Write one sentence of your own that saves its subject for last, and test it on the group.
Essay bridge

Who authors Pip?

For an essay on identity or narration, connect this opening to two later moments: Pip’s shame at Joe’s visit to London, and the revelation that Magwitch, not Miss Havisham, made him a gentleman. The novel keeps asking the question its first sentence stages—whether Pip names himself, or is named by others.
