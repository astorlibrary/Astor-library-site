Close reading 38 · The Scarlet Letter

“a wild rose-bush”

The novel opens at a prison door in Puritan Boston—iron, oak and weather-stains—with one wild rose-bush flowering at the threshold. Before any character appears, Hawthorne has laid out the whole book’s argument in a doorway.

Nathaniel HawthorneChapter 1 · The Prison-Door

Published in 1850. The first chapter is barely a page long: a threshold the narrative pauses on before Hester Prynne steps through it. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: treat every object as a candidate symbol—the narrator does. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Scarlet LetterChapter 1

The rust on the ponderous iron-work of its oaken door looked more antique than anything else in the New World.1 Like all that pertains to crime, it seemed never to have known a youthful era. Before this ugly edifice, and between it and the wheel-track of the street, was a grass-plot, much overgrown with burdock, pigweed, apple-peru, and such unsightly vegetation, which evidently found something congenial in the soil that had so early borne the black flower of civilized society, a prison2. But on one side of the portal, and rooted almost at the threshold, was a wild rose-bush, covered, in this month of June, with its delicate gems3, which might be imagined to offer their fragrance and fragile beauty to the prisoner as he went in, and to the condemned criminal as he came forth to his doom, in token that the deep heart of Nature could pity and be kind to him.

This rose-bush, by a strange chance, has been kept alive in history; but whether it had merely survived out of the stern old wilderness, … or whether, as there is fair authority for believing, it had sprung up under the footsteps of the sainted Ann Hutchinson4, as she entered the prison-door, we shall not take upon us to determine5. Finding it so directly on the threshold of our narrative, which is now about to issue from that inauspicious portal, we could hardly do otherwise than pluck one of its flowers, and present it to the reader. It may serve, let us hope, to symbolize some sweet moral blossom6, that may be found along the track, or relieve the darkening close of a tale of human frailty and sorrow.

The Scarlet Letter, Chapter 1, “The Prison-Door” · public-domain text · one clause shortened

1

Old before its time

The “New World” is barely a generation old, and already its most antique object is a prison door. The next sentence states the principle: whatever pertains to crime “seemed never to have known a youthful era”. Utopian settlement, Hawthorne implies, ages instantly at the point where it must punish—the chapter has already noted that the founders’ first allotments were a cemetery and a prison.

2

The black flower

The prison is “the black flower of civilized society”—an oxymoron that grows. Flowers bloom from congenial soil; so, the sentence says, do prisons, and the weeds around this one (burdock, pigweed, apple-peru—named like a botanist’s list) find the soil “congenial” too. Civilisation does not merely build its prison; it cultivates it. The metaphor sets up the rose as the counter-flower before the rose appears.

3

The counter-argument, rooted at the door

Against iron, oak and weeds: one wild rose-bush, in June bloom, “rooted almost at the threshold”. Its offer is carefully hedged—the flowers “might be imagined” to console the prisoner, “in token” that Nature’s deep heart can pity what society condemns. Wild, not planted: mercy in this book grows outside the settlement’s design. The whole novel’s geography—iron town against forest, law against nature—stands in this doorway.

4

“The sainted Ann Hutchinson”

The rose may have sprung up, the narrator offers, under the footsteps of Ann Hutchinson—the real dissenter tried and banished by Massachusetts Bay in 1638 for claiming direct spiritual authority against the ministers. “Sainted” is quietly audacious: Boston called her a heretic. Placing her legend at the prison door links Hester, before we meet her, to a lineage of women the settlement silenced—and hints that the colony’s roses grow where its women were condemned.

5

The refusal to decide

Offered two explanations—wilderness survival or miraculous legend—the narrator declines to choose: “we shall not take upon us to determine.” This is Hawthorne’s signature move, and the novel will repeat it at every crux: the meteor that may or may not form an A in the sky, the mark that may or may not be on the minister’s breast. Meaning is always plural, witnesses always disagree, and the reader is enrolled as the final court—beginning here, at the door.

6

A moral, wrapped in maybes

The narrator plucks a flower for the reader and hopes it “may serve… to symbolize some sweet moral blossom”. Every verb wobbles on purpose: may serve, let us hope, may be found. The gesture parodies the moral-tale convention it performs—a story that hands you its lesson at the door—while honestly naming what follows: “a tale of human frailty and sorrow”, whose close will darken and want relieving.

What the wording changes

A novel that teaches you how to read it in one page.

Chapter 1 contains no characters and no events—only a door, weeds, a rose, and a narrator handling them. That is its function: it is a training exercise. The reader learns the book’s method before meeting its people—objects will carry meanings; meanings will come in competing versions; the narrator will collect the versions and refuse to referee. By the time Hester emerges in the next chapter with the letter “fantastically embroidered” on her breast, we have been taught what to do with a symbol: hold its readings open.

The letter itself receives exactly the rose-bush treatment for the rest of the novel. The A is sentenced to mean adultery; Hester’s needle makes it art; the townspeople, years on, say it means “Able”; readers have added meanings ever since, which is the design. Objects in Hawthorne accumulate interpretations the way the prison accumulates rust—and the narrator’s early “we shall not take upon us to determine” is the rule that lets the accumulation happen. A lesser novelist would have decided; the deciding would have ended the book’s life.

The doorway also frames the book’s deepest opposition—the punishing settlement and the pitying wild—without letting either win. The rose is genuinely kind and genuinely powerless: it consoles no one in the plot, and the “sweet moral blossom” the narrator hopes for is never plainly delivered; the final chapter offers instead a heraldic motto on a shared gravestone, sombre and ambiguous to the last. Nature pities, in this book, but only society sentences—and Hawthorne, descendant of Salem judges (a fact the Custom-House introduction confesses with shame), keeps both truths flowering at the same threshold.

Take one question away

The narrator hands the reader a rose and refuses to say what it means. Is that generosity, evasion—or the novel’s whole moral method?

Before reading

Draw the doorway.

Sketch or list the scene’s objects and their positions: door, iron, weeds, wheel-track, grass-plot, rose-bush at the threshold. Note which belong to society and which to nature—and which one item sits exactly on the boundary.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Practise the Hawthorne hedge.

  1. Collect the passage’s hedging phrases: might be imagined, by a strange chance, fair authority for believing, we shall not take upon us to determine, may serve, let us hope.
  2. Rewrite two sentences with the hedges removed, as flat statements.
  3. Compare versions: what does certainty cost the passage?
  4. Find the same technique later in the novel—the midnight meteor is the classic case—and bring one example back.
Essay bridge

From the rose to the letter.

For an essay on symbolism, trace the method taught here through the letter’s career: the sentence that fixes its meaning, the embroidery that unfixes it, Pearl’s questions about it, the townspeople’s revision to “Able”, and the gravestone’s heraldry. The rose-bush chapter is the key; the novel is the lock it was cut for.
