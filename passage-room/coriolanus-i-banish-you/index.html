Passage Room/Coriolanus/I banish you

Close reading 70 · Coriolanus

“I banish you!”

Sentenced to exile by the people of Rome, the city’s greatest soldier turns the sentence round. Fourteen lines of contempt, and one exit line that has never stopped sounding both magnificent and doomed.

William ShakespeareAct 3, Scene 3

Probably written around 1608. The tribunes Sicinius and Brutus have led the plebeians to banish Coriolanus as “enemy to the people”. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: hear it as a man who has just been told to leave, deciding that he is the one doing the leaving. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

CoriolanusYou common cry of curs1, whose breath I hate

As the dead carcasses of unburied men

5And here remain with your uncertainty;

Fan you into despair! Have the power still
To banish your defenders4, till at length
10Your ignorance—which finds not till it feels,
Making but reservation of yourselves,
Still your own foes—deliver you,
As most abated captives to some nation
That won you without blows! Despising
15For you the city, thus I turn my back.5
There is a world elsewhere.6

Coriolanus, Act 3, Scene 3 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered from the start of the speech

1

A pack, not a people

“Cry” is a hunting term for a pack of hounds giving tongue. The citizens who have just chanted “It shall be so” in unison are, to Coriolanus, dogs baying together—and “common” is both plebeian and ordinary. The insult denies them individuality and humanity in three words. It is also, the play has shown, roughly how he has always spoken to them; the banishment is the bill for it.

2

Breath and rot

Their breath stinks like marsh gas; their love is as welcome as unburied corpses corrupting the air. The imagery is of contagion and putrefaction—the people as a disease of the atmosphere. Coriolanus’s contempt is always bodily; earlier he called them “scabs” and their votes “voices” he must beg with his wounds on show. His disgust is physical because his pride is: he cannot bear to be touched by them.

3

The reversal

“I banish you!” is one of the great reversals in Shakespeare—the sentenced man passing sentence on the court. It is also self-deception on a heroic scale. He cannot banish Rome; he can only leave it. But the line lets him keep his self-image intact: he is not expelled, he withdraws. Everything in the tragedy follows from a man who cannot accept being acted upon.

4

Banishing your defenders

The curse is political and, as it turns out, accurate: keep banishing the men who defend you, he says, until your enemies conquer you “without blows”. He is describing what he will shortly do himself—join the Volscians and march on Rome. The prophecy of the city’s fall is a plan disguised as a warning, and the play will make him carry it out and then be unable to finish it.

5

Turning the back

“Despising / For you the city, thus I turn my back”—he makes his exit a gesture of contempt, and the grammar is telling: he despises the city for them, on their account; Rome is spoiled by containing them. The soldier who was Rome’s sword now treats Rome as a thing to be walked away from. Volumnia, his mother, has made him the city’s champion; he has no self that is not Roman, and he is about to find out.

6

A world elsewhere

The exit line is proud, spare and untrue. There is no world elsewhere for Coriolanus: he has no life outside war for Rome, and when he goes to the Volscians he can only fight against Rome, which is still a way of belonging to it. The line sounds like freedom and is the beginning of his destruction. Actors have delivered it as a trumpet and as a whisper; the play supports both, because the man believes it and is wrong.

What the wording changes

The exit line is the tragedy in five words.

Coriolanus is Shakespeare’s most political tragedy, and this scene is its hinge: a war hero who cannot perform the humility the republic requires is expelled by the citizens he saved. The play does not take sides simply. The tribunes are manipulators; the people are fickle; Coriolanus is a magnificent soldier and an impossible citizen, incapable of the small hypocrisies—showing his wounds, asking for votes—on which civic life depends. His banishment speech is the moment his integrity and his arrogance are revealed to be the same quality. He will not bend, so he breaks, and makes the breaking a gesture of scorn.

“I banish you” and “There is a world elsewhere” belong together as a study in a mind that cannot receive. Coriolanus has spent the play turning away praise, turning away spoils, turning away from being looked at; now he will not accept the sentence. The reversal is thrilling on stage and the play lets it be. But the audience knows what he does not: that a man formed entirely by his mother and his city has nowhere else to be a self. When he arrives among the Volscians he can offer them only his hatred of Rome, and when Volumnia kneels to him before the gates, he cannot hold the hatred either. The “world elsewhere” turns out to be the same world with the sides reversed.

The speech’s images of stink and rot are worth reading against the play’s opening, where Menenius tells the hungry citizens the fable of the belly—the body politic in which every member has its use. Coriolanus never accepts the fable. To him the people are an infection in the body, and his language cannot imagine a Rome that includes them. Shakespeare, writing in the year of the Midland grain riots, gave the republic’s argument to a hero who cannot hear it, and made the hero’s deafness both his glory and his end.

Take one question away

“I banish you” is impossible and magnificent. Is Coriolanus’s refusal to be judged the noblest thing in the play, or the flaw that makes it a tragedy?

Before reading

Collect the bodily images.

List every image of smell, rot, disease or corpses. Coriolanus never argues with the people; he recoils from them. Ask what a politics based on disgust can and cannot do.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Deliver the exit.

  1. Say “There is a world elsewhere” as triumph, then as bravado, then as a man realising he has nowhere to go.
  2. Decide which reading the previous fifteen lines support.
  3. Ask what the citizens should do as he leaves: cheer, fall silent, or look at each other.
  4. Predict, from this speech alone, where he will go.
Essay bridge

The belly and the curs.

For an essay on the play’s politics, set this speech against Menenius’s fable of the belly in Act 1 and Volumnia’s plea before Rome in Act 5. Three accounts of what a citizen owes the city; ask which the play believes, and whether Coriolanus ever understands any of them.
