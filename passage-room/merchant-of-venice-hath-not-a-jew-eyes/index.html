Close reading 35 · The Merchant of Venice

“Hath not a Jew eyes?”

Baited by two Venetians about the bond, Shylock answers with the play’s most famous speech. It is remembered as a plea for tolerance. Read whole, it is something harder: an argument that ends in revenge, and indicts the men who taught it.

William ShakespeareAct 3, Scene 1

Probably written 1596–98. Shylock speaks in prose to Salarino and Solanio, just after learning that his daughter Jessica has fled with a Christian. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

Content note

The scene sits inside a play built on the antisemitic conventions of its age, and the two Venetians on stage are mocking Shylock as he speaks. The speech’s power—and the centuries of argument about it—come from reading it inside that hostility, not apart from it.

How to read this page: keep the listeners in view—the speech is spoken upward, at men who despise the speaker. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Merchant of Venice3.1

He hath disgrac’d me and hind’red me half a million, laugh’d at my losses, mock’d at my gains, scorned my nation, thwarted my bargains, cooled my friends, heated mine enemies.1 And what’s his reason? I am a Jew. Hath not a Jew eyes? Hath not a Jew hands, organs, dimensions, senses, affections, passions?2 Fed with the same food, hurt with the same weapons, subject to the same diseases, healed by the same means, warmed and cooled by the same winter and summer as a Christian is? If you prick us, do we not bleed?3 If you tickle us, do we not laugh? If you poison us, do we not die? And if you wrong us, shall we not revenge?4 If we are like you in the rest, we will resemble you in that.5 If a Jew wrong a Christian, what is his humility? Revenge. If a Christian wrong a Jew, what should his sufferance be by Christian example? Why, revenge! The villainy you teach me I will execute, and it shall go hard but I will better the instruction.6

The Merchant of Venice, Act 3, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

A ledger of injuries

The speech opens as an account-book: disgraced, hindered “half a million” (ducats of lost business), losses laughed at, bargains thwarted. Antonio’s offences are itemised in the paired, balanced clauses of a man used to reckoning—cooled my friends, heated mine enemies. Then the audit arrives at its finding, four flat words long: “I am a Jew.” The itemised injuries all trace to that single, unchosen fact.

2

The body as evidence

The famous questions locate common humanity in flesh, not doctrine: eyes, hands, organs, dimensions, senses, affections, passions. Nothing in the list is Christian or Jewish; everything is anatomical and felt. In a Venice that defines Shylock entirely by religion, he argues from the one text no one can dispute—the shared body, fed, hurt, healed and weathered alike. The rhetoric is anaphora, question stacked on question, each unanswerable.

3

Blood, before the trial

“If you prick us, do we not bleed?” is the speech’s most quoted line, and the play’s cruellest irony in waiting. Blood and flesh are precisely the terms of Shylock’s bond—and in the trial scene, Portia will defeat him with them: the bond grants a pound of flesh but “no jot of blood”. The image that here proves common humanity will there be turned into the technicality that destroys him.

4

The turn

One more question completes the series—and changes everything: “And if you wrong us, shall we not revenge?” The list of shared capacities has been building not to a plea for kindness but to a claim of shared entitlement to retaliation. Quotation usually stops before this line. The speech does not: revenge is its destination, prepared by every question before it.

5

Resemblance cuts both ways

“If we are like you in the rest, we will resemble you in that.” The logic is faultless and terrible: if Jews and Christians are alike in body and feeling, they are alike in vindictiveness too. Shylock claims equality only in the capacity for harm—because that is the only equality Venice has ever shown him. The sentence indicts both parties at once, which is why it has never stopped being uncomfortable.

6

The pupil surpasses the school

“The villainy you teach me I will execute”—revenge as education, with Christians for tutors—“and it shall go hard but I will better the instruction”: I will outdo my teachers. The speech ends by handing responsibility for what Shylock becomes to the society that formed him, while claiming, unmistakably, that he intends to become it. Explanation and menace occupy the same sentence, and the play never lets us separate them.

What the wording changes

A plea and a threat that cannot be pulled apart.

The context is deliberately squalid. Shylock is not addressing a court but two minor gentlemen who have stopped him in the street to jeer—about his losses, and about Jessica, the daughter who has just robbed him and eloped with a Christian. The great speech rises out of baiting, and returns to baiting: moments later Tubal arrives, and Shylock’s grief for his daughter, his ducats and his dead wife’s ring is played against news of Antonio’s ruined ships. Shakespeare embeds the play’s deepest speech in its shabbiest scene, daring the audience to notice what the mockers cannot.

Whether the play itself rises above the prejudices it stages has been argued for four centuries, and the honest answer is that the text supports the argument. Shylock is built from the stock materials of Elizabethan stage Jewry—the usurer, the knife, the obsession with the bond—and the comedy ends with his forced conversion, presented as mercy. Yet this speech exists, and nothing in Marlowe’s Barabas or the play’s other sources approaches it: a systematic demolition of the idea that the despised are different in kind. Performance history tracks the debate—Shylock has been clown, devil, and, increasingly since the nineteenth century, tragic centre, with this speech as the pivot every interpretation must turn on.

The one reading the text refuses is the comfortable one, in which the speech is simply a hymn to tolerance. Its questions about eyes and hands are unanswerable, and they end in “shall we not revenge?”—a conclusion the trial scene executes almost to the letter, until Portia’s “quality of mercy” speech offers the standard Venice itself then fails to meet, stripping Shylock of fortune and faith together. The play leaves the accusation standing: the villainy was taught, the instruction was bettered, and no one on stage is innocent of the curriculum.

Take one question away

The speech proves the shared humanity of persecutor and persecuted—and claims revenge as part of the inheritance. Can its argument be accepted in half?

Before reading

Note where quotation stops.

Most anthologies end this speech at “do we not die?”. Read the full text first, then mark the line where the familiar version stops. Ask what the trimmed version turns the speech into, and who benefits from the trim.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Follow the questions’ logic.

  1. List the capacities Shylock claims are shared: seeing, feeling, bleeding, laughing, dying.
  2. Identify the first question in the sequence that a listener might hesitate to answer “yes” to—and why.
  3. Trace how “revenge” is made to seem the same kind of item as “bleed” and “laugh”.
  4. Discuss: is that a flaw in the argument, or its sharpest point?
Essay bridge

Two speeches about mercy and blood.

For an essay on justice in the play, set this speech against Portia’s “The quality of mercy is not strain’d” in the trial scene—then against the sentence the court actually imposes. The play offers the two great speeches as rivals; its ending tests which one Venice believes, and the result is the play’s hardest question.
