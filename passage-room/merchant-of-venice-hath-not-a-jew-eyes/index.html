Close reading 35 · The Merchant of Venice

“Hath not a Jew eyes?”

Baited by two Venetians about the bond, Shylock answers with the play’s most famous speech. It is remembered as a plea for tolerance. The whole speech is harder than that: an argument that ends in revenge, and blames the men who taught it.

William ShakespeareAct 3, Scene 1

Probably written 1596–98. Shylock speaks in prose to Salarino and Solanio, just after learning that his daughter Jessica has fled with a Christian. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

Content note

The scene sits inside a play shaped by the antisemitic conventions of its age, and the two Venetians on stage are mocking Shylock as he speaks. The speech has to be read inside that hostility; its power, and the centuries of argument about it, come from there.

How to read this page: keep the listeners in view: Shylock is speaking to men who despise him and who outrank him in Venice. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Merchant of Venice3.1

He hath disgrac’d me and hind’red me half a million, laugh’d at my losses, mock’d at my gains, scorned my nation, thwarted my bargains, cooled my friends, heated mine enemies.1 And what’s his reason? I am a Jew. Hath not a Jew eyes? Hath not a Jew hands, organs, dimensions, senses, affections, passions?2 Fed with the same food, hurt with the same weapons, subject to the same diseases, healed by the same means, warmed and cooled by the same winter and summer as a Christian is? If you prick us, do we not bleed?3 If you tickle us, do we not laugh? If you poison us, do we not die? And if you wrong us, shall we not revenge?4 If we are like you in the rest, we will resemble you in that.5 If a Jew wrong a Christian, what is his humility? Revenge. If a Christian wrong a Jew, what should his sufferance be by Christian example? Why, revenge! The villainy you teach me I will execute, and it shall go hard but I will better the instruction.6

The Merchant of Venice, Act 3, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

A ledger of injuries

The speech opens as an account-book: disgraced, hindered “half a million” (ducats of lost business), losses laughed at, bargains thwarted. Antonio’s offences are itemised in the paired, balanced clauses of a man used to reckoning: cooled my friends, heated mine enemies. Then the audit arrives at its finding, four flat words long: “I am a Jew.” The itemised injuries all trace to that single, unchosen fact.

2

The body as evidence

The famous questions find common humanity in the flesh: eyes, hands, organs, dimensions, senses, affections, passions. Nothing in the list is Christian or Jewish; everything is anatomical and felt. In a Venice that defines Shylock entirely by religion, he argues from the one thing no one can dispute: the shared body, fed, hurt, healed and weathered alike. The rhetoric is anaphora, question stacked on question, each unanswerable.

3

Blood, before the trial

“If you prick us, do we not bleed?” is the speech’s most quoted line, and it waits for the play’s cruellest irony. Blood and flesh are the terms of Shylock’s bond, and in the trial scene Portia defeats him with them: the bond grants a pound of flesh but “no jot of blood”. The image that here proves common humanity becomes, there, the technicality that destroys him.

4

The turn

One more question completes the series and turns it: “And if you wrong us, shall we not revenge?” The list of shared capacities has been building to a claim of an equal right to retaliate. Quotation usually stops before this line, but the speech keeps going, and every question before it has been leading here.

5

Resemblance cuts both ways

“If we are like you in the rest, we will resemble you in that.” The logic is faultless and terrible: if Jews and Christians are alike in body and feeling, they are alike in vindictiveness too. Shylock ends by claiming equality in the capacity for harm, because that is the equality Venice has shown him. The sentence accuses both sides, which is why it has never stopped being uncomfortable.

6

Bettering the instruction

“The villainy you teach me I will execute, and it shall go hard but I will better the instruction”: revenge as education, with Christians for tutors, and a pupil who means to outdo them. The speech ends by handing responsibility for what Shylock becomes to the society that formed him, while making it unmistakable that he intends to become it. Explanation and menace share the same sentence, and the play never lets us separate them.

What the wording changes

Shylock’s speech

The context is deliberately squalid. Shylock is speaking to two minor gentlemen who have stopped him in the street to jeer about his losses, and about Jessica, the daughter who has just robbed him and eloped with a Christian. The great speech comes out of baiting, and what follows is hardly kinder: moments later Tubal arrives, and Shylock’s grief for his daughter, his ducats and his dead wife’s ring is played against news of Antonio’s ruined ships. Shakespeare puts the play’s deepest speech in its shabbiest scene, and leaves the audience to notice what the mockers cannot.

Whether the play rises above the prejudices it stages is still argued, and the text gives both sides evidence. Shylock is built from the stock materials of Elizabethan stage Jewry (the usurer, the knife, the obsession with the bond), and the comedy ends with his forced conversion, presented as mercy. Yet this speech exists, and nothing in Marlowe’s Barabas, or in the play’s sources, comes near it: a point-by-point demolition of the idea that the despised are different in kind. Performance history follows the debate. Shylock has been played as clown and devil and, increasingly since the nineteenth century, as the tragic centre, and every interpretation has to decide what to do with this speech.

The one reading the text will not support is the comfortable one, in which the speech is a hymn to tolerance and nothing more. Its questions about eyes and hands are unanswerable, and they end in “shall we not revenge?”, a conclusion the trial scene almost carries out to the letter, until Portia’s “quality of mercy” speech sets a standard that Venice itself then fails to meet, stripping Shylock of fortune and faith together. The play leaves the accusation standing: the villainy was taught, the instruction was bettered, and no one on stage comes out of it clean.

Take one question away

The speech proves the shared humanity of persecutor and persecuted, and claims revenge as part of the inheritance. Can a reader accept half of the argument and not the rest?

Before reading

Note where quotation stops.

Most anthologies end this speech at “do we not die?”. Read the full text first, then mark the line where the familiar version stops. Ask what the speech becomes when it is trimmed, and who benefits from the trim.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Follow the questions’ logic.

  1. List the capacities Shylock claims are shared: seeing, feeling, bleeding, laughing, dying.
  2. Identify the first question in the sequence that a listener might hesitate to answer “yes” to, and say why.
  3. Trace how “revenge” is made to seem the same kind of item as “bleed” and “laugh”.
  4. Discuss: is that a flaw in the argument, or its sharpest point?
Essay bridge

Two speeches about mercy and blood.

For an essay on justice in the play, set this speech against Portia’s “The quality of mercy is not strain’d” in the trial scene, and then against the sentence the court actually imposes. The two speeches are rivals, and the sentence shows how far Venice lives by either.
