Passage Room/Venus and Adonis/The opening

Close reading 89 · Venus and Adonis

“Hunting he lov’d, but love he laugh’d to scorn”

The first work Shakespeare published under his own name opens at dawn with a boy riding out to hunt and a goddess riding after him. Three stanzas set up a comedy of pursuit that will end, twelve hundred lines later, with a boar.

William ShakespeareLines 1–18

Published in 1593, while the theatres were closed by plague, and dedicated to the Earl of Southampton as “the first heir of my invention”. It went through at least ten editions in Shakespeare’s lifetime. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: hear the six-line stanza close on its couplet each time—that snap is the poem’s wit. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Venus and Adonis, lines 1–18 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered

1

The sun leaves the morning

The poem opens with a parting: the sun, red-faced (“purple” meant crimson), takes leave of a “weeping” dawn—the dew as tears. It is the first image of a lover leaving, and the whole poem will be about a lover who wants to leave and a goddess who will not let him. The mythological morning is already a small domestic scene.

2

The chiasmus that starts the plot

“Hunting he lov’d, but love he laugh’d to scorn”: love and hunting cross in the line, and the crossing is the poem. Adonis wants the chase; Venus wants Adonis; and at the end the hunt takes him. Shakespeare gives the plot in one balanced line before the goddess has opened her mouth.

3

The goddess as suitor

Venus is “sick-thoughted”—love-sick—and comes “amain”, at full speed, “like a bold-fac’d suitor”. The reversal is the poem’s comic engine: the goddess of love plays the pursuing male lover, and the beautiful boy plays the reluctant mistress. Elizabethan readers, raised on sonnets in which men beg and women refuse, got the joke immediately.

4

More lovely than a man

Venus’s praise makes Adonis a flower, a stain (an eclipse) to all nymphs, “more lovely than a man”—lovelier, that is, than a man should be. The compliments are the stock of the female blazon turned on a boy, and their extravagance is part of the comedy: Venus talks like a sonneteer, and Adonis is embarrassed.

5

Nature at strife with herself

The couplet claims that Nature, making Adonis, competed with herself and declared that “the world hath ending with thy life”—he is the peak of creation, after which nothing matters. It is hyperbole in the courtly manner, and it is also prophecy: Adonis will die, and the poem’s last stanzas will mourn him as the end of something. Venus’s flattery keeps telling the truth by accident.

6

Where never serpent hisses

Her invitation—dismount, sit, and “I’ll smother thee with kisses”—offers a place “where never serpent hisses”: an Eden without the snake. The promise is a garden of pure pleasure, and “smother” gives the game away. Venus’s love is overwhelming, suffocating, and the boy will spend the poem trying to get his breath. The couplet’s rhyme (hisses/kisses) makes the threat sound like a treat.

What the wording changes

The poem that made a playwright a poet.

In 1593 the London theatres were shut by plague, and William Shakespeare, a successful but unprinted dramatist, published a narrative poem under his own name with a dedication to a young earl. Venus and Adonis was the bestseller of his lifetime—reprinted more often than any of the plays, quoted by students, kept under pillows. Its opening stanzas show why. The verse is polished and quick, the six-line stanza snapping shut on a couplet each time; the situation is comic and slightly scandalous; and the mythology from Ovid is handled with a wit that treats gods as people with problems.

The reversal of roles is the poem’s idea. In the sonnet tradition the man pursues and the woman refuses; here the goddess of love pursues a boy who would rather hunt, and every convention of courtly wooing is put in a woman’s mouth and made faintly absurd. Adonis’s reluctance is the joke and, as the poem goes on, its seriousness: he is a boy, she is a goddess, and the poem is honest about the pressure of a desire that will not take no. When he escapes to the hunt and is killed by the boar, the comedy ends in grief, and the flower that springs from his blood is the poem’s last image: the boy she wanted, now something she can carry.

Shakespeare called it “the first heir of my invention”, and the phrase is careful: the plays were collaborative, performed, unowned; the poem was his. The opening stanzas are a young writer showing what he can do with a stanza, a myth and a reversal, and they announce the gifts the plays would spend—the balanced line, the comic inversion, the flattery that tells the truth by accident. The boy on his horse at dawn, laughing love to scorn, is the first character Shakespeare put his name to.

Take one question away

Venus woos like a sonneteer and Adonis refuses like a mistress. Is the reversal a joke about convention, or the poem’s way of showing what pursuit looks like from the other side?

Before reading

Hear the stanza.

Read one stanza aloud and mark the rhyme scheme: ababcc. The couplet closes each stanza like a door. Ask what the form does to Venus’s wooing—whether it makes her sound witty, or makes her sound like a poem.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Swap the roles back.

  1. Rewrite Venus’s first stanza with a man wooing a woman.
  2. Notice what becomes conventional and what becomes uncomfortable.
  3. Ask what the poem gains by giving the pursuer’s lines to a goddess.
  4. Find the word in the opening that already predicts the ending.
Essay bridge

Two narrative poems.

For an essay on Shakespeare the poet, set the comic pursuit of Venus and Adonis beside the tragic pursuit of The Rape of Lucrece, published a year later. One is a goddess wooing a boy, the other a prince assaulting a wife; ask what the two poems together say about desire, consent and the stanza forms Shakespeare chose for each.
