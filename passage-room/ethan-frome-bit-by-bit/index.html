Passage Room/Ethan Frome/Bit by bit

Close reading 65 · Ethan Frome

“each time it was a different story”

Wharton opens her bleakest novel with a confession about storytelling: the narrator got it in pieces, from people who disagreed, and what follows is his reconstruction. The first sentence tells you not to trust the rest—and then makes you.

Edith WhartonThe opening

Published in 1911. An unnamed engineer, wintering in a Massachusetts village, becomes curious about a crippled farmer. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: notice how much the narrator admits he does not know, and how confidently he then describes. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Ethan FromeThe opening

I had the story, bit by bit, from various people, and, as generally happens in such cases, each time it was a different story.1

If you know Starkfield, Massachusetts, you know the post-office. If you know the post-office you must have seen Ethan Frome drive up to it, drop the reins on his hollow-backed bay and drag himself across the brick pavement2 to the white colonnade; and you must have asked who he was.

It was there that, several years ago, I saw him for the first time; and the sight pulled me up sharp. Even then he was the most striking figure in Starkfield, though he was but the ruin of a man3. It was not so much his great height that marked him… it was the careless powerful look he had, in spite of a lameness checking each step like the jerk of a chain4. There was something bleak and unapproachable in his face, and he was so stiffened and grizzled that I took him for an old man and was surprised to hear that he was not more than fifty-two5.

Ethan Frome, the opening · public-domain text · one clause shortened

1

A warning label on the story

The first sentence is about the unreliability of the sentences to come. The narrator had the story “bit by bit”, from “various people”, and “each time it was a different story”. Wharton is building a frame: the tale of Ethan, Zeena and Mattie will be told by an outsider who assembled it from gossip and one night’s stay at the farm, and who admits in advance that his sources contradicted each other. The confession is also a licence—he is free to imagine.

2

“Drag himself”

Before we know anything about Ethan we watch him move: dropping the reins, dragging himself across the pavement. The verb does the work of a paragraph. Wharton introduces her hero by his gait, and the gait is the aftermath of the event the whole novel exists to explain. We are shown the wound and then made to wait for the story of it.

3

The ruin of a man

“The most striking figure in Starkfield, though he was but the ruin of a man”: the sentence holds greatness and wreckage together. Ruins are impressive because of what they were. The narrator’s interest—and ours—is archaeological: what building stood here? The novel is the excavation.

4

The chain

Ethan’s lameness checks “each step like the jerk of a chain”. It is the novel’s governing image, delivered in the opening paragraph: a man of “careless powerful” build, jerked back at every stride by something attached to him. The literal chain is the injury; the figurative one is Starkfield, Zeena, poverty, duty—everything that stopped him leaving. Wharton has told the story’s meaning before she has told the story.

5

Fifty-two

The narrator took him for an old man; he is fifty-two. The correction is small and devastating: whatever happened to Ethan happened early enough to age him decades, and long enough ago that he has been living in its aftermath for years. The number is precise where the story is vague. Wharton’s method is to give the reader hard facts—an age, a post-office, a lame walk—around a centre that must be guessed.

What the wording changes

The frame is the point.

Ethan Frome is built as a frame narrative, and this opening is the frame declaring itself. An educated outsider—an engineer on a winter job—sees a ruined man at a post-office, asks questions, gets contradictory answers, and finally, snowbound at the Frome farm for a night, constructs the story we then read. Wharton was explicit about the design: in her introduction to the novel she explained that the tragedy of inarticulate people in a harsh place needed a “sophisticated” narrator to give it shape, since the characters themselves could not. The first sentence hands us that narrator, and hands us his doubts.

The technique changes what kind of book this is. Because the central story is admitted to be reconstruction, everything in it—Ethan’s longing for Mattie, Zeena’s sourness, the sled ride—carries a faint question mark: this is how the narrator imagined it, from pieces. Yet Wharton then writes that story with such concreteness that the doubt vanishes in the reading and returns only at the end, when the frame closes and we are back at the post-office with an old man’s walk. The novel asks us to believe a story it has told us was pieced together, and the request is part of its bleakness. Ethan’s life is so small that its only full telling is a stranger’s guess.

New England is the other frame. Starkfield’s name says what it is, and the narrator will shortly learn that Ethan has spent “too many winters” there. Wharton, a New Yorker and Europhile, wrote this austere village tragedy partly as an answer to the softened, picturesque New England of earlier fiction; her Starkfield is a place that chains people. The opening’s post-office, brick pavement and white colonnade are the settled, respectable surface; the drag of Ethan’s step across them is the first sign of what the surface conceals.

Take one question away

The narrator says every version he heard was different. Does that make the story he tells less true—or the only kind of truth a life like Ethan’s can have?

Before reading

List what is certain.

Note every hard fact in the passage: a post-office, a bay horse, a lame walk, a height, an age. Then note every uncertainty: “various people”, “different story”, “I took him for”. The novel lives in the gap between the two lists.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Rebuild from fragments.

  1. Give each group one “bit” of Ethan’s story (from later in the novel) as a villager might tell it.
  2. Assemble the versions and note where they conflict.
  3. Write the narrator’s reconciling sentence.
  4. Discuss what a frame narrator adds, and what he cannot know.
Essay bridge

Frames and chains.

For an essay on the novel’s structure, connect the opening frame to its return at the end, when the narrator enters the farmhouse and sees who is living in it. Ask how the chain image of the first page is completed by the last, and whether the frame’s distance makes the tragedy more bearable or more terrible.
