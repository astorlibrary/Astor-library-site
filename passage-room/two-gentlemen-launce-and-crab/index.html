Close reading 88 · The Two Gentlemen of Verona

“I think Crab my dog be the sourest-natured dog that lives”

A servant leaving home re-enacts his family’s tearful farewell with two shoes, a staff and a hat, and cannot decide which prop is the dog. It is Shakespeare’s first great clown speech, and the dog on stage—a real one—steals it every time.

William ShakespeareAct 2, Scene 3

Probably among the earliest plays, written around 1590–91. Launce, servant to Proteus, is leaving Verona for Milan with his dog. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text, which spells the name Lance.

How to read this page: picture the props—and the dog, sitting there, doing nothing. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Two Gentlemen of Verona2.3

LaunceNay, ’twill be this hour ere I have done weeping; all the kind of the Launces have this very fault. I have received my proportion, like the prodigious son1, and am going with Sir Proteus to the Imperial’s court. I think Crab my dog be the sourest-natured dog that lives: my mother weeping, my father wailing, my sister crying, our maid howling, our cat wringing her hands, and all our house in a great perplexity, yet did not this cruel-hearted cur shed one tear.2 He is a stone, a very pebblestone, and has no more pity in him than a dog. … Nay, I’ll show you the manner of it. This shoe is my father. No, this left shoe is my father; no, no, this left shoe is my mother. Nay, that cannot be so neither. Yes, it is so, it is so; it hath the worser sole.3 This shoe with the hole in it is my mother, and this my father. A vengeance on ’t, there ’tis. Now, sir, this staff is my sister, for, look you, she is as white as a lily and as small as a wand. This hat is Nan, our maid. I am the dog. No, the dog is himself, and I am the dog. O, the dog is me, and I am myself.4 Ay, so, so. Now come I to my father: “Father, your blessing.” Now should not the shoe speak a word for weeping. … Now the dog all this while sheds not a tear nor speaks a word; but see how I lay the dust with my tears.5

The Two Gentlemen of Verona, Act 2, Scene 3 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · two stretches omitted at the ellipses

1

The prodigious son

Launce means the prodigal son, who received his portion and left home; he says “prodigious” and “proportion”. The malapropism is the clown’s signature—Dogberry and Mistress Quickly will inherit it—and it does real work: a servant framing his departure through scripture, and getting the words slightly wrong, is both funny and touching. He is a prodigal who has done nothing wrong.

2

The whole house weeps but the dog

The catalogue of grief is a crescendo—mother weeping, father wailing, sister crying, maid howling—until it reaches “our cat wringing her hands”, at which point the picture tips into absurdity and the audience laughs. Then the anticlimax: the dog did not shed a tear. The joke is that Launce is genuinely hurt by the dog’s composure, and the dog, on stage, is genuinely composed.

3

Which shoe is which

Launce re-stages the farewell with props, and cannot cast it. The left shoe is his father; no, his mother; no—and the deciding evidence is that it “hath the worser sole”, a pun on soul that is either an insult to his mother or a theological compliment. The confusion is the comedy, and it is also a small parody of the play’s own business: a drama about who loves whom, staged by a man who cannot tell his parents apart.

4

I am the dog

The most famous tangle in the speech: “I am the dog. No, the dog is himself, and I am the dog. O, the dog is me, and I am myself.” Launce tries to cast himself and Crab and keeps swapping. The lines are pure clowning and they touch something the play is about—the two gentlemen of the title also keep swapping identities and loyalties. Launce’s confusion about who is the dog is the comic version of Proteus’s confusion about who is his friend.

5

Laying the dust with tears

The speech ends where it began: the dog “sheds not a tear nor speaks a word”, and Launce weeps enough to settle the dust. The image is a labourer’s—water sprinkled on a dry road—and it makes his grief useful and ridiculous at once. Crab, meanwhile, has done nothing for forty lines, and the nothing is the performance. Every actor who has played Launce knows the dog gets the laugh.

What the wording changes

The first clown speech, and the dog that steals it.

The Two Gentlemen of Verona is probably Shakespeare’s first comedy, and it shows: the plot creaks, the ending is notoriously abrupt, and the two gentlemen are hard to like. But Launce and Crab are fully achieved, and they are the play’s gift to the future. Here is the pattern Shakespeare would use for thirty years—a clown whose confusion is wiser than the wits’ cleverness, whose malapropisms say more than the courtiers’ verse, and whose scenes comment sideways on the main plot without ever seeming to. Launce’s farewell is the prototype of Bottom, Dogberry and the Fool.

The speech is built for a live dog, and the dog is the joke’s secret. Everything Launce says about Crab’s hard-heartedness is confirmed by an animal that sits on stage doing nothing, or does something unscripted—and either way the audience laughs at the gap between the man’s grief and the dog’s indifference. Shakespeare wrote the first sustained comic double-act in English drama, and made one of the partners an animal who cannot act. The indifference is the point: Launce loves a creature that does not love him back, which is also, as it happens, the situation of every lover in the play.

That is the speech’s quiet function. Proteus will betray his friend Valentine and his lover Julia within two acts; the gentlemen’s loyalties swap as freely as Launce’s shoes. The servant who cannot decide whether he is the dog is the comic shadow of masters who cannot decide whom they love. Later in the play Launce will take a beating for Crab’s misbehaviour rather than see him whipped—the one act of unconditional loyalty in the whole comedy, performed by a clown for a dog. The play’s gentlemen never manage anything like it.

Take one question away

Launce loves a dog that cannot love him back, and the play’s gentlemen betray each other in verse. Which is the play’s real picture of devotion?

Before reading

Cast the props.

List the props: two shoes, a staff, a hat, the dog. Assign each a family member as Launce does, then note every time he changes his mind. The re-casting is the comedy; ask what it says about a man trying to stage his own grief.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Play it with a dog.

  1. Read the speech with one reader as Launce and another as Crab, who must do nothing.
  2. Note where the audience laughs, and whether it is at Launce or at Crab.
  3. Ask what changes if Crab does something—yawns, wanders off.
  4. Decide who the scene is really about.
Essay bridge

The clown and the gentlemen.

For an essay on the play’s structure, set Launce’s two Crab scenes beside Proteus’s betrayals and the notorious ending, in which Valentine offers Silvia to the man who tried to rape her. Ask whether the clown’s loyalty to a dog is the play’s comic relief or its moral centre.
