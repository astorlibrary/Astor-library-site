Passage Room/Dracula/The children of the night

Close reading 28 · Dracula

“What music they make!”

A young English solicitor sits up late with his courteous foreign client. Wolves begin to howl in the valley below, and his host says something no amount of politeness can make ordinary.

Bram StokerChapter 2 · Jonathan Harker’s journal

Published in 1897. The novel is assembled entirely from documents (journals, letters, phonograph diaries, news cuttings); this entry is written in shorthand at Castle Dracula.

How to read this page: read two records side by side: what the Count says, and what Harker’s eyes notice while he says it. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

DraculaHarker’s journal

Jonathan Harker’s Journal (kept in shorthand) · 5 May

We were both silent for a while; and as I looked towards the window I saw the first dim streak of the coming dawn1. There seemed a strange stillness over everything; but as I listened I heard as if from down below in the valley the howling of many wolves. The Count’s eyes gleamed2, and he said:—

“Listen to them—the children of the night3. What music they make!4” Seeing, I suppose, some expression in my face strange to him, he added:—

“Ah, sir, you dwellers in the city cannot enter into the feelings of the hunter5.” Then he rose and said:—

“But you must be tired. Your bedroom is all ready, and to-morrow you shall sleep as late as you will. I have to be away till the afternoon; so sleep well and dream well!6” With a courteous bow, he opened for me himself the door to the octagonal room, and I entered my bedroom….

I am all in a sea of wonders. I doubt; I fear; I think strange things7, which I dare not confess to my own soul. God keep me, if only for the sake of those dear to me!

Dracula, Chapter 2 · public-domain text

1

Dawn changes the host

The conversation has run all night: the Count keeps late hours, as Harker has begun to notice without understanding. The “first dim streak” of light is why this scene is about to end: within a paragraph Dracula will excuse himself. Stoker plants the vampire’s rules in plain sight as domestic habits, letting the reader assemble what the narrator cannot.

2

The body tells the truth

Harker records the gleam in the Count’s eyes before recording his words. Throughout these chapters, Dracula’s speech is formal and hospitable while his body gives the truth away: the protuberant teeth shown by a smile, the hairs in the centre of the palms, the gleam at the sound of predators. The journal notices everything and concludes nothing; the gap between observation and inference is where the horror lives.

3

A family of predators

“Children of the night” is a kinship claim: the wolves are his household, almost his offspring. The phrase ennobles the wolves, night’s children and not vermin, and makes the speaker night’s father. Later chapters will make the kinship literal, when the Count commands wolves as instruments. Harker hears poetry; the reader, on a second pass, hears an introduction to the family.

4

Killing heard as concert

The howling of hunting wolves is “music” to him. It is the Count’s taste that chills: he enjoys violence the way a connoisseur enjoys music. One sentence of appreciation defines him better than pages of menace could: he has a civilisation of his own, ancient, with its own taste.

5

Hunter against city-dweller

Noticing Harker’s face, the Count offers an explanation that is also a classification: you city people cannot enter the hunter’s feelings. The whole novel is folded into that division: the modern, professional London of trains, typewriters and land conveyances against something feudal that hunts. The courteous apology carries a threat neither speaker names: Harker is a dweller in the city, and he is in the hunter’s house.

6

Hospitality as control

Every element is impeccable: the ready bedroom, the invitation to sleep late, the bow, the door opened “for me himself”. And every element is a bar of the cage: the Count sets Harker’s hours of sleeping and waking, announces his own absence, controls every door. ‘Sleep well and dream well!’ from this host is an instruction. Harker will shortly discover that the castle’s doors lock from the outside.

7

Writing against fear

Alone, the journal’s voice changes: short clauses, and semicolons like held breaths (“I doubt; I fear; I think strange things”). He dare not confess his thoughts even “to my own soul”, yet he confesses them to shorthand. The novel’s method is here in miniature: writing things down is how Stoker’s moderns keep their reason, and eventually how they fight: the documents we are reading become, once assembled, the weapon that defeats the Count.

What the wording changes

Dramatic irony

Nothing supernatural happens in this passage. A host praises local wildlife, apologises for his enthusiasm, and shows his guest consideration. The horror is entirely a matter of position: Stoker has arranged for the reader to know more than Harker can allow himself to know, and every courteous sentence lands twice, once as manners and once as confession. Dramatic irony is usually the theatre’s tool; Dracula builds it from paperwork.

The journal form matters more than it first appears. Harker is a solicitor’s clerk newly qualified, taking professional notes in shorthand, a modern skill illegible to his ancient host. His writing is self-discipline (‘The habit of entering accurately must help to soothe me’, a later entry admits), a private refuge, and in the end evidence. Stoker’s novel trusts documents the way its vampire trusts night: the assembled journals, letters and phonograph cylinders are how scattered witnesses finally see the same monster.

The passage also sets the novel’s deepest opposition in place: not good against evil so much as the modern against the archaic. The Count speaks of hunters and night-children from inside a crumbling castle; his guest returns to his room and writes shorthand, worries about those dear to him, and prays. When the conflict moves to England, it will be fought with train timetables, typewritten copies and blood transfusions, modernity’s instruments against the hunter’s feelings. The wolves’ music is the old world announcing itself to the new.

Take one question away

The Count tells the truth constantly, about night, hunting, his family and his tastes. Why does honest speech make him more frightening?

Before reading

Read it twice.

Read the passage once as Harker experiences it: an eccentric host, an uneasy night. Then reread it knowing what the Count is. Mark every sentence that changes meaning on the second pass. That difference drives the novel.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

The perfect host.

  1. List every courteous act the Count performs in the passage.
  2. Beside each, write what it controls: Harker’s time, movement, sleep, knowledge.
  3. Draft the letter Harker might send his employer describing this evening: polite, professional, alarmed by nothing.
  4. Discuss: what social rules prevent Harker from naming his fear, and whom do those rules serve here?
Essay bridge

Documents against the dark.

For an essay on the novel’s form, follow the journal outward: Mina’s typewritten transcriptions, Seward’s phonograph, the assembled dossier the hunters read together. The novel argues that shared records defeat ancient evil; then note the irony that everything we know about the Count survives only because his victims wrote it down.
