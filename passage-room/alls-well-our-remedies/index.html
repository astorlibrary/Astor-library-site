Close reading 87 · All’s Well That Ends Well

“Our remedies oft in ourselves do lie”

A physician’s orphaned daughter loves a count who does not know she exists. Alone, she decides that heaven helps those who move—and plans to cure a king. In fourteen lines Helena stops waiting for heaven and decides to act.

William ShakespeareAct 1, Scene 1

Probably written around 1604–05. Helena, ward of the Countess of Rossillion, loves the Countess’s son Bertram; the King of France is dying of a fistula her father might have cured. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read the couplets as a decision being made—each pair of lines is a step. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

HelenaOur remedies oft in ourselves do lie,
Which we ascribe to heaven1: the fated sky
Gives us free scope; only doth backward pull
Our slow designs when we ourselves are dull.2

That makes me see, and cannot feed mine eye?
The mightiest space in fortune nature brings
To join like likes, and kiss like native things.
Impossible be strange attempts to those
10That weigh their pains in sense, and do suppose
What hath been cannot be.3 Who ever strove
To show her merit that did miss her love?4
The king’s disease,—my project may deceive me,
But my intents are fix’d, and will not leave me.5

All’s Well That Ends Well, Act 1, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered within the extract

1

Remedies in ourselves

The first couplet is the whole speech’s thesis and one of Shakespeare’s most quoted: the cures we credit to heaven are often in our own hands. “Remedies” is a physician’s word—Helena is a doctor’s daughter—and it will be literal within an act, when she cures the king with her father’s prescription. But she means it of love too. She has been weeping; now she diagnoses.

2

The fated sky gives free scope

The theology is careful: fate exists, but it “gives us free scope” and only pulls back “when we ourselves are dull”. Heaven does not do the work; it permits it. This is a Renaissance argument about providence and human effort, and Helena resolves it on the side of effort. The rhymes—lie/sky, pull/dull—make the resolution sound settled, like a proverb she has decided to live by.

3

Impossible only to the dull

Strange attempts are impossible only to people who “weigh their pains in sense”—calculate the cost—and assume that what has never happened cannot. Helena is talking herself past prudence. A poor girl marrying a count is exactly the kind of thing that ‘hath not been’; Helena does not accept that this means it cannot be. The speech is a manual for doing the improbable.

4

Merit and love

‘Who ever strove / To show her merit that did miss her love?’ The question is hopeful to the point of naivety, and the play puts it hard to the test: Helena shows her merit, wins Bertram by royal decree, and he runs away before the marriage can be consummated. Merit does not win love in this play; it wins the right to keep trying. Helena’s question is answered no, and she proceeds anyway.

5

The project

The speech ends with a plan stated in a half-line: “The king’s disease”. She will go to Paris, cure him, and claim as her reward the husband of her choice. “My project may deceive me, / But my intents are fix’d”: she admits the plan might fail and commits to it regardless. The couplet is the sound of a decision, and the play’s plot is its consequence. No other comedy heroine sets her own story in motion so deliberately.

What the wording changes

A heroine who declines to wait.

All’s Well That Ends Well is a “problem comedy”—a play whose happy ending is achieved by means the audience is not sure it approves—and the problem is Helena. She is the most active woman in Shakespeare’s comedies: she chooses her man, cures a king to earn him, is refused, follows him to Italy in disguise, substitutes herself for another woman in his bed, and returns pregnant to claim him. Readers have found her admirable, alarming and both. This early soliloquy is where the activity begins, and its argument—that remedies lie in ourselves—is the creed of a woman who will not let her story be written by anyone else.

The speech’s form is unusual: fourteen lines of rhymed couplets, closer to a sonnet than to Shakespeare’s usual soliloquy verse. The couplets give Helena’s reasoning a snapped-shut, proverbial quality, as if each step of her decision were being nailed down. It is the sound of someone converting feeling into policy. The play’s other lovers speak in loose blank verse and prose; Helena, at the moment of resolve, speaks in maxims.

The title is the play’s own question. Helena says it twice—“All’s well that ends well; still the fine’s the crown”—and the ending she engineers is technically well: Bertram accepts her, the king approves, the couples are matched. Whether all really is well is a question the play leaves open. Bertram’s last words are conditional (‘if she, my liege, can make me know this clearly’), and Helena’s triumph is a fact of the plot; nobody says it is a matter of the heart. Her opening soliloquy promised that remedies lie in ourselves. The play grants her the remedy and withholds the cure.

Take one question away

Helena decides that heaven helps those who act, and acts. Does the play reward her self-reliance, or show what it takes to get everything you set out to get?

Before reading

Count the couplets.

Mark the rhyme pairs. Fourteen lines, seven couplets, almost a sonnet. Ask what rhyme does to a decision: does it make Helena sound wise, or sound as if she is persuading herself?

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Test the thesis.

  1. State it: our remedies lie in ourselves, not heaven.
  2. List what Helena does in the play to prove it.
  3. List what she cannot make happen—Bertram’s love.
  4. Decide whether the play agrees with her first couplet or complicates it.
Essay bridge

Helena among the heroines.

For an essay on agency in the comedies, set this speech beside Rosalind’s management of Orlando in As You Like It and Viola’s patience in Twelfth Night. Three women, three ways of getting what they want; ask why Helena’s directness has made her the most controversial.
