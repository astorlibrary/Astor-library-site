Passage Room/Measure for Measure/Claudio on death

Close reading 57 · Measure for Measure

“Ay, but to die, and go we know not where”

A condemned young man has just agreed that death is better than his sister’s dishonour. Then he imagines dying—really imagines it—and the agreement collapses in the most physical speech about death Shakespeare wrote.

William ShakespeareAct 3, Scene 1

Probably written 1603–04. Claudio is sentenced to death for fornication; his sister Isabella, a novice nun, has learned that the deputy Angelo will spare him if she sleeps with Angelo. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read Claudio’s speech for its images of the body, then read Isabella’s answer for its fury. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Measure for Measure3.1

ClaudioAy, but to die, and go we know not where1;
To lie in cold obstruction, and to rot;
This sensible warm motion to become
A kneaded clod2; and the delighted spirit
To bathe in fiery floods, or to reside
In thrilling regions of thick-ribbed ice3;
To be imprisoned in the viewless winds
And blown with restless violence round about
The pendent world; or to be worse than worst
Of those that lawless and incertain thought
Imagine howling—’tis too horrible.
The weariest and most loathed worldly life
That age, ache, penury, and imprisonment
Can lay on nature is a paradise
To what we fear of death.4

IsabellaAlas, alas!

ClaudioSweet sister, let me live.5
What sin you do to save a brother’s life,
Nature dispenses with the deed so far
That it becomes a virtue.

IsabellaO, you beast!
O faithless coward! O dishonest wretch!
Wilt thou be made a man out of my vice?6
Is’t not a kind of incest to take life
From thine own sister’s shame?

Measure for Measure, Act 3, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text

1

“Ay, but”

Two small words bring a resolution down. A moment earlier Claudio agreed that “death is a fearful thing” but a shamed life “hateful”, and seemed to accept his sentence. “Ay, but” is the sound of a mind that has said the brave thing and then looked at what it means. The speech that follows works through imagination, and imagination is what breaks him.

2

The warm body made clay

“This sensible warm motion” is the living body—feeling, warm, moving—and it will become “a kneaded clod”: a lump of worked earth. The contrast is tactile, almost sculptural. Where Hamlet reasons about death as sleep and dream, Claudio feels it as cold, obstruction and rot. The speech is famous because it turns down every consolation and stays with the flesh.

3

The afterlife as a catalogue of torments

The soul may “bathe in fiery floods” or freeze in “thrilling regions of thick-ribbed ice”—thrilling meaning piercing, shivering—or be blown for ever round “the pendent world”, the earth hanging in space. Claudio mixes hell, purgatory and classical underworld without settling on any; the terror is that he does not know which is true, only that “lawless and incertain thought” can imagine worse than all of them.

4

Any life is paradise

The conclusion inverts his earlier bravery completely: the most wretched life imaginable—“age, ache, penury, and imprisonment” heaped together—is “a paradise / To what we fear of death”. The four nouns are a small hell of their own, and he prefers all of them to dying. It is one of the most honest things anyone says in Shakespeare, and the play makes him pay for it immediately.

5

The unbearable request

“Sweet sister, let me live.” Five words, and then a piece of casuistry: a sin done to save a brother becomes a virtue. Claudio is asking Isabella to give her body to Angelo. The plea is shameful and completely human, and the play has arranged things so that we understand him exactly while knowing what he is asking. There is no comfortable place for the audience to stand.

6

Isabella’s fury

Her reply is savage—beast, coward, wretch—and its logic is precise: “Wilt thou be made a man out of my vice?” She frames his survival as a kind of incest, life taken from a sister’s shame. Readers have divided over her for centuries: is this integrity under intolerable pressure, or coldness? The play, characteristically, gives her the moral high ground and makes it very hard to like her standing on it.

What the wording changes

A comedy with a death-speech at its centre.

Measure for Measure is filed among the comedies and ends in marriages, but its heart is this prison scene, and nothing in it is comic. Shakespeare built the play as a moral trap: a puritanical deputy enforcing an old law against fornication, a young man condemned under it, a novice sister who can save him only by breaking her own vows, and a disguised Duke watching it all. The scene between Claudio and Isabella is where the trap closes on the two people least able to bear it—and the play offers no exit that does not cost someone everything.

Claudio’s speech has no abstractions in it. Other Shakespearean meditations on death reason, compare, philosophise; this one imagines, in the body, what it will be like to be a corpse and then a soul in unknown torment. The images are drawn from a whole culture’s fears—Dante’s ice, the Church’s fire, the classical winds that whirl Paolo and Francesca—but Claudio does not know which to believe, and his uncertainty is the modern note. He is not a Christian resigned to judgment nor a stoic resigned to nothing; he is a young man who very much does not want to stop being warm.

The play’s title comes from the Sermon on the Mount—“with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again”—and the scene is a test of that arithmetic. Isabella measures her chastity against her brother’s life and will not trade; Claudio measures his life against her chastity and begs. Neither is simply right. The Duke’s later schemes rescue everyone, but they do not cancel what brother and sister said to each other here, which is why the ending has felt to so many readers like a solution imposed on a problem that had none.

Take one question away

Claudio begs; Isabella refuses and rages. The play calls itself a comedy. Whose measure is the audience asked to accept?

Before reading

Feel the temperatures.

Mark every word of heat and cold in Claudio’s speech: warm, cold, fiery, ice. The body he is losing is warm; every afterlife he imagines is an extreme of temperature. The speech is organised by the skin.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Hold the trial.

  1. Divide the group: prosecute Claudio for cowardice, and defend him; prosecute Isabella for cruelty, and defend her.
  2. Each side must quote from the passage only.
  3. Then ask the play’s question: is there any answer that is not a betrayal?
  4. Compare the Duke’s eventual solution with the choices available in this scene.
Essay bridge

Two speeches on death.

For an essay across plays, set Claudio’s speech beside Hamlet’s “To be, or not to be”. One reasons about “the undiscover’d country” and reaches a stalemate; the other imagines it and reaches terror. Ask what each speech knows that the other will not admit.
