Passage Room/The Picture of Dorian Gray/The Preface

Close reading 41 · The Picture of Dorian Gray

“All art is quite useless.”

Wilde wrote the Preface after his novel was attacked as immoral, and set it like a row of polished blades before the story. It is aestheticism’s creed, a legal defence in epigram form—and the novel behind it tests every sentence to destruction.

Oscar WildeThe Preface

The novel appeared in Lippincott’s Monthly Magazine in 1890 and was denounced in the press. Wilde published these aphorisms in reply in 1891, then set them before the revised, expanded book. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: read each aphorism twice—once as wit, once as argument. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Dorian GrayThe Preface

The artist is the creator of beautiful things. To reveal art and conceal the artist is art’s aim.1 The critic is he who can translate into another manner or a new material his impression of beautiful things.

There is no such thing as a moral or an immoral book. Books are well written, or badly written. That is all.2

The nineteenth century dislike of realism is the rage of Caliban seeing his own face in a glass3. The nineteenth century dislike of romanticism is the rage of Caliban not seeing his own face in a glass.

No artist has ethical sympathies. An ethical sympathy in an artist is an unpardonable mannerism of style. … Thought and language are to the artist instruments of an art. Vice and virtue are to the artist materials for an art.

All art is at once surface and symbol. Those who go beneath the surface do so at their peril. Those who read the symbol do so at their peril.4 It is the spectator, and not life, that art really mirrors.5

We can forgive a man for making a useful thing as long as he does not admire it. The only excuse for making a useless thing is that one admires it intensely.

All art is quite useless.6

The Picture of Dorian Gray, the Preface · public-domain text · two aphorisms omitted at the ellipsis

1

Concealing the artist

Art’s aim is “to reveal art and conceal the artist”—the maker should vanish behind the made thing. The novel that follows dramatises the failure of exactly this: Basil Hallward fears he has put “too much of myself” into Dorian’s portrait, and the picture becomes fatal precisely because artist, sitter and viewer are all entangled in it. The Preface states the rule; the story is the rule broken, with consequences.

2

The defence, in two sentences

This is the Preface’s legal core. Reviewers had called the 1890 version poisonous and prosecutable; Wilde’s answer moves the entire question of morality out of art’s jurisdiction: books can only be well or badly written. “That is all” performs finality—case closed. Four years later, at the Queensberry trial, opposing counsel read Wilde’s aphorisms back to him in court; the defence written for a book was cross-examined as evidence about its author.

3

Caliban at the mirror

Caliban is Shakespeare’s “savage” islander from The Tempest—here, the Victorian reading public. Shown realism, it rages at recognising its own face; shown romanticism, it rages at not finding its face there. The paired sentences form a perfect trap: whatever the public’s objection, it convicts the objector. It is also the Preface’s cruellest joke—Wilde’s attackers are cast as the monster, and their moral outrage as vanity insulted either way.

4

Peril on both routes

Art is “at once surface and symbol”—and both ways of reading it are declared dangerous. The doubled warning sounds like paradox for its own sake until the novel makes it literal: Basil goes beneath the surface of the portrait and is murdered beside it; Dorian reads himself as its symbol and is destroyed by it. The Preface’s airiest epigram is the plot’s exact mechanism, stated in advance like a curse over a cradle.

5

The mirror turned around

Art criticism since Hamlet had called art a mirror held up to nature. Wilde rotates the glass: art mirrors the spectator. What you find in a book—beauty, filth, temptation—is your own face looking back; the critic’s disgust is autobiography. And the novel is built on this sentence: a portrait that shows Dorian not as he is but as what he does becomes the book’s one truthful spectator—a mirror that keeps the receipts.

6

“Useless”, meant precisely

The famous last line is calibrated, not flippant. In the sentence before, usefulness is what excuses ordinary things; admiration is the only excuse for useless ones. “All art is quite useless” therefore places art beyond excuse and beyond use—to moralists, merchants and improving societies alike. It exists to be admired intensely, full stop. The word “quite” does the work: not nearly useless, not sadly useless—entirely, and proudly.

What the wording changes

A manifesto its own novel cross-examines.

The Preface has a battlefield history. The 1890 Lippincott’s version of the novel—shorter, franker about Basil’s adoration of Dorian—was savaged by the London press, and W. H. Smith pulled the magazine from its bookstalls. Wilde fought back in letters to the editors, then distilled the letters into these aphorisms, published them in 1891, and mounted them at the front of the revised book like a gate. Every reader since has entered the novel through its author’s counter-attack: the Preface is not an overture but a fortification.

The strangeness—and the fascination—is that the novel behind the gate keeps disobeying it. A book prefaced by “no such thing as a moral or an immoral book” proceeds to punish every character in strict proportion to their creed: Dorian, who lives the aesthetic doctrine of sensation, dies knife in hand before his own portrait; Basil, who moralises, is murdered; Lord Henry, who only talks, is left untouched and empty-handed. Wilde admitted the paradox himself, remarking in a letter that the book’s moral—all excess brings its punishment—was the one artistic flaw in it. The Preface says art has no ethical sympathies; the plot is practically a sermon.

That tension is why both survive. Read alone, the Preface is the most quotable statement of aestheticism in English. Read against its novel, it becomes something richer: one half of an argument Wilde was having with himself, staged in public—the critic who said art was surface writing a story in which every surface conceals a debt. The trials of 1895 gave the argument its terrible coda, when aphorisms designed to free art from moral judgment were quoted to subject their author to it.

Take one question away

The Preface says books cannot be immoral; the novel punishes its characters like a moralist. Which voice is Wilde’s—or is the disagreement the point?

Before reading

Hear the form.

Note the shape of each aphorism: short declaratives, balanced pairs, a closing snap. The form claims certainty the way a proof does. Ask what an aphorism cannot do—qualify, concede, argue back—and why that suits a defence.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Test the Preface against the plot.

  1. Take three aphorisms: no moral or immoral books; the spectator is mirrored; surface and symbol both perilous.
  2. For each, find the event in the novel that most directly tests it.
  3. Rule on each: upheld, overturned, or complicated.
  4. Write the one-sentence preface the novel’s ending would honestly require—then compare it with Wilde’s.
Essay bridge

Two texts, one novel.

For an essay on the book’s history, compare the 1890 magazine version with the 1891 revision—what was softened, what was added (the Preface, the James Vane subplot), and what the changes concede to the very critics the Preface scorns. The novel’s two versions are its own surface and symbol.
