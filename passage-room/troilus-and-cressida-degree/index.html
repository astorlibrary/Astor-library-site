Close reading 81 · Troilus and Cressida

“Take but degree away, untune that string”

The Greek army is losing because its best soldier will not fight. Ulysses explains why by describing the collapse of the universe. The speech has been quoted for four centuries as Shakespeare’s philosophy of order—by readers who forget who is speaking, and to what end.

William ShakespeareAct 1, Scene 3

Probably written around 1602. Ulysses addresses the Greek council, in the seventh year of the siege of Troy. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read the speech as a philosophy first, then as a politician’s move—the play needs both. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Troilus and Cressida1.3

UlyssesO, when degree is shak’d,
Which is the ladder of all high designs,
The enterprise is sick!1 How could communities,
Degrees in schools, and brotherhoods in cities,
5Peaceful commerce from dividable shores,
The primogenity and due of birth,
Prerogative of age, crowns, sceptres, laurels,
But by degree stand in authentic place?
Take but degree away, untune that string,
10And hark what discord follows!2 Each thing melts
In mere oppugnancy: the bounded waters
Should lift their bosoms higher than the shores,
And make a sop of all this solid globe;
Strength should be lord of imbecility,
15And the rude son should strike his father dead3;
Force should be right; or, rather, right and wrong—
Between whose endless jar justice resides—
Should lose their names, and so should justice too.
Then everything includes itself in power,
20Power into will, will into appetite;
And appetite, an universal wolf,
So doubly seconded with will and power,
Must make perforce an universal prey,
And last eat up himself.4 Great Agamemnon,
25This chaos, when degree is suffocate,
Follows the choking.
…
And ’tis this fever that keeps Troy on foot,
Not her own sinews.5 To end a tale of length,
30Troy in our weakness stands, not in her strength.6

Troilus and Cressida, Act 1, Scene 3 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · nine lines omitted at the ellipsis · lines numbered within the extract

1

Degree

“Degree” is rank—the step each thing occupies on a ladder that runs from God through kings and fathers to the lowest creature. The Elizabethan commonplace of the “great chain of being” is here given its most eloquent statement: shake the ladder, and the enterprise falls. Ulysses is speaking of an army, but the vocabulary (schools, cities, commerce, birth, age, crowns) makes the claim universal before he has finished the sentence.

2

The untuned string

Degree is a string on an instrument; remove it and “hark what discord follows”. The image draws on the old idea of cosmic harmony—the planets as a tuned scale—so that political disorder becomes literally unmusical. It is the speech’s most memorable line because it is its most sensory: you are asked to hear hierarchy, and to hear it break.

3

The son strikes the father

The catalogue of consequences escalates: seas over shores, strength over weakness, and then “the rude son should strike his father dead”. Patricide is the primal breach of degree, the family as the model of the state. Shakespeare returned to this image obsessively—in Lear, in Hamlet—and here it is the hinge between natural disorder and moral disorder: after this line, justice itself loses its name.

4

The universal wolf

The famous climax: power collapses into will, will into appetite, and appetite—“an universal wolf”—devours everything and finally itself. The chain of nouns is a chain of degradations, each word smaller and hungrier than the last. The wolf that eats itself is chaos’s end-state, and it is also a fair description of the war the speech is trying to win: seven years of appetite consuming the appetites that began it.

5

Troy on foot

After the cosmology, the diagnosis: the “fever” of emulation—each rank envying the one above—is what keeps Troy standing, “not her own sinews”. Ulysses has moved from the universe to the Greek camp in thirty lines, and the great chain of being turns out to have been an argument about staff discipline. The grandeur was in service of a practical complaint.

6

The tale of length ends

“To end a tale of length”—Ulysses knows he has been long, and closes with an epigram: “Troy in our weakness stands, not in her strength.” The next moment, Agamemnon asks for the remedy, and Ulysses names Achilles, who sulks in his tent mocking his generals. The whole speech was a preamble to a personnel problem, and its solution—a rigged lottery to shame Achilles into fighting—is the opposite of the harmony it praised.

What the wording changes

The philosophy is real; so is the politics that uses it.

For a long time the degree speech was read as Shakespeare’s own creed—E. M. W. Tillyard made it the centrepiece of his account of the “Elizabethan world picture”, a cosmos of ordered ranks that the plays were assumed to endorse. The speech supports that reading: nowhere else is the doctrine of hierarchy stated with such range and conviction, from the planets to the family to the market. But the play it sits in is Shakespeare’s most corrosive, a Trojan War in which the heroes are vain, the lovers faithless, and the cause (Helen) openly called not worth the cost. To take Ulysses’ speech as the play’s philosophy is to take a politician’s speech at his own valuation.

The context is deflating by design. Ulysses speaks to persuade a failing command that its failure is Achilles’ fault, and his remedy is to manipulate degree, fixing a lottery so that the blockhead Ajax fights Hector instead of Achilles, to provoke Achilles’ pride. The man who describes appetite as a universal wolf spends the rest of the play feeding it. Shakespeare gives the doctrine its finest expression and then shows its finest expositor using it as a tool. Both are true, which is the play’s characteristic bitterness.

What survives the irony is the speech’s analysis of collapse. The chain from power to will to appetite to self-consumption is not refuted by the play; it is demonstrated by it, as the war grinds on and Troilus’s love and Hector’s honour are eaten by the same hunger. Ulysses is right about the wolf. He is simply not exempt from it, and the speech’s afterlife as a hymn to order has always depended on forgetting the scene it was spoken in.

Take one question away

The speech is the greatest defence of hierarchy in English, spoken by a schemer to justify a trick. Can an argument be true when the man making it is not?

Before reading

Map the ladder.

List every rank or institution the speech names: schools, cities, commerce, birth, age, crowns, sceptres, laurels, the family, the planets. Ulysses builds the whole cosmos in a dozen lines. Ask what he leaves out—and whether Troy, or love, is on his ladder at all.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Read it twice.

  1. Read the speech as philosophy: is the account of collapse convincing?
  2. Read the lines that follow, where Ulysses proposes the rigged lottery.
  3. Decide whether the second reading changes the first.
  4. Argue for and against quoting the speech without its context.
Essay bridge

Order and its uses.

For an essay on politics in Shakespeare, set the degree speech beside Menenius’s fable of the belly in Coriolanus and Gaunt’s sceptred isle in Richard II. Each is a beautiful account of order spoken for a practical purpose. Ask whether the plays believe in the pictures their politicians paint.
