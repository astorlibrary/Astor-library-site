Passage Room/The Prince/Feared or loved

Close reading 43 · The Prince

“safer to be feared than loved”

The most notorious sentence in political thought is hedged on every side. Machiavelli would rather a ruler be both—and turns to fear only because he has already decided what human beings are.

Niccolò MachiavelliChapter XVII

Written in 1513 while Machiavelli was exiled from Florentine office; first printed in 1532, after his death. The wording below follows the public-domain W. K. Marriott translation.

How to read this page: notice every qualification before you reach the famous clause. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The PrinceChapter XVII

Upon this a question arises: whether it be better to be loved than feared or feared than loved?1 It may be answered that one should wish to be both, but, because it is difficult to unite them in one person, it is much safer to be feared than loved, when, of the two, either must be dispensed with.2

Because this is to be asserted in general of men, that they are ungrateful, fickle, false, cowardly, covetous3, and as long as you succeed they are yours entirely … but when it approaches they turn against you.

… men have less scruple in offending one who is beloved than one who is feared, for love is preserved by the link of obligation which, owing to the baseness of men, is broken at every opportunity4 for their advantage; but fear preserves you by a dread of punishment which never fails.

Nevertheless a prince ought to inspire fear in such a way that, if he does not win love, he avoids hatred5; because he can endure very well being feared whilst he is not hated, which will always be as long as he abstains from the property of his citizens and subjects and from their women.6

The Prince, Chapter XVII · W. K. Marriott translation · public-domain text · ellipses mark shortened stretches

1

Posed as a debate, not a decree

Machiavelli frames the most infamous idea in the book as an open quaestio—the format of a university disputation, where a question is stated so it can be argued. He is not issuing a command; he is reasoning in public. The notoriety the sentence later acquired flattened this: what reads as cynical decree is presented as the weighing of a genuine dilemma.

2

The buried condition

The famous conclusion arrives wrapped in conditions: one should “wish to be both”, and fear is chosen only “when… either must be dispensed with”. The preference is a fallback, invoked at the point where a ruler cannot have both. Quotation strips the “when” and leaves a slogan; the sentence itself is a reluctant last resort, not a first principle.

3

The premise doing the work

The whole argument rests on this clause about “men”: ungrateful, fickle, false, cowardly, covetous. Grant the premise and fear follows logically; the conclusion is only as dark as the picture of human nature that generates it. Machiavelli claims to describe people as they are, and not as moralists wish them; his own stated method is to follow ‘the real truth of the matter’ and not ‘the imagination of it’. Whether the portrait is realism or libel is the argument the book has provoked for five centuries.

4

Love as a broken link

Love is a “link of obligation”—a bond that depends on the other party choosing to honour it. Because Machiavelli has just declared men base, that link “is broken at every opportunity”. Fear, by contrast, works through “dread of punishment which never fails”: it does not rely on anyone’s virtue. The preference for fear is really a preference for a mechanism that does not depend on trusting people—which is the whole book in miniature.

5

The line everyone forgets

Immediately after choosing fear, Machiavelli sets its limit: avoid hatred. Fear without hatred is workable; hatred is fatal, because it makes subjects enemies who will act against you. This is the pragmatic core the reputation omits—he is not recommending cruelty for its own sake but calculating exactly how much hardness a ruler can afford before it recoils on him.

6

The startling practical rule

How to be feared without being hated? Leave people’s property and their families alone. The advice is almost mundane—and telling: Machiavelli observes that “men more quickly forget the death of their father than the loss of their patrimony”. Money and family are where hatred is bred. The prince may frighten, but must not rob or violate. Even the arch-realist draws a line, and locates it precisely.

What the wording changes

A cautious argument with a scandalous afterlife.

“Machiavellian” entered English as a synonym for treacherous cunning within decades of the book’s printing, and this chapter is the main reason. Yet the passage read whole is careful to the point of anxiety: it prefers both love and fear, chooses fear only as a fallback, forbids hatred, and hedges the choice with conditions. The gap between the sentence and its reputation is itself a lesson in close reading—how a slogan is manufactured by cutting away the clauses that qualify it.

What disturbs is less the preference for fear than the premise underneath it. Machiavelli separated politics from the moral philosophy that had governed “mirror for princes” books for centuries: instead of asking what a good ruler ought to do, he asked what a ruler must do to keep power, given people as he claims they actually are. The move is method, not malice: he analyses power as a physician analyses a disease, describing it without prescribing. But once you accept his description of men as “ungrateful, fickle, false”, the cold conclusions follow, and there is no room left for the argument that virtue is its own reward.

The book’s deepest ambiguity is who it is for. Dedicated to a Medici prince by a writer the Medici had tortured and exiled, The Prince has been read as sincere counsel, as a job application, and—by Rousseau and others—as covert satire, a republican exposing tyranny’s methods so that citizens might recognise them. This chapter supports every reading, which is why the argument has never been settled. What is certain is that Machiavelli made it impossible to discuss power without confronting the question he poses here—and just as impossible to answer it comfortably.

Take one question away

Is Machiavelli telling rulers how to behave—or telling the ruled how power actually works, so they can never again be surprised by it?

Before reading

Underline the hedges.

Mark every qualifying word before the famous clause: may be answered, wish to be both, difficult, when… either must be dispensed with. Then read only the words that survive the anthology version. The distance between the two is the chapter’s real subject.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Test the premise.

  1. Isolate Machiavelli’s claim about human nature (“ungrateful, fickle, false…”).
  2. Grant it, and trace how fear follows as the logical choice.
  3. Deny it—assume most people keep their word—and rebuild the argument. What happens to the conclusion?
  4. Decide whether the chapter’s darkness is about power or about people.
Essay bridge

The effectual truth.

For an essay on Machiavelli’s method, set this chapter beside his statement in Chapter XV that he will follow “the real truth of the matter” rather than “the imagination of it”, since “many have pictured republics and principalities which in fact have never been known or seen”. Ask what a politics that starts from description gains in realism, and what it loses when there is no standard left by which to call a ruler bad.
