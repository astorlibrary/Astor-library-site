Close reading 62 · The Sorrows of Young Werther

“A wonderful serenity has taken possession of my entire soul”

The letter that made a generation of young Europeans weep is, on its surface, about lying in long grass on a spring morning. Read closely, it is about a mind so full that it cannot make anything—and the book’s catastrophe is already in its happiest page.

J. W. von GoetheLetter of 10 May

Published in 1774, when Goethe was twenty-four; it caused a Europe-wide sensation. The novel is told entirely in Werther’s letters to his friend Wilhelm. The wording below follows R. D. Boylan’s public-domain translation.

How to read this page: read it as a letter—happy, overflowing, addressed to a friend. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Werther10 May

May 10.

A wonderful serenity has taken possession of my entire soul1, like these sweet mornings of spring which I enjoy with my whole heart. I am alone, and feel the charm of existence in this spot, which was created for the bliss of souls like mine. I am so happy, my dear friend, so absorbed in the exquisite sense of mere tranquil existence, that I neglect my talents. I should be incapable of drawing a single stroke at the present moment; and yet I feel that I never was a greater artist than now.2

When… I throw myself down among the tall grass by the trickling stream; and, as I lie close to the earth, a thousand unknown plants are noticed by me: when I hear the buzz of the little world among the stalks3, and grow familiar with the countless indescribable forms of the insects and flies, then I feel the presence of the Almighty, who formed us in his own image, and the breath of that universal love which bears and sustains us… and then, my friend, when darkness overspreads my eyes, and heaven and earth seem to dwell in my soul and absorb its power, like the form of a beloved mistress4, then I often think with longing, Oh, would I could describe these conceptions, could impress upon paper all that is living so full and warm within me5, that it might be the mirror of my soul, as my soul is the mirror of the infinite God!

The Sorrows of Young Werther, letter of 10 May · Boylan translation · public-domain text · ellipses mark shortened stretches

1

Possessed by calm

Serenity has “taken possession” of him—even Werther’s peace is described as a seizure. The verb is the first hint of the book’s pattern: his feelings do not visit him, they occupy him, and he never speaks of them as anything but total (“entire soul”, “whole heart”). The letter is happy, but the grammar of being overwhelmed is already in place, waiting for a darker feeling to use it.

2

The artist who cannot draw

The letter’s central paradox: he could not “draw a single stroke”, yet has never been “a greater artist”. For Werther, art lies in feeling: the fullness of the inner life counts for more than anything it produces. This is the Romantic creed a generation before Wordsworth, and it is also the seed of Werther’s ruin: a man whose feelings need no outlet will, when the feelings turn, have no outlet for them either.

3

The buzz of the little world

Lying “close to the earth”, he notices “a thousand unknown plants” and hears the insects’ “little world among the stalks”. The perspective drops to ground level, and the smallest life becomes a universe. Goethe was a serious naturalist, and the precision is his; but for Werther the observation goes straight to the Almighty—every fly is proof of universal love. He cannot look at anything without feeling everything.

4

The world as a mistress

Heaven and earth “dwell in my soul” and “absorb its power, like the form of a beloved mistress”. The simile is startling in a letter about landscape: nature is loved as a woman is loved, and the loving drains him. Lotte has not yet appeared in the novel. When she does, she will step into an emotional shape Werther has already made—a passion that absorbs everything and leaves nothing over.

5

Longing to describe

The letter ends in a wish it cannot fulfil: to “impress upon paper all that is living so full and warm within me”. He cannot; a few lines later he says the effort would destroy him. The insufficiency of expression is presented as a kind of glory—feeling too big for words—and it is also the trap. Everything in Werther is “so full” that nothing can hold it, and the novel is the record of a man finding, at last, that his life cannot hold it either.

What the wording changes

The happiest letter contains the whole tragedy.

Werther was a literary event without precedent. Published anonymously in 1774, it swept Europe: young men dressed in Werther’s blue coat and yellow waistcoat, the book was banned in some cities for fear of imitative suicides, and Napoleon reportedly told Goethe he had read it seven times. This letter—among the earliest in the book, before Lotte, before jealousy, before the pistols—is where readers fell in love with Werther, and it shows why. The voice is intimate, rapturous, alive to every stalk and insect, and it addresses the reader as a friend (“my dear friend”, “Wilhelm”) so that we feel confided in from the first page.

Goethe’s craft is to make the joy and the doom the same substance. Every feature of Werther’s later collapse is present in his spring happiness: the totality (entire soul, whole heart), the passivity (serenity possesses him; nature absorbs his power), the preference for feeling over doing, the love that models itself on a mistress before there is one, the conviction that his inner life is too vast for any expression. The epistolary form does the rest. We read Werther with no narrator to warn us, in his own present tense, and only in retrospect do we see that the letter about the little world among the stalks was already written in the language of a man who could not survive.

Goethe himself grew wary of the book. He had written it fast, out of his own unhappy love and a friend’s suicide, and spent the rest of his long life partly distancing himself from its hero—revising it in 1787 to make Werther less sympathetic, and telling later visitors that he could hardly bear to reread it. The 10 May letter is the case for the defence. Read alone, it is one of the most beautiful pages of European Romanticism; read as the first act of a tragedy, it is a study in how a temperament that feels everything can be, at the same moment, radiant and lost.

Take one question away

Werther calls himself a greater artist for making nothing. Is the letter a celebration of feeling over craft—or the first symptom of the illness that kills him?

Before reading

Mark the totals.

Underline every word of totality: entire, whole, thousand, countless, universal, infinite, all. Werther never feels partially. The letter is a lesson in a temperament, and the vocabulary is the diagnosis.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Read it twice.

  1. Read the letter as a first-time reader: a happy young man in a spring field.
  2. Read it again knowing the ending. Mark every phrase that now sounds like warning.
  3. Ask what the letter form does that a narrator could not—what it withholds.
  4. Discuss whether the book wants the reader’s sympathy for Werther, or puts that sympathy on trial.
Essay bridge

Nature, then Lotte.

For an essay on the novel’s structure, follow the language of this letter—possession, absorption, the beloved mistress—into the letters after Werther meets Lotte, and then into the final section where the “editor” takes over the narration. Ask whether Lotte causes Werther’s tragedy or merely gives a shape he already had a name.
