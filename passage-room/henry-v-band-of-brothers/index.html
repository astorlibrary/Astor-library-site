Close reading 34 · Henry V

“We few, we happy few”

Outnumbered before Agincourt, a king overhears his cousin wish for reinforcements—and answers with the most effective piece of persuasion in English drama. Its genius is that it tells the men, quite openly, how the story of this day will be improved in the telling.

William ShakespeareAct 4, Scene 3

Written around 1599. The battle is Agincourt, 25 October 1415—the feast day of Saints Crispin and Crispinian. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: read it as its first audience heard it—tired, frightened men being talked out of wishing they were elsewhere. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Henry V4.3

KING.
What’s he that wishes so?
My cousin Westmorland? No, my fair cousin.
If we are mark’d to die, we are enough
To do our country loss; and if to live,
The fewer men, the greater share of honour.1
God’s will! I pray thee, wish not one man more. …
But if it be a sin to covet honour,
I am the most offending soul alive.2 …
Rather proclaim it, Westmorland, through my host,
That he which hath no stomach to this fight,
Let him depart. His passport shall be made,
And crowns for convoy put into his purse.3
We would not die in that man’s company
That fears his fellowship to die with us.
This day is call’d the feast of Crispian.
He that outlives this day, and comes safe home,
Will stand a tip-toe when this day is named,
And rouse him at the name of Crispian. …
Old men forget; yet all shall be forgot,
But he’ll remember with advantages
What feats he did that day.4 Then shall our names,
Familiar in his mouth as household words,
Harry the King, Bedford, and Exeter,
Warwick and Talbot, Salisbury and Gloucester,
Be in their flowing cups freshly remembered. …
From this day to the ending of the world,
But we in it shall be remembered,
We few, we happy few, we band of brothers.
For he today that sheds his blood with me
Shall be my brother; be he ne’er so vile,
This day shall gentle his condition;5
And gentlemen in England now abed
Shall think themselves accurs’d they were not here6,
And hold their manhoods cheap whiles any speaks
That fought with us upon Saint Crispin’s day.

1

Honour by division

The reply reframes arithmetic. Westmorland wished for ten thousand more men; Henry treats honour as a fixed sum to be divided—the fewer the sharers, the richer each portion. It is economics vocabulary throughout: share, covetous, gold, cost. The move is audacious because it concedes the fact (we are few) and converts it, untouched, into the advantage.

2

A sin he confesses to

Henry has just forsworn greed for gold and fine garments; the one thing he admits coveting is honour—and if that is sinful, he is “the most offending soul alive”. The mock-confession does double work: it makes ambition sound like piety, and it quietly reminds the army that their king wants exactly what he is about to offer them a share of.

3

The door held open

Anyone without stomach for the fight may leave—with passport and travel money. The offer costs nothing (who will walk out now, alone, in front of the king?) and buys everything: every man who stays has now, formally, chosen to stay. The speech manufactures volunteers out of conscripts. From this line forward, being present is itself a boast.

4

“With advantages”

The speech’s most extraordinary honesty: the old soldier “will remember with advantages / What feats he did that day”—that is, he will exaggerate. Henry promises his men not accurate glory but improvable memory, a story that grows in the telling over decades of anniversary drinking. A recruitment speech that admits how legends are made, inside the play that is itself the legend, two centuries improved.

5

Blood that gentles

The tricolon lands—few, happy few, band of brothers—and then the astonishing offer: whoever sheds blood with the king today “shall be my brother”, and however low-born (“ne’er so vile”), this day shall “gentle his condition”—make him a gentleman. Battlefield kinship dissolves the entire social order for one day. Whether the promise outlives the day, the play never shows; the speech needs it true only until nightfall.

6

Shame for the absent

The final movement turns outward, to men not present: gentlemen “now abed” in England will think themselves accursed and “hold their manhoods cheap” when Agincourt is spoken of. Having made presence a choice and a privilege, Henry completes the circle—absence becomes the disgrace. Fear of missing the story replaces fear of dying in it; the speech ends having reversed which of the two is unbearable.

What the wording changes

The play gives us the speech—and the night before it.

As oratory the speech is close to perfect, and the play knows it: structure, timing, audience-management, the sequence from arithmetic to offer to prophecy to shame. But Shakespeare placed it the morning after a very different scene. In the dark of 4.1, Henry walks his camp disguised, and the common soldier Michael Williams tells him to his face what a king’s cause can cost: legs and arms and heads chopped off in battle, wives and children left destitute, debts unpaid—and that if the cause is wrong, the king has a heavy reckoning to make. Henry argues back, and does not entirely win. The St Crispin’s Day speech answers Williams not with argument but with transformation: it changes what the fight feels like, which is what oratory is for.

The speech’s promises deserve their own audit. “Be he ne’er so vile, / This day shall gentle his condition” is glorious and unredeemed—no commoner is ennobled in the play’s remaining scenes, and the casualty list Henry reads afterwards carefully preserves rank: four named gentlemen, then “of all other men / But five and twenty”. The brotherhood was rhetoric with a day-long warranty. Noticing this is not cynicism; the play builds the observation in, setting the Chorus’s golden narration against scenes—the ordered killing of the prisoners among them—that the golden version has to omit.

That doubleness is why the play and the speech have served every purpose since: recruitment and requiem, Olivier’s wartime film and the bitterest modern stagings, with the text supporting all of them. “With advantages” is the phrase to hold on to. The speech tells you, while it works on you, exactly how such stories get improved—and it works anyway. That is the most instructive thing rhetoric can do: succeed while showing its hand.

Take one question away

Henry admits the story will be exaggerated even as he creates it. Does that honesty make the speech more trustworthy—or more dangerous?

Before reading

Reconstruct the audience.

List who is listening: nobles named later in the speech, and thousands of unnamed archers and foot-soldiers, sick, hungry, five-to-one outnumbered. Mark which lines are aimed at the named and which at the nameless. The speech serves both audiences at once—find where it switches.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Map the persuasion.

  1. Divide the speech into its moves: reframing the numbers, refusing reinforcements, the offer to depart, the Crispin prophecy, the brotherhood, the shaming of the absent.
  2. For each move, name the emotion it replaces and the one it installs.
  3. Identify the one move the speech could not survive losing.
  4. Test the speech against Williams’s challenge from the night before: which of his points does it answer, and which does it simply drown out?
Essay bridge

The Chorus against the scenes.

For an essay on the play’s politics, set this speech between the Chorus’s epic framing (“O for a Muse of fire”) and the scenes the Chorus glosses over—the Southampton plot, the threats before Harfleur, the killing of the prisoners. The play keeps both registers running; decide whether it finally endorses the legend, exposes it, or insists we hold both.
