Passage Room/Antony and Cleopatra/The barge she sat in

Close reading 58 · Antony and Cleopatra

“The barge she sat in, like a burnished throne”

The most sumptuous description in Shakespeare is spoken in Rome, by a soldier, to two men who have never seen her. Cleopatra is never more present than when she is being reported.

William ShakespeareAct 2, Scene 2

Probably written around 1606–07. Enobarbus, Antony’s blunt lieutenant, describes to the Romans Maecenas and Agrippa how Cleopatra first met Antony on the river Cydnus. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: notice that the speaker is the play’s cynic—and that the description is his, not the poet’s. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Antony and Cleopatra2.2

EnobarbusI will tell you.
The barge she sat in, like a burnished throne,
Burned on the water.1 The poop was beaten gold;
Purple the sails, and so perfumed that
5The winds were love-sick with them2; the oars were silver,
Which to the tune of flutes kept stroke, and made
The water which they beat to follow faster,
As amorous of their strokes. For her own person,
It beggared all description3: she did lie
10In her pavilion, cloth-of-gold of tissue,
O’erpicturing that Venus where we see
The fancy outwork nature. On each side her
Stood pretty dimpled boys, like smiling Cupids,
With divers-coloured fans, whose wind did seem
15To glow the delicate cheeks which they did cool,
And what they undid did.4

AgrippaO, rare for Antony!

Enobarbus… From the barge
A strange invisible perfume hits the sense
Of the adjacent wharfs. The city cast
Her people out upon her5, and Antony,
Enthroned i’ th’ market-place, did sit alone,
Whistling to th’ air, which, but for vacancy,
Had gone to gaze on Cleopatra too,
And made a gap in nature.6

Antony and Cleopatra, Act 2, Scene 2 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered from the start of the description · six lines omitted at the ellipsis

1

Fire on water

The barge “burned on the water”—gold so bright it seems to set the river alight. Shakespeare took the scene from Plutarch (in North’s translation), where the barge simply has a gilded poop and purple sails; the burning is his. The first image fuses the elements that should not mix, and the whole speech follows: winds in love, water chasing oars, air that would leave its place. Cleopatra disorders nature by being looked at.

2

Everything falls in love

The sails are so perfumed that “the winds were love-sick with them”; the water, beaten by the oars, follows faster “as amorous of their strokes”. Enobarbus makes the physical world Cleopatra’s first set of lovers. The conceit is extravagant and precise at once—a boat moving upstream, wind in the sails, wake behind—every ordinary fact of sailing recast as desire.

3

The description that refuses to describe

When he reaches Cleopatra herself, Enobarbus says she “beggared all description”—and then describes her surroundings instead: the pavilion, the cloth-of-gold, a painting of Venus she outdoes. It is the oldest trick of praise, and the truest: her presence is rendered entirely through its effects. We never learn what she looks like. We learn what the wind, the water, the city and Antony did.

4

The fans that heat what they cool

The Cupid-boys’ fans make her cheeks glow even as they cool them—“what they undid did”. The paradox is Cleopatra’s essence in miniature: every attempt to reduce her intensity increases it. Enobarbus will say it outright a few lines later: “she makes hungry / Where most she satisfies.” The speech keeps finding physical images for a woman who reverses cause and effect.

5

A city empties itself

“The city cast / Her people out upon her”—Tarsus pours into the streets as if the city itself were expelling its population toward the river. The image makes crowds a natural force, like a tide. Against it stands Antony, “enthroned i’ th’ market-place”, sitting alone. The Roman general on his seat of power has been abandoned by an entire population for a better spectacle.

6

Whistling to a gap in nature

Antony is left “whistling to th’ air”—and even the air would have gone to look at Cleopatra, except that its going would leave a vacuum, “a gap in nature”, which nature abhors. The hyperbole is so total it becomes comic, and Enobarbus knows it. This is the great soldier reduced to a man whistling in an empty square; the rest of the tragedy is Antony trying, and failing, to leave that square.

What the wording changes

The cynic is the one who cannot stop.

The choice of speaker is the scene’s masterstroke. Enobarbus is the play’s plain-speaking Roman soldier, a man who mocks Antony’s infatuation and will later desert him. Shakespeare gives the most rapturous speech in the play to its least rapturous character, and lets us watch the description run away with him: prompted by a sceptical Roman’s remark, he means to report a spectacle and finds himself building it, image on image, until even the air is in love. The reluctance of the witness is the proof of the thing witnessed. If Enobarbus talks like this, the Romans—and the audience—must believe her.

The speech also establishes the play’s method of making Cleopatra. She is on stage for much of the play, but her power is created largely by report—by what others say she is, what she did on the Cydnus, what Antony gave up for her. Enobarbus never describes her face; he describes a world rearranged around her. This is how the play handles its central impossibility: an actor (a boy, in Shakespeare’s theatre) cannot embody “infinite variety”, so the language builds it elsewhere, in winds and water and emptied cities, and lets the audience supply the rest.

The scene’s placement is pointed. Enobarbus speaks in Rome, moments after Antony has agreed to a political marriage with Octavia to mend his alliance with Caesar. The Romans ask whether Antony will now leave Cleopatra; Enobarbus answers with this speech, and then, flatly: “Never. He will not.” The description of the barge is the argument. A man who has seen that cannot marry Octavia and mean it—and the tragedy is already in motion, told as a memory of a river.

Take one question away

Enobarbus says Cleopatra “beggared all description” and then describes for thirty lines. Does the speech capture her, or prove that she cannot be captured?

Before reading

Read Plutarch first.

Find North’s Plutarch on the Cydnus (Shakespeare’s source, freely available). Lay the prose beside the verse and mark every phrase Shakespeare added—“burned on the water”, the love-sick winds, the gap in nature. The additions are where the poetry lives.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

What she looks like.

  1. List every detail of Cleopatra’s appearance in the speech.
  2. Notice how short the list is. List instead what the world does in response to her.
  3. Discuss why describing effects is more persuasive than describing features.
  4. Try the technique on someone or something else in a paragraph of your own.
Essay bridge

Rome and Egypt.

For an essay on the play’s two worlds, set this speech beside Caesar’s cold account of Antony in Act 1 and Cleopatra’s own dream of Antony in Act 5 (“His legs bestrid the ocean”). Each is a portrait made by report. Ask how the play uses description to stage a contest between Roman measure and Egyptian excess.
