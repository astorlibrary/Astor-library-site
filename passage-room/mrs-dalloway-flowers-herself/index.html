Passage Room/Mrs Dalloway/The flowers herself

Close reading 54 · Mrs Dalloway

“buy the flowers herself”

A woman decides to fetch her own flowers for a party. In the space of a sentence, the errand opens a door—and thirty years, a lost love, and the whole weather of a life come pouring through it.

Virginia WoolfThe opening

Published in 1925. The novel follows a single June day in post-war London as Clarissa Dalloway prepares for an evening party. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: watch a present-day errand become a memory without any seam between them. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

Mrs DallowayThe opening

Mrs. Dalloway said she would buy the flowers herself.1

For Lucy had her work cut out for her. The doors would be taken off their hinges2; Rumpelmayer’s men were coming. And then, thought Clarissa Dalloway, what a morning—fresh as if issued to children on a beach.3

What a lark! What a plunge!4 For so it had always seemed to her, when, with a little squeak of the hinges, which she could hear now, she had burst open the French windows and plunged at Bourton into the open air.5

… standing there at the open window, that something awful was about to happen; looking at the flowers … until Peter Walsh said, “Musing among the vegetables?”6

Mrs Dalloway, opening · public-domain text · ellipses mark shortened stretches

1

The whole method in one line

The first sentence is domestic, ordinary, and quietly strange. It is not “Mrs Dalloway bought the flowers” but “said she would buy the flowers herself”—reported speech, mid-conversation, with a whole household implied (the servants who might have gone instead). Woolf drops us into an existing life already in motion, seen from a slight, sidelong angle. The famous novel of consciousness begins with an errand, because Woolf’s wager is that a life is fully present in its smallest choices.

2

Doors off their hinges

A practical detail—the party means the doors will be unhinged for the caterers—and a quiet piece of foreshadowing. All day the novel will take things off their hinges: memory swinging open into the present, the boundary between minds giving way, a whole world unfastened. The image also prepares the hinge that squeaks two sentences later, the sound that will swing Clarissa thirty years backward.

3

Free indirect style

“And then, thought Clarissa Dalloway, what a morning—”. The narration slips inside her mind without quotation marks, blending the author’s voice with the character’s. We are neither told about Clarissa from outside nor given a first-person “I”; we are somehow in her thought while a narrator hovers. This free indirect style is Woolf’s central instrument—it lets the book move through many minds in a single day while keeping the intimacy of each.

4

“What a lark! What a plunge!”

Two exclamations, and with the second the present tips into the past. “Plunge” is the pivot word: it belongs to this June morning’s freshness and, in the same instant, to a specific remembered plunge through the French windows at Bourton, decades ago. Woolf lets a single word carry two times at once. The sentence is where the novel’s method announces itself—the deep past always one association away from the surface of now.

5

The squeak that opens the past

The hinge Clarissa “could hear now” is both the caterers’ door in 1923 and the French window at Bourton when she was eighteen. A tiny present sound unlocks a flood of memory—this is how the whole novel works, associatively rather than chronologically, the mind moving by resemblance and sensation. Woolf models consciousness not as a line but as a field, where a squeak in London can stand open onto a girlhood morning half a life away.

6

A voice out of the past, mid-sentence

Into the Bourton memory walks Peter Walsh—the man Clarissa did not marry—with a real remembered line: “Musing among the vegetables?” He arrives inside the reverie, teasing, decades ago, before he will arrive in person that afternoon in the actual plot. Woolf introduces the novel’s central figure of the road not taken as a fragment of overheard speech, surfacing unbidden in a woman’s mind while she thinks about flowers. The whole drama of Clarissa’s choices is already present, folded into an errand.

What the wording changes

A single day, opened all the way down.

Mrs Dalloway takes place in one ordinary June day and contains, through memory, whole lifetimes. This opening establishes the technique that makes that possible. Woolf begins with the most mundane action imaginable—a woman deciding to buy flowers—and within four sentences has moved, without any visible transition, from a Westminster morning in 1923 to a remembered morning at Bourton around 1890, and introduced the man whose proposal Clarissa refused. The past is not narrated as flashback; it wells up through a sound, a word, an association, exactly as it does in a real mind on a real morning. Nothing announces the shift because, Woolf believed, nothing announces it in consciousness either.

The instrument is free indirect discourse, and Woolf uses it more fluidly than almost any writer before her. The narration is neither wholly outside Clarissa nor trapped inside her; it flows in and out of her thought and, as the day goes on, in and out of the thoughts of strangers who never meet her—a shell-shocked veteran, a passer-by, a shop assistant—all linked by the same striking clocks, the same aeroplane, the same June light. This opening teaches the reader the grammar of that movement in miniature: how a “said” can become a “thought”, how a “now” can hold a “then”, so that by the time the book widens to a whole city the reader already knows how to follow a mind through time.

What the errand also opens is the novel’s real subject: a life measured by its unchosen alternatives. That Peter Walsh surfaces here, mid-memory, teasing across thirty years, tells us before any plot begins that Clarissa’s settled, successful present is haunted by the plunge she once took and the plunges she did not—the passionate self she was at Bourton, the man she refused, the different life that might have been. Woolf’s wager, made good in this first page, is that you do not need large events to reach the depths of a person. You need only a woman, a June morning, an open window, and the willingness to follow her mind all the way down through the small squeak of a hinge.

Take one question away

Woolf reaches a whole life through the choice to buy flowers. What does she gain by refusing the large events—and asking us to find a person in her passing thoughts instead?

Before reading

Find the seam.

Read from “What a lark!” and mark the exact word where 1923 becomes Bourton, decades earlier. There is no paragraph break, no “she remembered”. Woolf hides the transition inside a single word. Locate it—that word is the whole technique.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Trace the point of view.

  1. Mark each sentence as narrator’s voice, Clarissa’s thought, or an inseparable blend of both.
  2. Note where quotation marks appear and where thought is given without them.
  3. Rewrite the opening in plain first person (“I said I would buy the flowers…”). What intimacy or distance changes?
  4. Discuss how this method could let the novel enter a stranger’s mind later in the day.
Essay bridge

Time and the party.

For an essay on the novel’s structure, connect this opening’s plunge into the past with the striking of Big Ben through the day and with Septimus Smith, whose shattered sense of time mirrors and darkens Clarissa’s. Ask how a book bounded by a single day uses memory and clock-time to hold a whole life—and a whole society after the war—inside it.
