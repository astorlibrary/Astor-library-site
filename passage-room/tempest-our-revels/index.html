“Our revels now are ended”

Prospero has staged a wedding masque for his daughter, then stopped it dead: he has remembered a plot against his life. The speech that follows turns an interrupted entertainment into a statement about everything.

William ShakespeareAct 4, Scene 1

Probably written 1610–11, performed at court in 1611 and first printed in the 1623 Folio. The wording below follows the public-domain Project Gutenberg text.

How to read this page: remember that the speech is spoken to calm a startled young man—and that the speaker himself is not calm. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The Tempest4.1

PROSPEROYou do look, my son, in a mov’d sort,
As if you were dismay’d: be cheerful, sir:
Our revels now are ended.1 These our actors,
As I foretold you, were all spirits and
5Are melted into air, into thin air2:
And, like the baseless fabric of this vision3,
The cloud-capp’d towers, the gorgeous palaces,
The solemn temples, the great globe itself4,
Yea, all which it inherit, shall dissolve,
10And, like this insubstantial pageant faded,
Leave not a rack behind.5 We are such stuff
As dreams are made on6, and our little life
Is rounded with a sleep.7 Sir, I am vex’d8:
Bear with my weakness; my old brain is troubled.
15Be not disturb’d with my infirmity.

The Tempest, Act 4, Scene 1 · public-domain Project Gutenberg text · lines numbered from the start of the speech

1

A technical word

“Revels” was the official term for courtly entertainments—the Master of the Revels licensed every play in England. Prospero means, narrowly, “the masque is over”. The speech works by letting that narrow meaning widen, sentence by sentence, until “revels” has quietly come to mean everything human beings do.

2

Air, then thinner air

The performers really were spirits, so their vanishing is literal. The repetition—“into air, into thin air”—enacts a double dissolving: first gone, then gone more completely. The phrase has become an everyday idiom for disappearance; here it is exact stagecraft.

3

A building without foundations

“Fabric” meant a building or structure, not cloth; “baseless” means without foundations. A structure with no base should not stand at all—which is precisely what a vision is. The paradox prepares the next move: if this pageant could look so solid, what does solidity prove?

4

The globe and the Globe

The catalogue climbs—towers, palaces, temples—then takes in the world and everyone who will ever inherit it. For the first audiences “the great globe” also named the theatre some of them were standing in. The pun folds the playhouse into the prophecy: the building where worlds are staged will dissolve like one of its own shows.

5

Not even a wisp of cloud

A “rack” is a drifting streak of high cloud—the last trace a storm leaves. When everything dissolves, not even that much residue will remain. The word keeps the speech inside weather and theatre at once: cloud-capped towers vanish without leaving a cloud.

6

Stuff and dreams

“Stuff” is material—the cloth or substance a thing is cut from; “made on” is Jacobean idiom for “made of”. The claim is stranger than its familiarity suggests: not that we have dreams, but that dreams are what we are made from. Substance and illusion swap places in six words.

7

Rounded, not ended

“Rounded” can mean surrounded, completed, even crowned. Life is a brief waking enclosed on both sides by sleep—before birth and after death. The image is gentler than Macbeth’s brief candle: it does not say life means nothing, only that it is small and bounded, like a play with a beginning and an end.

8

The philosopher is shaking

Having spoken the calmest sentences in Shakespeare, Prospero immediately asks pardon for his agitation—vexed, weak, troubled, infirm, disturbed. The serenity and the distress are the same moment. He has remembered Caliban’s plot to murder him; the speech about universal dissolution is what a frightened, angry man says to steady himself.

What the wording changes

Consolation built on the edge of anger.

The speech is addressed to Ferdinand, who is alarmed that the wedding entertainment stopped so abruptly. Read as consolation it is curious comfort: do not be dismayed that the show has ended, because everything—including you, me and the world—will end the same way. That the lines nevertheless feel consoling is their achievement. The verse moves with such evenness that dissolution sounds less like loss than like a natural completion.

Context complicates the calm. Prospero has interrupted his own masque because he remembered, mid-performance, the “foul conspiracy” of Caliban and his confederates against his life. The framing lines—“be cheerful, sir” at one end, “Sir, I am vex’d” at the other—show a man performing composure he does not feel. The greatest statement of detachment in the play is produced by an attachment: to life, to his daughter’s safety, to the project he has not yet finished.

A long tradition hears Shakespeare’s own farewell to the theatre in this speech and in Prospero’s later renunciation of his “rough magic”. The play gives the idea its force—a magician-director dissolving his company of spirits—but it remains a reading, not a fact: Shakespeare went on to collaborate on further plays. What the text itself insists on is smaller and stranger: the man describing the world as a pageant cannot yet leave the stage, because the plot against him is still running.

Take one question away

If the speech is true—if life is an insubstantial pageant—why does Prospero go straight back to work protecting his?

Before reading

Mark the frame.

Bracket the first two lines and the last three: the speech to Ferdinand about dismay, and the apology for being troubled. What remains between the brackets is the famous part. Notice how differently it reads with and without its frame.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Weigh the two theatre speeches.

  1. Set this speech beside Macbeth’s “poor player, / That struts and frets his hour upon the stage”.
  2. List what each claims: about actors, about endings, about meaning.
  3. Find the one word in each that most sets the tone—defend your choice.
  4. Decide: are these two speeches saying the same thing in different moods, or different things?
Essay bridge

Magic renounced.

For an essay on art, power or endings, pair this speech with Prospero’s “Ye elves of hills” renunciation in Act 5 and the epilogue, where he asks the audience’s applause to set him free. Across the three, staged illusion is first dissolved, then surrendered, then handed to us.
