Close reading 51 · The Moonstone

“such a book as Robinson Crusoe never was written”

Before the diamond is stolen, before the detective arrives, an old house-steward sits down to write the story and confesses that he governs his whole life by one book. The voice he does it in is why The Moonstone is still read.

Wilkie CollinsFirst Period · Gabriel Betteredge

Published in 1868 and often called the first great English detective novel. It is told, like The Woman in White, as assembled testimony; the first narrator is Lady Verinder’s old servant. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: listen to the voice—the mystery has not started, but the character has. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The MoonstoneBetteredge

The Events related by Gabriel Betteredge, house-steward in the service of Julia, Lady Verinder

I am not superstitious; I have read a heap of books in my time; I am a scholar in my own way. Though turned seventy, I possess an active memory, and legs to correspond.1 You are not to take it… as the saying of an ignorant man, when I express my opinion that such a book as Robinson Crusoe never was written, and never will be written again.2

I have tried that book for years—generally in combination with a pipe of tobacco—and I have found it my friend in need in all the necessities of this mortal life.3 When my spirits are bad—Robinson Crusoe. When I want advice—Robinson Crusoe. … I have worn out six stout Robinson Crusoes with hard work in my service.4

Still, this don’t look much like starting the story of the Diamond—does it? I seem to be wandering off in search of Lord knows what, Lord knows where.5 We will take a new sheet of paper, if you please, and begin over again, with my best respects to you.6

The Moonstone, First Period · public-domain text · ellipses mark shortened stretches

1

A character drawn in one clause

“An active memory, and legs to correspond”—the little joke, pairing mind and legs as though both were kept fit for service, tells you everything about Betteredge: proud, practical, faintly vain, entirely a servant. Collins establishes a whole personality in a subordinate clause before the plot has moved an inch. The novel’s pleasure is in the teller as much as the tale.

2

A book about a book

Betteredge’s grand claim—that Robinson Crusoe is the greatest book ever written and never to be equalled—is comic in its absoluteness, but it also quietly announces Collins’s lineage. Defoe’s castaway, all method and inventory and reasoning from evidence, is an ancestor of the detective novel Betteredge is about to narrate. The homage is a joke and a family tree at once.

3

Scripture in plain clothes

Betteredge treats Robinson Crusoe exactly as a devout man treats the Bible—“my friend in need in all the necessities of this mortal life”. He opens it at random for guidance and reads the result as prophecy. Collins gives a comic character a genuine faith, and locates it not in religion but in a novel. The joke has an edge: it is a book about how completely a reader can be governed by a book.

4

“Six stout Robinson Crusoes”

The devotion is measured in worn-out copies—six of them, read to pieces “with hard work in my service”. The detail is absurd and touching, and it does real work: it makes Betteredge’s bookishness physical, lifelong, and slightly ridiculous, so that we trust the voice completely and expect to be amused by it. Collins is buying the reader’s affection before spending it on the mystery.

5

The narrator who knows he digresses

“I seem to be wandering off… Lord knows where.” Betteredge catches his own rambling and apologises for it—a self-aware digressiveness that becomes the novel’s comic engine. His detours are the book’s texture and, often, its misdirection: a detective story hides its clues most safely inside the chatter of a narrator who cannot keep to the point and tells you so.

6

The manuscript speaks to you

“We will take a new sheet of paper, if you please… with my best respects to you.” Betteredge addresses the reader directly, as a writer conscious of the page in front of him. The novel never lets you forget it is a written document, composed by a specific hand for a specific purpose—which is the whole method: The Moonstone is a case file, and this is the first witness clearing his throat.

What the wording changes

The detective novel begins with a voice, not a crime.

T. S. Eliot called The Moonstone “the first, the longest, and the best of modern English detective novels”, and much of what later crime fiction inherited is already here: the country-house theft, the famous professional detective (Sergeant Cuff), the red herrings, the reconstruction of a night no one clearly remembers. But Collins’s real innovation is structural and human—he tells the whole mystery through a chain of first-person narrators, each with a distinct voice, none of whom sees the whole. The reader must assemble the truth from partial, biased, sometimes comic testimonies. This opening establishes the method by giving the first witness so vivid a personality that we learn to read through him—to enjoy his digressions while watching for the facts they bury.

Betteredge’s Robinson Crusoe obsession is a running joke, but it is also a key to how the book wants to be read. Defoe’s novel is the great early monument of circumstantial realism—a man surviving by observation, deduction and the patient reading of evidence (a single footprint changes everything, as another passage in this room explores). By making his narrator worship that book, Collins signals the tradition he is working in and the faculty the reader will need: attention to detail, suspicion of the obvious, the reading of small signs. That Betteredge uses the book as an oracle rather than a manual is the comedy; that Collins uses it as a lineage is the craft.

Above all, the passage shows how much of a mystery’s pleasure lives in the telling. Nothing has happened—no diamond, no theft, no detective—yet the page is thoroughly enjoyable, because Collins understood that a story assembled from testimony rises or falls on the trustworthiness and charm of its witnesses. The reader who is delighted by Betteredge in these opening pages will follow him anywhere, and will absorb, half-laughing, the details that a plainer narrator would have flagged as clues. The voice is the misdirection, and the misdirection is the art.

Take one question away

Collins spends the opening of a mystery on a comic old man and his favourite book. Why is winning the reader’s trust in a voice the first move a detective novel has to make?

Before reading

Sketch the narrator.

From these three paragraphs alone, list what you know about Betteredge: age, class, self-image, sense of humour, faith. Collins characterises entirely through voice. The fullness of the portrait, before any event, is the point.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Voice as misdirection.

  1. Mark every digression in the passage—every place Betteredge wanders from “the story of the Diamond”.
  2. Ask what a reader forgives in a charming narrator that they would not in a plain one.
  3. Consider how a real clue could hide inside such chatter.
  4. Rewrite one paragraph as a neutral report and describe what the novel loses.
Essay bridge

Two Crusoes.

For an essay on the detective novel’s origins, connect Betteredge’s devotion to Robinson Crusoe with Defoe’s footprint scene and its forensic reasoning. Trace the faculty both books prize—the reading of evidence—and argue how the realist novel of observation became the detective novel of deduction.
