Close reading 51 · The Moonstone

“such a book as Robinson Crusoe never was written”

Before the diamond is stolen, before the detective arrives, an old house-steward sits down to write the story and admits that he governs his whole life by one book, Robinson Crusoe.

Wilkie CollinsFirst Period · Gabriel Betteredge

Published in 1868 and often called the first great English detective novel. It is told, like The Woman in White, as assembled testimony; the first narrator is Lady Verinder’s old servant. The wording below follows the public-domain text.

How to read this page: listen to the voice. The mystery has not started yet, but the character has. Then select or focus a numbered phrase to bring its note forward.

The MoonstoneBetteredge

The Events related by Gabriel Betteredge, house-steward in the service of Julia, Lady Verinder

I am not superstitious; I have read a heap of books in my time; I am a scholar in my own way. Though turned seventy, I possess an active memory, and legs to correspond.1 You are not to take it… as the saying of an ignorant man, when I express my opinion that such a book as Robinson Crusoe never was written, and never will be written again.2

I have tried that book for years—generally in combination with a pipe of tobacco—and I have found it my friend in need in all the necessities of this mortal life.3 When my spirits are bad—Robinson Crusoe. When I want advice—Robinson Crusoe. … I have worn out six stout Robinson Crusoes with hard work in my service.4

Still, this don’t look much like starting the story of the Diamond—does it? I seem to be wandering off in search of Lord knows what, Lord knows where.5 We will take a new sheet of paper, if you please, and begin over again, with my best respects to you.6

The Moonstone, First Period · public-domain text · ellipses mark shortened stretches

1

A character drawn in one clause

The little joke in “an active memory, and legs to correspond” pairs mind and legs as though both were kept fit for service, and it tells you a good deal about Betteredge. He is proud, practical, faintly vain and entirely a servant. Collins sets up a whole personality in a subordinate clause before the plot has moved an inch. The novel’s pleasure is in the teller as much as the tale.

2

A book about a book

Betteredge’s grand claim, that Robinson Crusoe is the greatest book ever written and never to be equalled, is comic in its absoluteness, and it also tells us where Collins comes from. Defoe’s castaway, all method and inventory and reasoning from evidence, is an ancestor of the detective novel Betteredge is about to narrate.

3

Scripture in plain clothes

Betteredge treats Robinson Crusoe as a devout man treats the Bible: “my friend in need in all the necessities of this mortal life”. He opens it at random for guidance and reads the result as prophecy. Collins gives a comic character a real faith, and makes its object a novel. The joke has an edge, because it shows how completely a reader can be governed by a book.

4

“Six stout Robinson Crusoes”

The devotion is measured in worn-out copies: six of them, read to pieces “with hard work in my service”. The detail is absurd and touching. It makes Betteredge’s bookishness physical, lifelong and slightly ridiculous, so that we trust the voice and expect to be amused by it. Collins is buying the reader’s affection before spending it on the mystery.

5

The narrator who knows he digresses

“I seem to be wandering off… Lord knows where.” Betteredge catches his own rambling and apologises for it, and that self-aware rambling drives the novel’s comedy. His detours are the book’s texture and, often, its misdirection: a detective story hides its clues most safely inside the chatter of a narrator who cannot keep to the point and tells you so.

6

Addressing the reader

“We will take a new sheet of paper, if you please… with my best respects to you.” Betteredge addresses the reader directly, as a writer conscious of the page in front of him. The novel never lets you forget it is a written document, composed by a specific hand for a specific purpose. The Moonstone is a case file, and this is the first witness clearing his throat.

What the wording changes

The first witness

The Moonstone is often called the first full-length English detective novel, and much of what later crime fiction inherited is already here: the country-house theft, the famous professional detective (Sergeant Cuff), the red herrings, the reconstruction of a night no one clearly remembers. Collins tells the whole mystery, as he had told The Woman in White, through a chain of first-person narrators, each with a distinct voice, none of whom sees the whole. The reader must assemble the truth from partial, biased, sometimes comic testimonies. This opening sets up the method: the first witness has so vivid a personality that we learn to read through him, enjoying his digressions while watching for the facts they bury.

Betteredge’s Robinson Crusoe obsession is a running joke, and also a guide to reading the book. Defoe’s novel is the great early monument of circumstantial realism: a man surviving by observation, deduction and the patient reading of evidence (a single footprint changes everything, as another passage in this room shows). By making his narrator worship that book, Collins signals the tradition he is working in and the faculty the reader will need: attention to detail, suspicion of the obvious, the reading of small signs.

The passage also shows how much of a mystery’s pleasure lies in the telling. Nothing has happened yet (no diamond, no theft, no detective), and the page is still a pleasure, because a story assembled from testimony stands or falls on the trustworthiness and charm of its witnesses. A reader delighted by Betteredge in these opening pages will follow him anywhere, and will absorb, half-laughing, details that a plainer narrator would have flagged as clues. The voice is the misdirection.

Take one question away

Collins spends the opening of a mystery on a comic old man and his favourite book. Why might a detective novel need the reader to trust a voice before anything else happens?

Before reading

Sketch the narrator.

From these three paragraphs alone, list what you know about Betteredge: age, class, self-image, sense of humour, faith. Collins characterises entirely through voice, and the portrait is full before any event has happened.

Classroom task · 15 minutes

Voice as misdirection.

  1. Mark every digression in the passage, every place Betteredge wanders from “the story of the Diamond”.
  2. Ask what a reader forgives in a charming narrator that they would not in a plain one.
  3. Consider how a real clue could hide inside such chatter.
  4. Rewrite one paragraph as a neutral report and describe what the novel loses.
Essay bridge

Two Crusoes.

For an essay on the detective novel’s origins, connect Betteredge’s devotion to Robinson Crusoe with Defoe’s footprint scene and its forensic reasoning. Trace the faculty both books prize, the reading of evidence, and argue how the realist novel of observation became the detective novel of deduction.
