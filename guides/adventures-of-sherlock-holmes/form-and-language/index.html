The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes · Arthur Conan Doyle

Form and language

The chain of inference

A conclusion presented as a series of small observations, each of which the reader can check.

Doyle’s trick is to show the links only after the effect: Holmes states the conclusion, is challenged, and then walks the reader back through boots, cuffs and postmarks, so surprise and explanation arrive in that order.

You see, but you do not observe. The distinction is clear.

Sherlock Holmes, A Scandal in Bohemia

Retrospective narration

An account given after the event by a narrator who already knows how it ends.

Watson is writing these up from notes, sometimes years later, and says so. He therefore controls the order of disclosure completely: the reader learns what Holmes noticed at the moment Watson chooses to record it.

To Sherlock Holmes she is always the woman.

Dr Watson, A Scandal in Bohemia

Disguise

A character adopting another identity in full view of the audience.

Holmes goes out as a drunken groom and comes back as a Nonconformist clergyman; the collection’s two best-hidden criminals are also in make-up, which puts detective and quarry in the same trade.

The law cannot, as you say, touch you

Sherlock Holmes, A Case of Identity

Frame narrative

A story enclosed within another story, often several layers deep.

Most cases contain a long uninterrupted first-person account by the client, inside Watson’s account, inside the collection, and the client’s version is always the one with the mistake in it.

It was only by trying begging as an amateur that I could get the facts upon which to base my articles.

Neville St. Clair, The Man with the Twisted Lip

Misdirection

A conspicuous fact placed where the reader will take it for the important one.

The absurdity of the Red-Headed League occupies all the attention the cellar should be getting; a gypsy band, a dying man’s last word and a hat left in the street work the same way.

There is nothing more deceptive than an obvious fact

Sherlock Holmes, The Boscombe Valley Mystery

Idiolect

Giving each character a distinct and consistent way of speaking.

Holmes talks in imperatives and epigrams, Watson in careful subordinate clauses, and the clients in the vocabulary of their trade: a pawnbroker, a banker and an engineer each describe the same city in different nouns.

My life is spent in one long effort to escape from the commonplaces of existence. These little problems help me to do so.

Sherlock Holmes, The Red-Headed League
