Observation

The collection’s founding distinction is that everyone in it has the same evidence and only one person uses it. Holmes reads sleeves, boots, hats and mud, but the stories are honest that this is not enough on its own: he also writes letters, pays informants, consults reference books and puts on costumes.

Through the book Stated flatly in the first story with the stair-count, tested when the method wins and loses in the same case, and handed to a client in the last story, where Violet Hunter does the observing herself.

In the text

He was, I take it, the most perfect reasoning and observing machine that the world has seen, but as a lover he would have placed himself in a false position.

Dr Watson, A Scandal in Bohemia

‘I take it’ is a hedge, and it belongs to Watson: the narrator is offering an assessment he cannot verify about a man he lives with. The machine image will be contradicted within twenty pages by the same narrator noticing that Holmes has kept a photograph.

It is quite a three pipe problem, and I beg that you won’t speak to me for fifty minutes.

Sherlock Holmes, The Red-Headed League

Thinking is given a unit of measurement and a duration, a very Victorian way of describing genius, as a quantity of labour. The fifty minutes also buys Doyle a scene break, and the reader is kept outside the reasoning until the cellar.

Justice and testimony

Holmes is not a policeman, and several stories end with him deciding that the law would produce the worse outcome. He releases a thief at Christmas, keeps a dead man’s secret, tells a client nothing, and in one case can only tell a scoundrel to his face that nothing can touch him.

Through the book The pattern hardens across the book: early cases end in explanation, later ones in a private verdict, until the reader has to weigh the detective’s authority as well as admire his method.

In the text

The law cannot, as you say, touch you

Sherlock Holmes, A Case of Identity

He concedes the legal point and then, in the same breath, tells Windibank that no man ever deserved punishment more, before reaching for a hunting crop. The story ends with the crime proved, the criminal untouchable and the victim never told, and Doyle repeats that combination often enough for it to be deliberate.

None of those which come to me are. I am the last court of appeal.

Sherlock Holmes, The Five Orange Pips

He describes himself in the language of a legal hierarchy while standing entirely outside it, and the claim is about his position: people reach him when the institutions have already failed. The story then makes the boast unbearable, since this client is dead within hours.

Respectability

What most clients are really protecting is a position: a photograph that would end a royal engagement, a coronet that a banker cannot be known to have lost, a husband whose City friends must not learn where his money comes from. The crimes are frequently less shocking than the exposure they threaten.

Through the book From a king’s marriage in the first story to a banker’s household name in the eleventh, the pressure is the same, and the last story moves it out of London to show that distance from neighbours makes it worse.

In the text

To Sherlock Holmes she is always the woman.

Dr Watson, A Scandal in Bohemia

The present tense is the surprising part: Watson is writing long afterwards and the habit still holds. Calling her by a definite article instead of a name makes a person a category of one, and the sentence announces that the book will begin with the case its hero lost.

No, the debt is not to me. You owe a very humble apology to that noble lad, your son, who has carried himself in this matter as I should be proud to see my own son do, should I ever chance to have one.

Sherlock Holmes, The Adventure of the Beryl Coronet

The case is solved by physical evidence and judged on character, and the judgement falls on the client more than the thief. The closing clause is the one glimpse of a private life the collection allows Holmes, offered as a measure of someone else’s conduct.

Class and money

Almost every case has a figure in pounds at its centre: four pounds a week for copying, a hundred a year from an annuity, seven hundred a year from begging, fifty thousand pounds against a coronet. Doyle’s London is a place where an exact income explains a person’s conduct more reliably than their character does.

Through the book The money is comic in the early stories and lethal by the middle ones, where two wills and an annuity are motives for killing.

In the text

It was only by trying begging as an amateur that I could get the facts upon which to base my articles.

Neville St. Clair, The Man with the Twisted Lip

Everything turns on the word ‘amateur’: he begins as a professional writer imitating a beggar and ends as a professional beggar imitating a gentleman, and the change is measured only in income. Doyle lets the man tell it himself, so the reader gets the whole social argument in the voice of someone who would rather not be making it.

I have lost my thumb and I have lost a fifty-guinea fee, and what have I gained?

Victor Hatherley, The Adventure of the Engineer’s Thumb

He totals the case as a balance sheet and finds it in deficit, which is the only story in the collection to end that way. Holmes answers ‘Experience’, and the joke does not quite cover the fact that the detective arrived after everything and caught nobody.

Marriage and money

Weddings in these stories are transactions with documents attached. A stepfather invents a fiancé to keep his stepdaughter’s income at home; a doctor needs his stepdaughters unmarried; a peer marries American mining money; a bride walks out of her own wedding breakfast.

Through the book The collection comes back again and again to the moment just before or just after a marriage, which is when money moves and someone becomes dangerous.

In the text

It is not cold which makes me shiver

Helen Stoner, The Adventure of the Speckled Band

Holmes has offered a physical explanation and she corrects it, which is the first sign that this client can be relied on to report accurately. Doyle gets a whole atmosphere out of a denial: the word ‘terror’ is never needed because the body has already supplied it.

It is very good of Lord St. Simon to honour my head by putting it on a level with his own

Sherlock Holmes, The Adventure of the Noble Bachelor

The client intended a courtesy and has delivered an insult, and Holmes returns it by taking the words literally. It is the collection’s sharpest joke about rank, made by a man who has solved the case before the peer finished stating it and who declines to be thanked for it.

Disguise and the law

Faces are unreliable throughout: Holmes as a groom and as a clergyman, a stepfather in whiskers and tinted spectacles, a journalist in a beggar’s greasepaint, a governess made to sit where her face cannot be seen. Identity in this book is something worn, and often something owned by somebody else.

Through the book Disguise begins as Holmes’s tool, becomes the criminal’s method by the third story, and ends as a way of controlling other people’s bodies at the Copper Beeches.

It was only by trying begging as an amateur that I could get the facts upon which to base my articles.

Neville St. Clair, The Man with the Twisted Lip
