The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes · Arthur Conan Doyle

Themes

Observation

The collection’s founding distinction is that everyone in it has the same evidence and only one person uses it. Holmes reads sleeves, boots, hats and mud, but the stories are honest that this is not enough on its own: he also writes letters, pays informants, consults reference books and puts on costumes.

Stated flatly in the first story with the stair-count, tested when the method wins and loses in the same case, and handed to a client in the last story, where Violet Hunter does the observing herself.

He was, I take it, the most perfect reasoning and observing machine that the world has seen, but as a lover he would have placed himself in a false position.

Dr Watson, A Scandal in Bohemia

You see, but you do not observe. The distinction is clear.

Sherlock Holmes, A Scandal in Bohemia

Justice and testimony

Holmes is not a policeman, and several stories end with him deciding that the law would produce the worse outcome. He releases a thief at Christmas, keeps a dead man’s secret, tells a client nothing, and in one case can only tell a scoundrel to his face that nothing can touch him.

The pattern hardens across the book: early cases end in explanation, later ones in a private verdict, until the reader has to weigh the detective’s authority as well as admire his method.

And that was how a great scandal threatened to affect the kingdom of Bohemia, and how the best plans of Mr. Sherlock Holmes were beaten by a woman’s wit.

Dr Watson, A Scandal in Bohemia

My life is spent in one long effort to escape from the commonplaces of existence. These little problems help me to do so.

Sherlock Holmes, The Red-Headed League

Respectability

What most clients are really protecting is a position: a photograph that would end a royal engagement, a coronet that a banker cannot be known to have lost, a husband whose City friends must not learn where his money comes from. The crimes are frequently less shocking than the exposure they threaten.

From a king’s marriage in the first story to a banker’s household name in the eleventh, the pressure is the same, and the last story moves it out of London to show that distance from neighbours makes it worse.

To Sherlock Holmes she is always the woman.

Dr Watson, A Scandal in Bohemia

And that was how a great scandal threatened to affect the kingdom of Bohemia, and how the best plans of Mr. Sherlock Holmes were beaten by a woman’s wit.

Dr Watson, A Scandal in Bohemia

Class and money

Almost every case has a figure in pounds at its centre: four pounds a week for copying, a hundred a year from an annuity, seven hundred a year from begging, fifty thousand pounds against a coronet. Doyle’s London is a place where an exact income explains a person’s conduct more reliably than their character does.

The money is comic in the early stories and lethal by the middle ones, where two wills and an annuity are motives for killing.

Depend upon it, there is nothing so unnatural as the commonplace.

Sherlock Holmes, A Case of Identity

The law cannot, as you say, touch you

Sherlock Holmes, A Case of Identity

Marriage and money

Weddings in these stories are transactions with documents attached. A stepfather invents a fiancé to keep his stepdaughter’s income at home; a doctor needs his stepdaughters unmarried; a peer marries American mining money; a bride walks out of her own wedding breakfast.

The collection comes back again and again to the moment just before or just after a marriage, which is when money moves and someone becomes dangerous.

The law cannot, as you say, touch you

Sherlock Holmes, A Case of Identity

It is not cold which makes me shiver

Helen Stoner, The Adventure of the Speckled Band

Disguise and the law

Faces are unreliable throughout: Holmes as a groom and as a clergyman, a stepfather in whiskers and tinted spectacles, a journalist in a beggar’s greasepaint, a governess made to sit where her face cannot be seen. Identity in this book is something worn, and often something owned by somebody else.

Disguise begins as Holmes’s tool, becomes the criminal’s method by the third story, and ends as a way of controlling other people’s bodies at the Copper Beeches.

It was only by trying begging as an amateur that I could get the facts upon which to base my articles.

Neville St. Clair, The Man with the Twisted Lip
