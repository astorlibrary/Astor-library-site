The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes · Arthur Conan Doyle

Characters

Sherlock Holmes

Consulting detective, 221B Baker Street; first appears A Scandal in Bohemia

He works for interest more than money, turns down cases that bore him, and treats the law as one option among several. The stories are careful to show his conclusions resting on specialist knowledge, correspondence, disguise and paid informants as much as on the famous glance at a sleeve.

Dr John Watson

Army surgeon, friend, narrator; first appears A Scandal in Bohemia

He decides what the reader is told and when, which makes him the collection’s most important technical device. His admiration colours every portrait of Holmes, and his bafflement keeps a chain of inference feeling like a story.

Irene Adler

Contralto, formerly of the Warsaw Imperial Opera; first appears A Scandal in Bohemia

She recognises Holmes in disguise, follows him home in men’s clothes to be certain, and leaves the country with the photograph and a better husband. Watson records that Holmes has kept her picture and stopped joking about the cleverness of women.

Mary Sutherland

A typist with an income of her own; first appears A Case of Identity

Her hundred a year from a New Zealand annuity is the whole engine of the plot, and she cannot see it because she has been taught to think of the money as her mother’s. Holmes solves her case and then declines to tell her the answer.

Helen Stoner

Stepdaughter of Dr Grimesby Roylott, of Stoke Moran; first appears The Adventure of the Speckled Band

She arrives at Baker Street before seven in the morning, shaking, with bruises on her wrist from the man she lives with. Her sister died two weeks before a wedding and she is about to be married herself, which is the arithmetic the case actually runs on.

Dr Grimesby Roylott

Physician, formerly of Calcutta, of Stoke Moran; first appears The Adventure of the Speckled Band

A doctor with a temper, a prison record and a will that pays him while his stepdaughters stay unmarried. Doyle makes him physically enormous and has him bend a poker in the sitting room, so that the eventual method, small, quiet and indirect, comes as a real surprise.

Violet Hunter

Governess at the Copper Beeches; first appears The Adventure of the Copper Beeches

She is asked to cut her hair, wear a particular blue dress and sit with her back to a window, and she does all three while keeping a record. By the time Holmes arrives she has found a coil of hair like her own in a locked drawer, got into the shut-up wing and worked out roughly what is being concealed.

Neville St. Clair

A gentleman of Lee, and the beggar Hugh Boone; first appears The Man with the Twisted Lip

A journalist who went out to research an article on begging, discovered how much better it paid than writing, and spent years changing his face in a room above an opium den. He is the collection’s clearest statement that respectability is a costume with an income attached.

Jabez Wilson

Pawnbroker, of Saxe-Coburg Square; first appears The Red-Headed League

A slow, red-headed, thoroughly ordinary man who is paid four pounds a week to copy out an encyclopaedia and never wonders why. His complete lack of curiosity is the mechanism of the crime, and Doyle makes him likeable enough that the reader shares it.

Inspector Lestrade

Scotland Yard; first appears The Boscombe Valley Mystery

He appears to be there to be wrong, but the stories are more careful than that: he brings official power Holmes does not have, makes arrests, and is generally right about who had the opportunity and wrong only about what it means.
