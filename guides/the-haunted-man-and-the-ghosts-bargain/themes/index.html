The use of painful memory

The bargain rests on the idea that people would be happier without their memory of sorrow, wrong and trouble. Every scene after it proves the idea false: gratitude, forgiveness and love turn out to be made from the memories Redlaw gave away.

Through the book Redlaw begins by calling memory his curse and ends by thanking Heaven for its return; the book’s last words are the founder’s prayer that his memory be kept green.

In the text

Everybody said he looked like a haunted man.

Chapter I

The first sentence of the book is Everybody said so, and this is what everybody said. Dickens lets the title come to us as gossip, and then spends two pages agreeing with it, so the reader meets Redlaw through his appearance before hearing him speak.

but for some trouble and sorrow we should never know half the good there is about us.

Chapter II

Trouble and sorrow are two of Redlaw’s three words, and Milly gives them the opposite value. She says it with no idea that the man hiding in the next room has just given his away.

Remembering in order to forgive

Milly’s answer to Redlaw is that people should remember the wrongs done to them so that they can forgive them. A man who cannot remember a wrong can only pay for it; he cannot pardon it.

Through the book Redlaw can offer Longford money and an abstract forgiveness in the third chapter, but the real forgiveness comes only after his memory returns.

In the text

Thus, memory is my curse; and, if I could forget my sorrow and my wrong, I would!

Chapter I

Spoken by the ghost, the wish belongs to the man, which is why Redlaw cannot argue with it. The whole book is the testing of this one sentence.

That we may forgive it.

Chapter III

Five words from a woman who says she has no learning answer the learned man’s reasoning of the first chapter. Forgetting a wrong makes it impossible to pardon.

The street child

The boy is the one person on whom the gift has no effect, because nothing kind has ever happened to him. The Phantom makes him the book’s social argument: a nation that leaves children to grow up like this will reap what it sows.

Through the book Redlaw moves from disgust at the boy to seeing his own face in him, and finally to vowing to protect and teach him.

In the text

A baby savage, a young monster, a child who had never been a child

Chapter I

Each phrase is harsher than the last and each is qualified by his age. Dickens uses the language of horror for a six-year-old deliberately, so that the reader has to ask who made him so.

He is the growth of man’s indifference; you are the growth of man’s presumption.

Chapter III

The Phantom’s sentence sets the two characters at opposite poles and then joins them. The boy is what neglect makes; Redlaw is what pride has made of himself; both have arrived at the same emptiness.

Home and hardship

The Tetterbys have tried every trade and have eight children and little money, yet Mrs Tetterby comes to see their struggles as the thing that made them one. The book sets their crowded parlour against Redlaw’s solitary, vault-like rooms.

Through the book The gift reverses the Tetterbys’ feelings overnight, and Milly’s arrival restores them, with Mrs Tetterby finding ‘the air of home’ in her husband.

In the text

There’s a motherly feeling in Mrs. William’s breast that must and will have went.

Chapter I

The grammar collapses under the weight of his admiration, which is the joke and also the truth. William cannot say why Milly mothers everyone, only that she must; the book supplies the reason at the end, a child she lost.

All our struggles for a livelihood, all our cares and wants since we have been married, all the times of sickness, all the hours of watching, we have ever had, by one another, or by the children, seemed to speak to me, and say that they had made us one

Chapter II

Everything she lists is a hardship, and every hardship is what binds them. It is Milly’s argument made in a poor family’s parlour before Redlaw arrives, and it is the thing his touch will take away a few minutes later.

Learning and feeling

Redlaw keeps all his knowledge and loses only the feelings bound up with his memories. The question is what learning is worth when it is cut off from sympathy, and the book reverses the positions of the learned man and the uneducated woman.

Through the book Redlaw reaches the truth through his own science, reflecting that in the material world no atom can be lost without a gap, and applies it at last to good and evil in memory.

In the text

I am infected! I am infectious! I am charged with poison for my own mind, and the minds of all mankind.

Chapter II

A chemist to the last, he reaches for the language of contagion. The antidote he wanted in the first chapter has turned out to be a poison he carries to others.

In the material world as I have long taught, nothing can be spared; no step or atom in the wondrous structure could be lost, without a blank being made in the great universe.

Chapter II

Redlaw finds his repentance in his own subject. He applies the conservation of matter to memory, and concludes that sorrow and wrong cannot be subtracted without leaving a gap.

Poverty

The characters live in a college lodge, a failing shop, a garret and a lodging-house for travellers under a viaduct. Dickens puts the destitute boy and the woman on the stairs in front of a learned man who has shut himself away from them.

Through the book What begins as private grief becomes, in the Phantom’s speech over the sleeping boy, a charge against every parent, every country and every religion that tolerates such children.

In the text

The gift that I have given, you shall give again, go where you will.

Chapter I

As in a fairy-tale bargain, the sting comes after the contract. Redlaw had asked what he himself would lose; he never asked what others would, and the rest of the book is the answer.

Woe, tenfold, to the nation that shall count its monsters such as this, lying here, by hundreds and by thousands!

Chapter III

The ghost turns from Redlaw’s private case to the country’s. The word monsters picks up the narrator’s description of the boy in the first chapter and throws it back at the reader.
