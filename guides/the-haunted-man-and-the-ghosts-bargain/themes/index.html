The Haunted Man and the Ghost’s Bargain · Charles Dickens

Themes

The use of painful memory

The bargain rests on the idea that people would be happier without their memory of sorrow, wrong and trouble. Every scene after it proves the idea false: gratitude, forgiveness and love turn out to be made from the memories Redlaw gave away.

Redlaw begins by calling memory his curse and ends by thanking Heaven for its return; the book’s last words are the founder’s prayer that his memory be kept green.

Everybody said he looked like a haunted man.

Chapter I

Lord! keep my memory green!

Chapter I

Remembering in order to forgive

Milly’s answer to Redlaw is that people should remember the wrongs done to them so that they can forgive them. A man who cannot remember a wrong can only pay for it; he cannot pardon it.

Redlaw can offer Longford money and an abstract forgiveness in the third chapter, but the real forgiveness comes only after his memory returns.

Thus, memory is my curse; and, if I could forget my sorrow and my wrong, I would!

Chapter I

I have lived bold, and I mean to die bold.

Chapter II

The street child

The boy is the one person on whom the gift has no effect, because nothing kind has ever happened to him. The Phantom makes him the book’s social argument: a nation that leaves children to grow up like this will reap what it sows.

Redlaw moves from disgust at the boy to seeing his own face in him, and finally to vowing to protect and teach him.

A baby savage, a young monster, a child who had never been a child

Chapter I

He is the growth of man’s indifference; you are the growth of man’s presumption.

Chapter III

Home and hardship

The Tetterbys have tried every trade and have eight children and little money, yet Mrs Tetterby comes to see their struggles as the thing that made them one. The book sets their crowded parlour against Redlaw’s solitary, vault-like rooms.

The gift reverses the Tetterbys’ feelings overnight, and Milly’s arrival restores them, with Mrs Tetterby finding ‘the air of home’ in her husband.

There’s a motherly feeling in Mrs. William’s breast that must and will have went.

Chapter I

All our struggles for a livelihood, all our cares and wants since we have been married, all the times of sickness, all the hours of watching, we have ever had, by one another, or by the children, seemed to speak to me, and say that they had made us one

Chapter II

Learning and feeling

Redlaw keeps all his knowledge and loses only the feelings bound up with his memories. What is learning worth when it is cut off from sympathy? The book reverses the positions of the learned man and the uneducated woman.

Redlaw reaches the truth through his own science, reflecting that in the material world no atom can be lost without a gap, and applies it at last to good and evil in memory.

I am infected! I am infectious! I am charged with poison for my own mind, and the minds of all mankind.

Chapter II

In the material world as I have long taught, nothing can be spared; no step or atom in the wondrous structure could be lost, without a blank being made in the great universe.

Chapter II

Poverty

The characters live in a college lodge, a failing shop, a garret and a lodging-house for travellers under a viaduct. Dickens puts the destitute boy and the woman on the stairs in front of a learned man who has shut himself away from them.

What begins as private grief becomes, in the Phantom’s speech over the sleeping boy, a charge against every parent, every country and every religion that tolerates such children.

The gift that I have given, you shall give again, go where you will.
