The Haunted Man and the Ghost’s Bargain · Charles Dickens

Form and language

The double

A figure who mirrors or embodies part of another character.

The Phantom has Redlaw’s face and speaks his thoughts as an echo, and the boy’s face comes to wear Redlaw’s expression. Dickens dramatises a man bargaining with himself.

Thus, memory is my curse; and, if I could forget my sorrow and my wrong, I would!

Chapter I

Refrains

A phrase that recurs and gathers meaning each time.

Sorrow, wrong and trouble; the gift that I have given; Lord, keep my memory green; Philip’s I’m eighty-seven. Each returns in a changed situation, and the last one closes the book.

Lord! keep my memory green!

Chapter I

Idiolect

A way of talking that belongs to one character.

William’s That’s what I always say, Philip’s Where’s my son William?, Milly’s That’s another. The phrases make the Swidgers comic, and their loss or distortion under the gift is how the reader sees the change.

There’s a motherly feeling in Mrs. William’s breast that must and will have went.

Chapter I

Catalogue

A long list of parallel clauses building a scene.

The first chapter’s run of sentences beginning When, from the city streets to ships at sea and children afraid of the stairs, slows the story down to the moment the shadows gather and the knock comes.

A baby savage, a young monster, a child who had never been a child

Chapter I

Symbolic objects

Objects that carry the book’s meaning.

Philip’s holly withers on Redlaw’s wall as the Phantom appears, and the old man later nibbles at a sprig with no memory of why it matters. The founder’s portrait, with its scroll, presides over the final dinner.

Lord! keep my memory green!

Chapter I

Narrative restraint

The narrator speaking in his own voice to the reader.

The narrator opens by doubting what everybody says and closes by listing what people have said since about the story, before adding I say nothing, and then saying one thing more.

Everybody said he looked like a haunted man.

Chapter I

Balanced opposites

Setting opposed ideas against each other in parallel phrasing.

The Phantom’s verdict on the boy and Redlaw rests on two matched clauses, and Mrs Tetterby answers her husband by taking each of her unkind thoughts about him and finding in it a reason to love him.

He is the growth of man’s indifference; you are the growth of man’s presumption.

Chapter III

Irony

A gap between what is said or intended and what turns out to be true.

The gift is offered as a blessing and works as a curse, George’s defiance is the sound of his repentance being taken away, and the learned man learns his lesson from a woman who says she does not know how to think properly.

I have lived bold, and I mean to die bold.
