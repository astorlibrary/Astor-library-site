Heart of Darkness · Joseph Conrad

What happens

Part I, opening On the Thames

Five old friends wait on the yawl Nellie for the tide to turn below Gravesend. As the sun sets over London, Marlow says that this too has been one of the dark places of the earth, and imagines a Roman commander sent up the Thames nineteen hundred years ago. Conquest, he says, is robbery with violence, redeemed only by the idea behind it. Then he begins the story of the time he turned fresh-water sailor.

Part I
The Director of Companies, the Lawyer, the Accountant, the first narrator, and Marlow sitting like an idol.
Part I
A trireme on the Thames, the fascination of the abomination, and the idea that redeems conquest.

Part I The Company

As a boy Marlow put his finger on the blank spaces of the map. Now the blankest has become a place of darkness, with a river like a snake. His aunt gets him the command of a steamer whose last captain was killed in a quarrel over two hens. In a city like a whited sepulchre he signs his contract, passes two women knitting black wool, and has his skull measured by the Company doctor. A French steamer carries him down the coast, past a man-of-war firing into the continent.

Part I
A blank space turned dark, Fresleven and the hens, and a lady who talks of weaning the ignorant millions.
Part I
The knitters guarding the door of Darkness, forty-five seconds with the great man, and a doctor with calipers.
Part I
Soldiers and custom-house clerks landed in the surf, and a warship shelling the bush.

Part I The Company’s stations

At the Company’s station Marlow sees a chain gang, pointless blasting, and a grove where worn-out labourers have crawled away to die. The immaculate chief accountant first mentions Mr Kurtz. After a two-hundred-mile march Marlow reaches the Central Station, where the manager has wrecked his steamer. For months he waits for rivets that never come, while the agents intrigue for posts where ivory is to be had, the brickmaker pumps him for news of Europe, and the Eldorado Exploring Expedition passes through on its way to plunder.

Part I
A chain gang, a hole with no purpose, and a boy with a bit of white worsted round his neck.
Part I
Starched collars, correct entries, and the first mention of Mr Kurtz.
Part I
A sunken steamer, a manager who is never ill, a brickmaker with no bricks, and no rivets.

Part II Up the river

Lying on his steamer, Marlow overhears the manager and his uncle hoping the climate will do away with Kurtz. The journey upriver takes two months, with a crew of cannibals and a few pilgrims with their staves. Fifty miles below the Inner Station Marlow finds a woodpile, a warning and an old book on seamanship annotated in what looks like cipher. Two hours after a morning fog lifts, the steamer is attacked from the bank; the helmsman is killed by a spear, and Marlow throws his bloodied shoes overboard. Here he breaks off to tell his listeners what he later learned of Kurtz: the ivory, the Intended, and the report that ends Exterminate all the brutes!

Part II
A conversation overheard, and a short arm flung out towards the forest.
Part II
The woodpile, the warning, and a book that feels like something unmistakably real.
Part II
Fog, restraint among the starving crew, sticks that are arrows, and the helmsman’s last look.
Part II
The report for the International Society for the Suppression of Savage Customs and its postscript.

Parts II–III The Inner Station

A young Russian in patched clothes greets the steamer and talks of Kurtz as a man who has enlarged his mind. Through his glasses Marlow sees that the knobs on the fence posts are human heads. Kurtz is carried down on a stretcher, and a magnificent woman walks along the shore and raises her arms to the sky. The manager calls Kurtz’s method unsound; the Russian confesses that Kurtz ordered the attack. That night Kurtz crawls off towards the fires in the forest, and Marlow goes after him alone and brings him back.

Part II
A Russian in motley, Towson’s book returned, and a man who must be listened to.
Part III
Round knobs that are symbolic, a man hollow at the core, and rebels.
Part III
Kurtz carried down, the woman on the shore, and a choice of nightmares.
Part III
A trail through wet grass, a horned sorcerer by the fire, and a soul that has gone mad.

Part III Downriver

The steamer leaves at noon while the crowd on the shore calls after Kurtz; Marlow scatters them with the steam-whistle, though the pilgrims fire into the bush anyway. Kurtz talks of his Intended, his station and his career, gives Marlow a packet of papers and a photograph, and one night, lying in the dark waiting for death, cries out twice, The horror! The horror! The manager’s boy announces his death at dinner. Marlow falls ill and nearly dies, and concludes that Kurtz’s cry was a moral victory.

Part III
Three men painted red, a woman stretching out her arms, and the steam-whistle.
Part III
Papers and a photograph, a candle, and a cry that was no more than a breath.
Part III
An impalpable greyness, and why Kurtz was a remarkable man.

Part III, ending The sepulchral city

Back in Europe, Marlow resents the people in the streets. A Company man demands Kurtz’s papers, a cousin says Kurtz was a great musician, and a journalist says he would have led an extreme party. More than a year after the death, Marlow visits Kurtz’s Intended, still in mourning, and when she begs for his last words he tells her it was her name. On the Nellie the tide has turned, and the waterway seems to lead into the heart of an immense darkness.

Part III
The report without its postscript, a cousin, a journalist, and a portrait.
Part III
A drawing-room at dusk, a woman in black, and a lie.
Part III
Marlow in the pose of a meditating Buddha, and a black bank of clouds.
