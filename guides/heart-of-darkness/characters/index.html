Heart of Darkness · Joseph Conrad

Characters

Marlow

A seaman, telling the story aboard the Nellie; first appears Part I

Charlie Marlow is an English merchant seaman, restless between voyages, who gets command of a river steamer through an aunt’s connections. He is ironic, practical and full of contempt for the agents he meets, and he holds on to work, rivets and the surface-truth of steering as a defence against what he sees. He says he hates lies, yet he lets the brickmaker believe he has influence and ends by lying to Kurtz’s Intended. Telling the story later, he admits that it is not very clear to him even now.

Kurtz

Chief of the Inner Station, the Company’s most successful agent; first appears Part I (by name); Part III (in person)

Kurtz went out with moral ideas and a gift for eloquence: a painter, a writer for the papers, a musician, according to whom one asks, and the author of a report on the suppression of savage customs. His mother was half English and his father half French, and he is a man to whom all Europe contributed. At the Inner Station he raids the country for ivory, is worshipped by the people of the lake, and decorates his fence with heads. Dying, he talks of his Intended and his career, and at the end pronounces a judgement: The horror! The horror!

The manager

General manager of the Central Station; first appears Part I

Commonplace in complexion, feature and voice, he has risen because he is never ill. He inspires uneasiness, seals his sentences with a smile that gives nothing away, and says that men who come out should have no entrails. Marlow’s steamer was sunk on a trip he ordered, perhaps on purpose, and he delays the relief of the Inner Station, and hopes with his uncle that the climate will remove Kurtz. When Kurtz is found, he objects only that his method is unsound.

The brickmaker

An agent at the Central Station, said to be the manager’s spy; first appears Part I

Young, gentlemanly and stand-offish, with a forked beard and mica eyes, he is supposed to make bricks but has made none in a year. He keeps a whole candle to himself, pumps Marlow for news of his connections in Europe, and assumes Marlow belongs to the new gang of virtue that sent Kurtz. Marlow calls him a papier-mâché Mephistopheles, with nothing inside but a little loose dirt.

The chief accountant

The Company’s book-keeper at its coastal station; first appears Part I

In starched collars, white cuffs and varnished boots, he keeps his books in apple-pie order a few yards from the grove where labourers lie dying. Marlow respects his backbone, the achievement of looking correct in the middle of chaos. It is from him that Marlow first hears the name of Kurtz, whom he predicts will be a somebody in the Administration.

The helmsman

Marlow’s steersman on the river; first appears Part II

A man from the coast, trained by Marlow’s predecessor, who wears brass earrings and a blue wrapper and steers with a swagger while he is watched. When the steamer is attacked he drops the wheel, throws open the shutter and fires into the bush, and a spear kills him. His last look at Marlow, like a claim of distant kinship, stays in Marlow’s memory to the day he tells the story.

The Russian

A young wanderer in patched clothes, Kurtz’s disciple; first appears Part II

The son of an arch-priest from Tambov, twenty-five, who ran away to sea and has wandered alone on the river for two years. Dressed in patches like a harlequin, he is all glamour and audacity, and his devotion to Kurtz is total: Kurtz has enlarged his mind, talked to him all night of everything, and once threatened to shoot him for his ivory. He leaves the Inner Station at night with Marlow’s cartridges, an old pair of shoes and Towson’s book.

The African woman

A woman at the Inner Station, apparently Kurtz’s companion; first appears Part III

She walks along the shore in fringed cloths, brass leggings and glass beads worth several elephant tusks, faces the steamer, and raises her arms to the sky. The Russian says she argued with Kurtz and that he risked his life to keep her out of the house. As the steamer leaves she stretches her arms after it. She never speaks a word Marlow can understand, and Marlow sees her again in the Intended’s gesture.

The Intended

Kurtz’s fiancée in Europe; first appears Part II (mentioned); Part III

A fair-haired woman, no longer girlish, whose family disapproved of the engagement because Kurtz was poor. More than a year after his death she is still in black, sure that she knew him best and that his goodness shone in every act. She asks Marlow for his last words so that she will have something to live with, and Marlow gives her a lie she can keep.

Marlow’s aunt

His well-connected relation on the Continent; first appears Part I

A dear enthusiastic soul who knows the wife of a high personage in the Administration and gets Marlow his appointment. She believes the Company’s talk of civilising mission and speaks of weaning the ignorant millions from their horrid ways, until Marlow ventures that the Company is run for profit. After his return she tries to nurse up his strength.

The Company doctor

The doctor who examines men going out; first appears Part I

An unshaven little man in slippers who measures the skulls of those going out, in the interests of science, and asks whether there is madness in the family. He never sees them come back, and in any case, he says, the changes take place inside. Marlow remembers him on the march when his companion faints.

The first narrator

An unnamed listener aboard the Nellie; first appears Part I

One of four friends of the Director of Companies, all men bound by the sea, who hears Marlow’s story on the Nellie and reports it. He opens with the Thames as the river of Drake and Franklin and the germs of empires, stays awake while the others may be asleep, and ends by seeing the same river lead into the heart of an immense darkness.

The manager’s boy

The manager’s servant; first appears Part I

An overfed young man from the coast whom the manager allows to treat the white agents with insolence. He announces Kurtz’s death at dinner, putting his head round the door, in a tone of scathing contempt.
