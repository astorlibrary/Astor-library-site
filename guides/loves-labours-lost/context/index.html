Love’s Labour’s Lost · William Shakespeare

Context

Dates

1578
A French embassy visits the real Navarre Catherine de’ Medici and her daughter Marguerite de Valois travelled to Henri of Navarre’s court at Nérac with a train of ladies, to settle the terms of Marguerite’s dowry and the disputed lands of the south-west. The play’s situation is this visit with the politics kept and the outcome changed.
1591
English troops fight beside Navarre’s commanders Essex took an English force to Normandy in support of Henri of Navarre. The Duc de Biron, the Duc de Longueville and the Duc de Mayenne were named in the newsbooks that came back, and Shakespeare gave three of them to the lords of the academy.
1595
The play written The rhyming, the sonnets set into the dialogue and the density of the wordplay place it beside A Midsummer Night’s Dream and Romeo and Juliet in the middle of the 1590s. No document records a first performance.
1597
Presented before the Queen at Christmas The title page of the following year’s quarto says the comedy was presented before her Highness the last Christmas, which puts a court performance in the holiday season of 1597 and 1598.
1598
The first quarto printed William White printed it for the bookseller Cuthbert Burby, describing it as newly corrected and augmented, with Shakespeare’s name on the title page. It is the only substantive early text: the Folio was set from a copy of it.
1598
Francis Meres names it Palladis Tamia lists Love’s Labour’s Lost among the comedies that show Shakespeare most excellent, alongside a title, Love’s Labour’s Won, that nobody has since identified.
1598
Robert Tofte reports seeing it played The poem Alba: The Months Minde of a Melancholy Lover describes watching Love’s Labour’s Lost and finding the comedy painful to sit through as a man in love. It is the earliest response to a performance by anyone in the audience.
1605
Revived for Queen Anne Sir Walter Cope wrote to Robert Cecil in January that Burbage knew of no new play the Queen had not seen, but that the company had revived an old one called Love’s Labour’s Lost, which for wit and mirth he thought would please her exceedingly.
1623
Printed in the First Folio Heminges and Condell placed it among the comedies, in a text set from the 1598 quarto. The Folio keeps the quarto’s duplicated passages in Act IV and Act V, where an earlier and a revised version of the same speech both stand.
1631
A second quarto John Smethwick published a fresh quarto, printed from the Folio text. After this the play disappears from the record of performance for two hundred years.
1839
Madame Vestris revives it at Covent Garden The first recorded professional production since the reign of James I. It ran briefly and was not much liked, and the comedy kept its reputation as an early experiment too clever to play.
1946
Peter Brook’s production at Stratford Brook set the play in the world of Watteau, with Paul Scofield as Armado, and made Marcadé’s entrance the hinge of the evening. The production is generally credited with returning the comedy to the regular repertory.

Places

Nérac
Henri of Navarre’s court, where a French queen and her ladies came to negotiate in 1578 and were entertained in the park.
Pamplona
The old capital of Navarre, the kingdom the King rules and proposes to make the wonder of the world.
Bordeaux
In Aquitaine, the province held in surety for a hundred thousand crowns and argued over in Act II.
Whitehall Palace, London
Where a court performance at Christmas 1597 is most likely to have taken place, as the 1598 title page reports.
Cornhill, London
Cuthbert Burby kept his shop here and sold the first quarto in 1598.
Stratford-upon-Avon
Peter Brook’s 1946 production here brought the comedy back into the repertory after a century of neglect.

Critics

Walter Pater

“Love’s Labours Lost”, in Appreciations (1889)

Pater read the comedy as a play about fashions of speech, the euphuism and wordplay of the 1590s, written by a young dramatist who loved those fashions while he laughed at them.
