Love’s Labour’s Lost · William Shakespeare

Characters

Berowne

The lord who argues against the oath, signs it, and breaks it first; first appears 1.1

He sees through the academy in Act I, lists everything wrong with it, and puts his name to the schedule anyway. His wit is the fastest on stage and he uses it on everyone, including himself, which is why the play gives him the longest speech in its defence of love and then makes him renounce that style completely in Act V.

Rosaline

The lady who matches Berowne line for line and sets his penance; first appears 2.1

She beats him in their first exchange in Act II before either has been introduced, leads the counter-masque in Act V, and ends by sending him to a hospital for a year to make the dying laugh. She is the one character who says plainly what a jest is worth and who decides it, and her judgement closes the play’s argument about wit.

The King of Navarre

Founder of the academy, also called Ferdinand; first appears 1.1

He writes a three-year statute, suspends it within an hour for a visiting princess, and is the first of the four to be overheard reading a love poem. He wants Navarre to be the wonder of the world and keeps choosing the version of the rule that suits the moment. At the end he is sent to a hermitage to live the withdrawal he had planned as a route to fame.

The Princess of France

Ambassador for her father, and queen before the play ends; first appears 2.1

She comes to recover Aquitaine and a hundred thousand crowns, is refused entry, and conducts the business from a tent. She refuses Boyet’s flattery in her first speech and knows exactly what her own deer-shooting is worth. When Marcadé arrives she becomes a queen in mourning, and she is the one who fixes the terms on which any of the four courtships may continue.

Boyet

The lord attending the Princess, and the play’s best reader of faces; first appears 2.1

He flatters in his first speech and is told to stop. Afterwards he reads Navarre’s infatuation off the King’s own eyes and reports it, overhears the Muscovite rehearsal from under a sycamore and warns the women, then heckles the Nine Worthies. Berowne calls him a pedlar of wit and blames him for wrecking the masque, which is exactly what he did.

Don Adriano de Armado

A Spanish gentleman kept at court for entertainment; first appears 1.2

He reports Costard for being taken with Jaquenetta and is in love with her himself by the end of the scene. His prose is the most elaborate in the play, built of clauses that make a walk in the park sound like a military dispatch. By Act V he has no shirt, a challenge on his hands, and a vow to hold the plough for Jaquenetta for three years.

Moth

Armado’s page, and the fastest wit in the subplot; first appears 1.2

He takes his master apart in every exchange they have, proves that three years of study can be done in two words, and is the one who says what the learned men are doing with language when he calls it a feast whose scraps they have stolen. Cast as Hercules in minority, he is sent on to strangle a snake and sent off again before he can speak.

Costard

A countryman arrested in the first scene for keeping company with Jaquenetta; first appears 1.1

He is the first person punished under the new statute and the only character employed by both plots, carrying Armado’s letter and Berowne’s poem and mixing them up. He treats every word he is given as a coin to be valued, and in the last scene he is the one who tells the court that Jaquenetta is pregnant, which stops the pageant dead.

Holofernes

A schoolmaster who corrects everyone’s Latin; first appears 4.2

He describes a dead deer in Latin and English at once, writes a poem that plays on the letter L, and objects to people who say ‘dout’ without the b. He is ridiculous, and he is also the only man in the play who reads Berowne’s sonnet properly and sends it where it belongs. The lords bait him out of the pageant, and his exit line is the plainest complaint in the play.

Jaquenetta

A countrywoman, and the cause of the first arrest; first appears 1.2

She has fewer than twenty lines and sets most of the plot moving. Costard is jailed for being seen with her, Armado falls for her in one exchange of six short answers, and she carries Berowne’s misdirected sonnet to the schoolmaster, who sends it to the King. In the last scene Costard announces she is two months pregnant, and Armado vows three years at the plough.
