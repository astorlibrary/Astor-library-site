Love’s Labour’s Lost · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Punning

Playing on a word’s second meaning so that both senses are live together.

Light runs through Act V as brightness, weight and loose behaviour in the same exchange between Rosaline and Katharine. Holofernes builds a whole poem out of adding an L to sore. The play is thick with puns, and most of them are in the mouths of people trying to win.

Did not I dance with you in Brabant once?

Rosaline, 2.1.114

Sonnets inside the play

A complete fourteen-line poem set into the dialogue and spoken as a written object.

Four of them are read aloud: Berowne’s to Rosaline in Act IV Scene 2, then the King’s, Longaville’s and Dumaine’s in the overhearing scene. Each is a competent example of the fashion. Hearing four in a row makes the fashion audible, and the feeling underneath it much harder to hear.

Assist me, some extemporal god of rhyme, for I am sure I shall turn sonnet.

Don Adriano de Armado, 1.2.149

The two songs

A sung passage set outside the dialogue, free to say what the speaking characters cannot.

Spring and Winter close the play with cuckoo-buds, ploughmen’s clocks, frozen milk and a maid keeling a pot. Nothing in either song is courtly, and after five acts of taffeta phrases they are the plainest speech the audience hears from the stage.

When icicles hang by the wall, And Dick the shepherd blows his nail

Winter, 5.2.895

The subplot

A second story running beside the first, repeating its problem in another key.

Armado, Moth, Costard, Jaquenetta, Holofernes and Nathaniel are doing what the lords are doing: writing letters, showing off learning, staging entertainments, falling in love against orders. The lords laugh at them for it in Act V, not long after being caught doing the same things themselves.

I’ll lay my head to any good man’s hat These oaths and laws will prove an idle scorn.

Berowne, 1.1.275

The Muscovite masque

Characters concealing their identity, so that the audience knows more than the people on stage do.

The men arrive in Russian habit with a rehearsed speech. The women mask too and swap the gifts each man sent, so that every whispered vow lands on the wrong ear. The disguise fails twice over: the women know who is under it, and the men do not know who they are talking to.

The overhearing scene

A self-contained episode built to a single effect and complete in itself.

Act IV Scene 3 stacks four men in one park. Berowne hides, the King comes in and reads, the King hides, Longaville comes in and reads, and so on, until Berowne steps out to whip hypocrisy and is exposed by his own torn letter. The structure is a machine, and every man in it thinks he is the audience.

Let fame, that all hunt after in their lives, Live registered upon our brazen tombs

King of Navarre, 1.1.1

Idiolect

A speech habit belonging to one character alone, so that they can be recognised before they are named.

Armado writes in nested clauses that postpone the verb. Holofernes doubles every English word with its Latin twin. Costard hears words as coins. Moth answers in two-beat replies. Boyet can read a man’s face out loud. Few speeches in the play need their speech prefix.

Navarre shall be the wonder of the world; Our court shall be a little academe

King of Navarre, 1.1.12
