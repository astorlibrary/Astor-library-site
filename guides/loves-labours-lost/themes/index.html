Love’s Labour’s Lost · William Shakespeare

Themes

Words and value

Everyone in the play treats words as things they own and can spend. Costard prices remuneration at three farthings and finds guerdon elevenpence farthing better. Holofernes hoards Latin, Armado hoards clauses, Nathaniel copies good phrases into a notebook, and Moth says the learned men have been at a feast of languages and stolen the scraps.

In Act I the men sign away three years for the fame of having studied. By Act V Berowne swears off taffeta phrases and promises russet yeas and honest kersey noes, and the last thing the play offers is two songs about cuckoos, frozen milk and a greasy kitchen maid.

Assist me, some extemporal god of rhyme, for I am sure I shall turn sonnet.

Don Adriano de Armado, 1.2.149

Beauty is bought by judgement of the eye, Not uttered by base sale of chapmen’s tongues.

Princess of France, 2.1.15

Persuasion and proof

Berowne can argue any case. He argues against study and signs the schedule, argues that necessity excuses perjury, then argues in some seventy lines that love is the only real learning and that breaking the oath is a religious duty. Nobody on stage answers the argument; the women simply decline to act on it.

The men’s case gets better and better and persuades nobody who matters. The Princess answers it once, in Act V, when the King asks for her love at the latest minute of the hour: the time, she says, is too short to make a world-without-end bargain in, and the argument stops there.

Small have continual plodders ever won, Save base authority from others’ books.

Berowne, 1.1.86

Necessity will make us all forsworn Three thousand times within this three years’ space

Berowne, 1.1.146

Study

The academy is founded to make Navarre the wonder of the world, with fame as the stated prize. Berowne’s objection in Act I is that plodders win nothing except the right to quote other men’s books, and that giving a name to every fixed star is not the same as knowing anything.

Berowne replaces the library with women’s eyes and calls them the books, the arts, the academes. The ending replaces both: a hermitage for the King, a hospital ward for Berowne, and a year’s wait for men who wanted three years of reading.

Let fame, that all hunt after in their lives, Live registered upon our brazen tombs

King of Navarre, 1.1.1

Navarre shall be the wonder of the world; Our court shall be a little academe

King of Navarre, 1.1.12

Courtship

Each of the four men writes a love poem and is found out: three are read aloud by their authors and overheard, and Berowne’s is read by a curate to a country wench. Then all four put on Russian costume and send a boy ahead with a prepared speech. The women watch the rehearsal reported by Boyet before they see the performance, and they know it is a performance before a word is spoken.

The masque fails because the women swap favours and each man courts the wrong woman, proving that the show never depended on who was inside it. When the men return as themselves the Princess asks for confession, and after Marcadé the courtship games stop for good.

Navarre shall be the wonder of the world; Our court shall be a little academe

King of Navarre, 1.1.12

Why, all his behaviours did make their retire To the court of his eye, peeping thorough desire.

Boyet, 2.1.230

Judgement

The women are lodged in a field and have the better position from the start. The Princess refuses Boyet’s flattery in her first speech, catches the King out on the Aquitaine accounts, and directs the counter-masque. Rosaline names what a jest is worth and to whom, and sets the terms Berowne must meet.

In Act IV the women are the objects of four love poems. In Act V they are the ones handing out sentences: a hermitage, a hospital, a twelvemonth and a day. The play’s last judgement on every male performance in it is delivered by the four people it was aimed at.

Beauty is bought by judgement of the eye, Not uttered by base sale of chapmen’s tongues.

Princess of France, 2.1.15

’Tis deadly sin to keep that oath, my lord, And sin to break it.

Princess of France, 2.1.105

The oath and desire

The statute forbids women, and desire is already inside the gates when it is signed. Costard is arrested in the first scene for being taken with Jaquenetta; Armado, who reported him, is in love with her himself by the end of the second; and Berowne is writing sonnets before the third act is over.

Desire starts as a legal offence with a penalty attached and ends as a pregnancy announced in the middle of a pageant. Armado’s vow to hold the plough for Jaquenetta for three years is the only promise in the play that anybody is actually keeping when the curtain falls.

Necessity will make us all forsworn Three thousand times within this three years’ space

Berowne, 1.1.146

I’ll lay my head to any good man’s hat These oaths and laws will prove an idle scorn.

Berowne, 1.1.275

Time and measurement

The play measures everything. Three years of study, a hundred thousand crowns, a year’s imprisonment for being taken with a wench, three farthings for a word, love asked for at the latest minute of the hour. The Princess is the one who notices that the courtships have had no time in them at all, and who supplies the missing year.

The men set a three-year term in Act I and abandon it in Act IV. The women set a twelve-month term in Act V and leave to keep it. Berowne says a twelvemonth is too long for a play, which is the only complaint about the ending the play does not answer.

Let fame, that all hunt after in their lives, Live registered upon our brazen tombs

King of Navarre, 1.1.1

Let us once lose our oaths to find ourselves, Or else we lose ourselves to keep our oaths.

Berowne, 4.3.359
