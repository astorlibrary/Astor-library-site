Words and value

Everyone in the play treats words as things they own and can spend. Costard prices remuneration at three farthings and finds guerdon elevenpence farthing better. Holofernes hoards Latin, Armado hoards clauses, Nathaniel copies good phrases into a notebook, and Moth says the learned men have been at a feast of languages and stolen the scraps.

Through the play In Act I the men sign away three years for the fame of having studied. By Act V Berowne swears off taffeta phrases and promises russet yeas and honest kersey noes, and the last thing the play offers is two songs about cuckoos, frozen milk and a greasy kitchen maid.

In the text

Assist me, some extemporal god of rhyme, for I am sure I shall turn sonnet.

Don Adriano de Armado, 1.2.149

‘Turn sonnet’, as a man might turn Turk or turn soldier: love makes a literary form of him. Armado gets there three acts before the lords do, and his letter to Jaquenetta arrives in the Princess’s hands in Act IV while Berowne’s reaches a schoolmaster. The subplot is ahead of the main plot.

They have been at a great feast of languages and stolen the scraps.

Moth, 5.1.32

A page says in one sentence what the play takes five acts to demonstrate. The insult is precise: the scraps are real food, taken from a real feast, but nobody at this table cooked anything. Costard carries it on straight away, saying they have lived long on the alms-basket of words, which makes the learned men beggars living on other people’s leftovers.

Persuasion and proof

Berowne can argue any case. He argues against study and signs the schedule, argues that necessity excuses perjury, then argues in some seventy lines that love is the only real learning and that breaking the oath is a religious duty. Nobody on stage answers the argument; the women simply decline to act on it.

Through the play The men’s case gets better and better and persuades nobody who matters. The Princess answers it once, in Act V, when the King asks for her love at the latest minute of the hour: the time, she says, is too short to make a world-without-end bargain in, and the argument stops there.

In the text

Necessity will make us all forsworn Three thousand times within this three years’ space

Berowne, 1.1.146

Berowne signs and predicts the failure in the same breath, which makes him honest and useless. Three thousand is a joke and also roughly right: every man on stage is forsworn within three acts. The play is more interested in what he does after being proved correct than in the prediction, and what he does is write a sonnet.

’Tis deadly sin to keep that oath, my lord, And sin to break it.

Princess of France, 2.1.105

She puts the men in a position they never work out of: the oath is wrong either way, so nothing they do about it can be virtuous. Berowne spends a hundred lines in Act IV arguing that breaking it is a religious duty, which is only half of what she said here, and she never grants him the other half.

Study

The academy is founded to make Navarre the wonder of the world, with fame as the stated prize. Berowne’s objection in Act I is that plodders win nothing except the right to quote other men’s books, and that giving a name to every fixed star is not the same as knowing anything.

Through the play Berowne replaces the library with women’s eyes and calls them the books, the arts, the academes. The ending replaces both: a hermitage for the King, a hospital ward for Berowne, and a year’s wait for men who wanted three years of reading.

In the text

Let fame, that all hunt after in their lives, Live registered upon our brazen tombs

King of Navarre, 1.1.1

The academy is founded on an epitaph. Before any rule is read out, the King has imagined the tomb the studying is for, and the study itself is only the means. Berowne will spend eighty lines attacking exactly this, and the Princess will arrive in Act V in mourning for a father whose death makes all brazen tombs look different.

From women’s eyes this doctrine I derive. They sparkle still the right Promethean fire; They are the books, the arts, the academes, That show, contain, and nourish, all the world

Berowne, 4.3.348

The academy is moved elsewhere, with women installed where the library used to be. It is a magnificent argument, and it leaves the men exactly where they started, treating women as the means to something else. The Princess answers it in Act V by asking for a year, and none of these hundred lines is any use to Berowne then.

Courtship

Each of the four men writes a love poem and is found out: three are read aloud by their authors and overheard, and Berowne’s is read by a curate to a country wench. Then all four put on Russian costume and send a boy ahead with a prepared speech. The women watch the rehearsal reported by Boyet before they see the performance, and they know it is a performance before a word is spoken.

Through the play The masque fails because the women swap favours and each man courts the wrong woman, proving that the show never depended on who was inside it. When the men return as themselves the Princess asks for confession, and after Marcadé the courtship games stop for good.

In the text

Why, all his behaviours did make their retire To the court of his eye, peeping thorough desire.

Boyet, 2.1.230

Boyet reads a face the way the lords read books, and he is right. The pun on court makes the point: the King’s whole household has withdrawn into one organ, so the academy has already shrunk to the size of a man looking at a woman. Berowne will build his Act IV defence of love out of eyes, and Boyet has got there first.

Glory grows guilty of detested crimes, When, for fame’s sake, for praise, an outward part, We bend to that the working of the heart

Princess of France, 4.1.31

She is describing herself: she will shoot for the credit of shooting well. The lines put a name to what the King did in the play’s first speech, hunting fame and bending the heart to it, and they come from the only character who admits to doing it. Four scenes later the deer is dead and Holofernes has written a poem about it.

Judgement

The women are lodged in a field and have the better position from the start. The Princess refuses Boyet’s flattery in her first speech, catches the King out on the Aquitaine accounts, and directs the counter-masque. Rosaline names what a jest is worth and to whom, and sets the terms Berowne must meet.

Through the play In Act IV the women are the objects of four love poems. In Act V they are the ones handing out sentences: a hermitage, a hospital, a twelvemonth and a day. The play’s last judgement on every male performance in it is delivered by the four people it was aimed at.

In the text

Beauty is bought by judgement of the eye, Not uttered by base sale of chapmen’s tongues.

Princess of France, 2.1.15

She rejects the currency the play runs on before she has been on stage twenty lines, and does it in the language of trade: beauty is not stock to be talked up by a pedlar. Everything the men do afterwards, from sonnets to the Muscovite masque, is base sale of chapmen’s tongues, and she has already declined to buy.

That he should be my fool, and I his fate.

Rosaline, 5.2.69

Said as a fantasy of revenge, and by the end of the scene it has come true in a form she has not imagined: she does become his fate, and the sentence she passes is a year among the dying. The line is worth setting beside her last speech, where the same wit that plans this torture is used to explain what a jest costs.

The oath and desire

The statute forbids women, and desire is already inside the gates when it is signed. Costard is arrested in the first scene for being taken with Jaquenetta; Armado, who reported him, is in love with her himself by the end of the second; and Berowne is writing sonnets before the third act is over.

Through the play Desire starts as a legal offence with a penalty attached and ends as a pregnancy announced in the middle of a pageant. Armado’s vow to hold the plough for Jaquenetta for three years is the only promise in the play that anybody is actually keeping when the curtain falls.

In the text

I’ll lay my head to any good man’s hat These oaths and laws will prove an idle scorn.

Berowne, 1.1.275

The bet is laid in the same scene as the law, and Berowne wins it before the interval. The placement lifts the line above a joke: he says it while escorting the first man punished under the statute, so the audience hears the law’s collapse predicted over the head of the one person it has actually cost anything.

This wimpled, whining, purblind, wayward boy, This Signior Junior, giant-dwarf, Dan Cupid

Berowne, 3.1.156

Four adjectives and then a title that contradicts itself twice over, junior and signior, giant and dwarf. Berowne abuses Cupid in the most elaborate style he has, which is the joke: the man complaining about love-rhymes is composing one. The speech ends with him agreeing to love, write, sigh, pray, sue and groan, in that order.

Time and measurement

The play measures everything. Three years of study, a hundred thousand crowns, a year’s imprisonment for being taken with a wench, three farthings for a word, love asked for at the latest minute of the hour. The Princess is the one who notices that the courtships have had no time in them at all, and who supplies the missing year.

Through the play The men set a three-year term in Act I and abandon it in Act IV. The women set a twelve-month term in Act V and leave to keep it. Berowne says a twelvemonth is too long for a play, which is the only complaint about the ending the play does not answer.

In the text

Let us once lose our oaths to find ourselves, Or else we lose ourselves to keep our oaths.

Berowne, 4.3.359

The couplet turns on one verb used twice, and the balance is so neat that nobody on stage looks for the hole in it. Berowne is arguing that a promise you regret was never binding, which is what he told the King in Act I as a joke. The women’s reply is to give the four men a promise with a year attached.

I am sorry, madam, for the news I bring Is heavy in my tongue. The King your father—

Marcadé, 5.2.703

He never finishes the sentence; the Princess does it for him. Two and a half lines of plain statement stop a scene that has run nearly seven hundred, and Berowne’s response is the stage direction in words: worthies away, the scene begins to cloud. Nothing said after this has the same footing as anything said before it.
