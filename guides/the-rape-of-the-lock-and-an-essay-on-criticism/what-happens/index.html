The Rape of the Lock and An Essay on Criticism · Alexander Pope

What happens

Canto I The dream and the dressing table

The poet announces his subject, the dire offence that rose from amorous causes, and asks the Muse what could make a well-bred lord assault a gentle belle. At noon Belinda is still asleep, and her guardian sylph, Ariel, sends her a dream. A glittering youth tells her that thousands of spirits of the air protect her, explains that the souls of dead coquettes become sylphs and those of prudes become gnomes, and warns her of some dreadful event that day. Her lapdog Shock licks her awake, and a love letter drives the vision from her head. At her dressing table she worships her own reflection while the sylphs help her maid, Betty, to arm her for the day.

Canto I, 1–12
Mighty contests from trivial things, and a question for the Muse.
Canto I, 13–114
A morning dream of sylphs, gnomes and the afterlife of coquettes, ending with a warning against Man.
Canto I, 115–120
Shock wakes his mistress, and a love letter chases the dream away.
Canto I, 121–148
Cosmetic powers worshipped at a dressing table laid with the offerings of the world.

Canto II The Thames and the Baron’s altar

Belinda sails up the Thames, as bright as the sun and as impartial with her smiles, wearing a cross that Jews might kiss and infidels adore. Two curls hang down her neck. The Baron has resolved to have one of them, by force or by fraud, and before dawn he built an altar of twelve French romances and burned old love letters on it; the gods granted half his prayer. On the boat Ariel gathers the sylphs, reminds them of their duties to the fair, gives each a charge, and sets fifty of them to guard the petticoat, threatening any deserter with pins, pomatum and the chocolate mill.

Canto II, 1–28
The rival of the sun, her cross and her two locks.
Canto II, 29–46
An altar of French romances and a prayer half granted.
Canto II, 47–142
Sylphs in the rigging, the duties of the airy kind, and a guard for the petticoat.

Canto III Hampton Court, ombre and the scissors

At Hampton Court, where Queen Anne sometimes takes counsel and sometimes tea, the company gossips while judges hurry their sentences so that the jury can dine. Belinda challenges two knights to ombre, the card game is fought as an epic battle, and she wins the last trick. Coffee is served, and its fumes send new stratagems to the Baron’s brain. Clarissa hands him a pair of scissors. The sylphs blow back the hair and tug at Belinda’s earring, but Ariel finds an earthly lover in her thoughts and gives up his guard; a sylph who gets in the way is cut in two, and the lock is severed. Belinda screams, and the Baron boasts that his honour will live as long as ladies pay visits.

Canto III, 1–24
Heroes and nymphs at Hampton Court, gossip, snuff and the hungry judges.
Canto III, 25–104
Kings, queens and knaves in battle on the velvet plain.
Canto III, 105–160
Coffee, Clarissa’s scissors, Ariel’s retreat and a scream.
Canto III, 161–178
Wreaths of triumph and the conquering force of steel.

Canto IV The Cave of Spleen

No captive king or ageing beauty ever felt such rage as Belinda. The gnome Umbriel flies down to the Cave of Spleen, a gloomy grotto where the goddess sighs on her bed between Pain and Megrim, attended by Ill-nature and Affectation, among living teapots, a sighing jar and a talking goose-pie. He asks her to touch Belinda with chagrin, and she gives him a bag of sighs, sobs and quarrels and a vial of fears, sorrows and tears. He empties the bag over Belinda and her friend Thalestris. Thalestris sends her beau, Sir Plume, to demand the lock; he swears at the Baron, who will not give it up. Umbriel breaks the vial, and Belinda weeps and wishes she had never seen Hampton Court.

Canto IV, 1–10
Rage, resentment and despair for a ravished hair.
Canto IV, 11–88
The grotto of Spleen, her handmaids, her phantoms, the bag and the vial.
Canto IV, 89–140
A call to defend Belinda’s honour, and a beau’s oaths.
Canto IV, 141–176
Tears, remembered omens, and the sister-lock that still hangs.

Canto V The battle and the new star

The Baron is unmoved. Clarissa waves her fan for silence and makes a speech that Pope added in 1717: beauty fades and locks turn grey, so good sense and good humour must keep what beauty wins. No one applauds, and Thalestris calls her a prude. The beaux and belles fall on each other in a battle of frowns, fans and snuff, with Umbriel watching from a sconce, and Jove weighs the men’s wits against the lady’s hair. Belinda throws snuff at the Baron’s nose, draws a bodkin that has come down through her family, and demands the lock, but it has vanished. The Muse saw it rise into the heavens as a new star, which will keep Belinda’s name alive when her own beauty is dust.

Canto V, 1–36
Good sense and good humour, added in 1717.
Canto V, 37–74
Beaux die in metaphor and in song, and Jove weighs wits against hair.
Canto V, 75–112
Belinda’s charge, the Baron’s fall, and a lock nowhere to be found.
Canto V, 113–150
The lunar sphere, the Muse’s sight, and a trail of hair across the sky.

An Essay on Criticism, Part I Nature, the rules and the ancients

The Essay begins by weighing bad critics against bad writers and finds the critics more dangerous. True taste is as rare as true genius, though most people have the seeds of judgement until false learning spoils them. A critic must know the limits of his own powers and follow Nature, the universal standard. The rules of the ancients are Nature methodised, discovered in the practice of Homer and the other great poets, and Virgil found that Nature and Homer were the same. Some beauties lie beyond any rule, and great poets may take a lucky licence. Part I ends with praise of the ancient bards and a prayer that some spark of their fire may inspire the last and meanest of their sons.

Essay, 1–67
Judging ill is worse than writing ill; taste, false learning and the limits of wit.
Essay, 68–140
Nature as the standard, the rules as Nature methodised, and Virgil’s discovery.
Essay, 141–200
A grace beyond the reach of art, and a hymn to the ancient bards.

An Essay on Criticism, Part II Why critics judge badly

Part II sets out the causes that hinder true judgement. The first is pride, the second a little learning. Critics judge by parts and miss the whole, and fix on conceits, on language or on the smoothness of the metre; here Pope makes his lines imitate the faults and virtues they describe, so that ‘the sound must seem an echo to the sense’. Other critics are too hard to please or too quick to admire, partial to the ancients or the moderns, swayed by fashion, by a lord’s name, by party or by envy. Towards the end comes a plea for good nature (‘to err is human, to forgive, divine’) and a direction for the critic’s anger: obscenity and blasphemy deserve it.

Essay, 201–232
The never-failing vice of fools, and the Alps of learning.
Essay, 233–383
Conceit, language and numbers, and the sound that echoes the sense.
Essay, 384–507
Too hard to please, too apt to admire, following the town, the lord and the party.
Essay, 508–559
To err is human, and the crimes that deserve a critic’s rage.

An Essay on Criticism, Part III The good critic

Part III turns from judgement to manners. The critic should be candid and modest, own his errors, give advice without arrogance, and know when to stay silent, since some bad poets cannot be mended and some critics, like the bookful blockhead, will talk you dead even at the altar. Pope then asks where the ideal critic is to be found, and answers with a history of criticism: Aristotle, Horace, Dionysius, Petronius, Quintilian and Longinus; the fall of learning with Rome and its revival under Erasmus and Pope Leo X; Vida in Italy, Boileau in France, and in England Roscommon and William Walsh, the friend who had taught Pope’s Muse to sing. The Essay closes with a portrait of that Muse.

Essay, 560–630
Candour, good breeding, silence, and the bookful blockhead.
Essay, 631–680
Where is the man? Aristotle, Horace, Quintilian and Longinus.
Essay, 681–744
Monks and Goths, Erasmus, Vida, Boileau, Roscommon and Walsh.
