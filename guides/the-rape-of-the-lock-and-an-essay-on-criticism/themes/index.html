The trivial and the great

The Rape of the Lock names its subject in the second line: mighty contests rising from trivial things. Pope gives a stolen curl the whole apparatus of epic, with gods, prayers, a council, a battle and a transformation, and the joke cuts two ways. The quarrel looks absurd dressed as the Trojan War, and the grand manner looks a little absurd when it fits a card table so well. Now and then something serious appears among the trifles, the hanging of wretches so that jurymen may dine, and the reader sees how badly this society measures what matters.

Through the book What mighty contests rise from trivial things; When husbands, or when lapdogs breathe their last; the ombre battle; Steel could the labour of the Gods destroy; the lock made a star.

In the text

What dire offence from am’rous causes springs, / What mighty contests rise from trivial things,

Canto I, 1–2

Epic poems begin by announcing their subject, as the Iliad announces the anger of Achilles. Pope announces a quarrel from amorous causes, and his second line admits that the contest is mighty only in its effects. The rhyme of springs and things sets the scale of the whole poem.

Not louder shrieks to pitying heav’n are cast, / When husbands, or when lapdogs breathe their last;

Canto III, 157–158

Belinda’s screams are compared to the shrieks of ladies at the death of husbands or of lapdogs, and the line places the two side by side as if they were equal losses. The next couplet adds broken china. It is the poem’s sharpest line on a society that cannot tell great things from small.

Beauty and display

Belinda’s beauty is her power, and Pope describes its making in the language of religion and war: the dressing table is an altar, her reflection a goddess, her cosmetics her arms. Her cross is an ornament. Clarissa states the lesson that beauty fades and good humour must keep what it wins, and the last lines promise that the lock will outlast the head it grew on.

Through the book The toilet; Puffs, Powders, Patches, Bibles, Billet-doux; the sparkling cross; Fair tresses man’s imperial race ensnare; Clarissa’s speech; the new star.

In the text

Here files of pins extend their shining rows, / Puffs, Powders, Patches, Bibles, Billet-doux.

Canto I, 137–138

Pins stand in files like soldiers, and every item in the second line begins with P or B, so that Bibles become one more cosmetic among puffs and patches. Love letters lie beside scripture with no sense of difference. It is one of the best-known lines of bathos in English.

On her white breast a sparkling Cross she wore, / Which Jews might kiss, and Infidels adore.

Canto II, 7–8

Belinda’s cross is jewellery, admired even by those who reject what it stands for. Pope balances kiss and adore so neatly that the religious sign disappears into its effect on the onlookers. Her faith, like the Bibles on her table, is part of the display.

Honour and reputation

Honour in the Rape means reputation, what the town says about a woman. Ariel tells Belinda that the sylphs keep maids safe, though honour is the word with men below. Thalestris fears for Belinda’s name if the Baron shows off the lock, and at Hampton Court a reputation dies at every word. The Baron wants a trophy that will keep his own honour and name alive. Pope’s zeugmas set honour beside brocade, as if the two could be stained in the same way.

Through the book Tho’ Honour is the word with Men below; stain her honour or her new brocade; At ev’ry word a reputation dies; a degraded toast; the Baron’s boast.

In the text

’Tis but their Sylph, the wise Celestials know, / Tho’ Honour is the word with Men below.

Ariel, Canto I, 77–78

According to Ariel, maids stay chaste because sylphs guard them, whatever men may call it. Honour becomes a word for public use, while the real safeguard is invisible and a little absurd. The couplet is an early sign that the poem treats virtue as a matter of what can be seen.

Or some frail China jar receive a flaw; / Or stain her honour or her new brocade; / Forget her pray’rs, or miss a masquerade; / Or lose her heart, or necklace, at a ball;

Ariel, Canto II, 106–109

Each line sets a serious loss beside a trivial one: honour beside brocade, prayers beside a masquerade, a heart beside a necklace. The zeugma makes it impossible to say which Ariel fears more, and perhaps which Belinda would. Collecting china was a new fashion, brought over from Holland by Queen Mary.

Men and women

The poem is a comedy of courtship in which the sexes are at war: belles armed with eyes and fans, beaux who die in metaphor, a baron who plans to win by force or fraud. Its innuendo never stops, from Ariel’s warning to beware of man to Belinda’s wish that the Baron had seized hairs less in sight. It can be read as an affectionate portrait of women or, starting from the dedication’s remarks about the fair sex, as condescension; the moral is spoken by the woman who lent the Baron her scissors.

Through the book Beware of all, but most beware of Man!; the Baron’s altar; An earthly Lover lurking at her heart; the Cave of Spleen; Hairs less in sight; Clarissa’s speech; the battle of the beaux and belles.

In the text

Beware of all, but most beware of Man!

Ariel, Canto I, 114

Ariel foresees a dreadful event but not its shape, so his warning covers every man Belinda will meet. It sounds like an oracle and works as a joke about chaperones. Belinda forgets it as soon as she wakes to a love letter.

But chiefly Love—to Love an Altar built, / Of twelve vast French Romances, neatly gilt.

Canto II, 37–38

Heroes in epic sacrifice to the gods before battle, and the Baron builds his altar from French romances, the enormous gilt-bound love stories of fashionable reading. He burns the trophies of his former loves on it. The gods grant half his prayer, as Apollo grants half of a warrior’s prayer in the Aeneid: he will win the lock but not keep it.

Fashion and luxury

Queen Anne’s London is present in every canto: the court at Hampton, the queen taking counsel and tea, gossip, snuff, coffee, china, Indian screens, sedan chairs, patch-boxes and the card game of ombre. The treasures on Belinda’s dressing table come from India and Arabia, with combs of tortoiseshell and ivory, and the fashionable world is seen to depend on trade with half the globe. Pope makes these things glitter, and the glitter is part of the satire.

Through the book Here thou, great Anna!; This casket India’s glowing gems unlocks; coffee and china; ombre; Where none learn Ombre, none e’er taste Bohea!

In the text

This casket India’s glowing gems unlocks, / And all Arabia breathes from yonder box.

Canto I, 133–134

Jewels from India and perfume from Arabia arrive at the dressing table as offerings to a goddess. Pope enjoys the richness of the list, and he also shows that a London lady’s morning depends on the trade of half the world. The next couplet adds combs of tortoiseshell and ivory.

Here thou, great Anna! whom three realms obey, / Dost sometimes counsel take—and sometimes Tea.

Canto III, 7–8

Queen Anne receives full ceremony until the last word. The dash before Tea makes the reader wait, and the zeugma sets affairs of state beside a luxury that in 1710 cost from twelve to twenty-eight shillings a pound. Politics and pleasure share one verb.

Nature and the rules

For Pope, Nature is the clear, unchanging, universal order of things that reason recognises, and the rules of poetry are Nature methodised: principles the ancient critics discovered in the practice of the great poets. Following Nature therefore means following the ancients, as Virgil found. Pope also allows a grace beyond the reach of art, a licence that great poets may take when it serves the purpose the rules exist for.

Through the book First follow Nature; Nature methodiz’d; Nature and Homer were, he found, the same; a grace beyond the reach of art; True Wit is Nature to advantage dress’d.

In the text

First follow Nature, and your judgment frame / By her just standard, which is still the same:

Essay, 68–69

Nature here means the order of things as reason understands it, universal and unchanging, the same for every age. Pope makes it a standard, a measure against which judgement is framed. The next lines call Nature the source, the end and the test of art.

True Wit is Nature to advantage dress’d, / What oft was thought, but ne’er so well express’d;

Essay, 297–298

True wit, on this definition, gives a common thought its best expression, so that readers recognise it as true at first sight. Novelty lies in the phrasing. Pope’s couplet proves the point, since the idea was old and his wording made it memorable.

Wit and judgement

Wit in Pope’s time could mean intelligence, invention, a clever turn of phrase or a clever man about town, and the Essay uses the word in several of these senses. Its argument is that wit needs judgement, as a spirited horse needs a rider, and that true wit expresses what oft was thought, but ne’er so well. The word slides between these senses from one couplet to the next. In the Rape the wits are the beaux, and their wits are weighed against a lady’s hair and found lighter.

Through the book For wit and judgment often are at strife; True Wit is Nature to advantage dress’d; Some have at first for Wits, then Poets past; the wits mount up, the hairs subside.

In the text

’Tis with our judgments as our watches, none / Go just alike, yet each believes his own.

Essay, 9–10

Judgements are compared to watches: no two keep the same time, and each owner believes his own. The comparison is homely and exact, and the line break after none makes the reader wait for the verb. It introduces the Essay’s concern with knowing the limits of one’s own taste.

A needless Alexandrine ends the song / That, like a wounded snake, drags its slow length along.

Essay, 356–357

An Alexandrine is a line of six feet in place of the usual five. Pope makes the second line one, loaded with long syllables and heavy stresses, so that it drags along as it describes. He is making fun of poets who end a paragraph with one out of habit.

Pride

Pride is the first cause of bad judgement in the Essay, the never-failing vice of fools, filling up the void where sense is wanting. The good critic is modest, owns his errors and admires superior sense. In the Rape pride is a religion: Belinda’s toilet is the sacred rite of Pride, the Baron boasts that his fame will last for ever, and every character is vain of something, down to Sir Plume and his snuff-box.

Through the book Is Pride, the never-failing vice of fools; A little learning is a dang’rous thing; the sacred rites of Pride; the Baron’s boast; Sir Plume of amber snuff-box justly vain.

In the text

Let Spades be trumps! she said, and trumps they were.

Canto III, 46

God said, Let there be light: and there was light. The line gives Belinda’s declaration the same creative power. In ombre the player who names trumps becomes the ombre and plays alone against the other two, so for a moment her word is law.

Of all the Causes which conspire to blind / Man’s erring judgment, and misguide the mind, / What the weak head with strongest bias rules, / Is Pride, the never-failing vice of fools.

Essay, 201–204

Pride comes first among the causes of bad judgement, and it rules the weak head most strongly because such a head has least else to steer by. Pope’s sentence holds its subject back for four lines and delivers it at the end. The following lines say that pride fills up the mighty void of sense.

Ancients and moderns

Pope wrote during a long argument over whether modern writers could equal the Greeks and Romans, and the Essay mentions critics who prize only the ancients or only the moderns. It takes the side of the ancients: Homer is the model, the rules are theirs, and modern poets may take only the licences the ancients took. It closes with a history in which criticism falls with Rome and revives with Erasmus, Vida and Boileau. The Rape of the Lock sets the ancients’ epic down at a modern tea-table.

Through the book Be Homer’s works your study and delight; Nature and Homer were, he found, the same; The Ancients only, or the Moderns prize; the history of criticism from Aristotle to Walsh; the machinery of the Rape.

In the text

Those Rules of old discovered, not devis’d, / Are Nature still, but Nature methodiz’d;

Essay, 88–89

Rules were discovered in the practice of the great poets, and critics only set them in order, so obeying them is obeying Nature. The antithesis of discovered and devised carries the argument. The next couplet compares Nature to liberty, restrained only by the laws it made itself.

But when t’ examine ev’ry part he came, / Nature and Homer were, he found, the same.

Essay, 134–135

Virgil sets out to draw only from Nature and discovers, on close study, that Homer has done it before him. Imitating the ancients is therefore the same as following Nature. The line packs the neoclassical creed into ten syllables.

The good critic

An Essay on Criticism cares as much about the critic’s character as about poetry. A good critic judges the whole work in the spirit in which it was written, reads without malice, forgives small faults and keeps severity for real vices. He must be candid, modest and well-bred, able to show a friend his faults and to praise a foe. Pope sums up the ideal in the man who is still pleased to teach, and yet not proud to know.

Through the book A perfect Judge will read each work of Wit / With the same spirit that its author writ; To err is human, to forgive, divine; Men must be taught as if you taught them not; Appius; the portrait of the good critic.

In the text

Good-nature and good-sense must ever join; / To err is human, to forgive, divine.

Essay, 524–525

Good nature and good sense are joined in the first line, and error and forgiveness are divided between humanity and God in the second. The antithesis gives forgiveness a divine status, and the line has become one of the most quoted in English. In context it is advice to critics tempted to attack other writers out of envy.

And stares, tremendous, with a threat’ning eye, / Like some fierce Tyrant in old tapestry.

Essay, 586–587

Appius was John Dennis, author of the tragedy Appius and Virginia, and tremendous was one of his favourite words. The simile shrinks his rage to a figure on a faded wall-hanging. Dennis replied with a pamphlet that mocked Pope’s body as well as his poem.
