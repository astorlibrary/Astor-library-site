The Rape of the Lock and An Essay on Criticism · Alexander Pope

Themes

The trivial and the great

The Rape of the Lock names its subject in the second line: mighty contests rising from trivial things. Pope gives a stolen curl the whole apparatus of epic, with gods, prayers, a council, a battle and a transformation, and the joke cuts two ways. The quarrel looks absurd dressed as the Trojan War, and the grand manner looks a little absurd when it fits a card table so well. Now and then something serious appears among the trifles, the hanging of wretches so that jurymen may dine, and the reader sees how badly this society measures what matters.

What mighty contests rise from trivial things; When husbands, or when lapdogs breathe their last; the ombre battle; Steel could the labour of the Gods destroy; the lock made a star.

What dire offence from am'rous causes springs, / What mighty contests rise from trivial things,

Canto I, 1–2

Here files of pins extend their shining rows, / Puffs, Powders, Patches, Bibles, Billet-doux.

Canto I, 137–138

Beauty and display

Belinda’s beauty is her power, and Pope describes its making in the language of religion and war: the dressing table is an altar, her reflection a goddess, her cosmetics her arms. Her cross is an ornament. Clarissa states the lesson that beauty fades and good humour must keep what it wins, and the last lines promise that the lock will outlast the head it grew on.

The toilet; Puffs, Powders, Patches, Bibles, Billet-doux; the sparkling cross; Fair tresses man’s imperial race ensnare; Clarissa’s speech; the new star.

This casket India's glowing gems unlocks, / And all Arabia breathes from yonder box.

Canto I, 133–134

Here files of pins extend their shining rows, / Puffs, Powders, Patches, Bibles, Billet-doux.

Canto I, 137–138

Honour and reputation

Honour in the Rape means reputation, what the town says about a woman. Ariel tells Belinda that the sylphs keep maids safe, though honour is the word with men below. Thalestris fears for Belinda’s name if the Baron shows off the lock, and at Hampton Court a reputation dies at every word. The Baron wants a trophy that will keep his own honour and name alive. Pope’s zeugmas set honour beside brocade, as if the two could be stained in the same way.

Tho’ Honour is the word with Men below; stain her honour or her new brocade; At ev’ry word a reputation dies; a degraded toast; the Baron’s boast.

'Tis but their Sylph, the wise Celestials know, / Tho' Honour is the word with Men below.

Ariel, Canto I, 77–78

Or some frail China jar receive a flaw; / Or stain her honour or her new brocade; / Forget her pray'rs, or miss a masquerade; / Or lose her heart, or necklace, at a ball;

Ariel, Canto II, 106–109

Men and women

The poem is a comedy of courtship in which the sexes are at war: belles armed with eyes and fans, beaux who die in metaphor, a baron who plans to win by force or fraud. Its innuendo never stops, from Ariel’s warning to beware of man to Belinda’s wish that the Baron had seized hairs less in sight. Some readers find an affectionate portrait of women in it; others, starting from the dedication’s remarks about the fair sex, find condescension, and note that the moral is spoken by the woman who lent the Baron her scissors.

Beware of all, but most beware of Man!; the Baron’s altar; An earthly Lover lurking at her heart; the Cave of Spleen; Hairs less in sight; Clarissa’s speech; the battle of the beaux and belles.

'Tis but their Sylph, the wise Celestials know, / Tho' Honour is the word with Men below.

Ariel, Canto I, 77–78

Beware of all, but most beware of Man!

Ariel, Canto I, 114

Fashion and luxury

Queen Anne’s London is present in every canto: the court at Hampton, the queen taking counsel and tea, gossip, snuff, coffee, china, Indian screens, sedan chairs, patch-boxes and the card game of ombre. The treasures on Belinda’s dressing table come from India and Arabia, with combs of tortoiseshell and ivory, and the fashionable world is seen to depend on trade with half the globe. Pope makes these things glitter, and the glitter is part of the satire.

Here thou, great Anna!; This casket India’s glowing gems unlocks; coffee and china; ombre; Where none learn Ombre, none e’er taste Bohea!

This casket India's glowing gems unlocks, / And all Arabia breathes from yonder box.

Canto I, 133–134

On her white breast a sparkling Cross she wore, / Which Jews might kiss, and Infidels adore.

Canto II, 7–8

Nature and the rules

For Pope, Nature is the clear, unchanging, universal order of things that reason recognises, and the rules of poetry are Nature methodised: principles the ancient critics discovered in the practice of the great poets. Following Nature therefore means following the ancients, as Virgil found. Pope also allows a grace beyond the reach of art, a licence that great poets may take when it serves the purpose the rules exist for.

First follow Nature; Nature methodiz’d; Nature and Homer were, he found, the same; a grace beyond the reach of art; True Wit is Nature to advantage dress’d.

First follow Nature, and your judgment frame / By her just standard, which is still the same:

Essay, 68–69

Those Rules of old discovered, not devis'd, / Are Nature still, but Nature methodiz'd;

Essay, 88–89

Wit and judgement

Wit in Pope’s time could mean intelligence, invention, a clever turn of phrase or a clever man about town, and the Essay uses the word in several of these senses. Its argument is that wit needs judgement, as a spirited horse needs a rider, and that true wit expresses what oft was thought, but ne’er so well. The word slides between these senses from one couplet to the next. In the Rape the wits are the beaux, and their wits are weighed against a lady’s hair and found lighter.

For wit and judgment often are at strife; True Wit is Nature to advantage dress’d; Some have at first for Wits, then Poets past; the wits mount up, the hairs subside.

Now Jove suspends his golden scales in air, / Weighs the Men's wits against the Lady's hair; / The doubtful beam long nods from side to side; / At length the wits mount up, the hairs subside.

Canto V, 71–74

'Tis with our judgments as our watches, none / Go just alike, yet each believes his own.

Essay, 9–10

Pride

Pride is the first cause of bad judgement in the Essay, the never-failing vice of fools, filling up the void where sense is wanting. The good critic is modest, owns his errors and admires superior sense. In the Rape pride is a religion: Belinda’s toilet is the sacred rite of Pride, the Baron boasts that his fame will last for ever, and every character is vain of something, down to Sir Plume and his snuff-box.

Is Pride, the never-failing vice of fools; A little learning is a dang’rous thing; the sacred rites of Pride; the Baron’s boast; Sir Plume of amber snuff-box justly vain.

Let Spades be trumps! she said, and trumps they were.

Canto III, 46

While nymphs take treats, or assignations give, / So long my honour, name, and praise shall live!

The Baron, Canto III, 169–170

Ancients and moderns

Pope wrote during a long argument over whether modern writers could equal the Greeks and Romans, and the Essay mentions critics who prize only the ancients or only the moderns. It takes the side of the ancients: Homer is the model, the rules are theirs, and modern poets may take only the licences the ancients took. It closes with a history in which criticism falls with Rome and revives with Erasmus, Vida and Boileau. The Rape of the Lock sets the ancients’ epic down at a modern tea-table.

Be Homer’s works your study and delight; Nature and Homer were, he found, the same; The Ancients only, or the Moderns prize; the history of criticism from Aristotle to Walsh; the machinery of the Rape.

Those Rules of old discovered, not devis'd, / Are Nature still, but Nature methodiz'd;

Essay, 88–89

But when t' examine ev'ry part he came, / Nature and Homer were, he found, the same.

Essay, 134–135

The good critic

An Essay on Criticism cares as much about the critic’s character as about poetry. A good critic judges the whole work in the spirit in which it was written, reads without malice, forgives small faults and keeps severity for real vices. He must be candid, modest and well-bred, able to show a friend his faults and to praise a foe. Pope sums up the ideal in the man who is still pleased to teach, and yet not proud to know.

A perfect Judge will read each work of Wit / With the same spirit that its author writ; To err is human, to forgive, divine; Men must be taught as if you taught them not; Appius; the portrait of the good critic.

Good-nature and good-sense must ever join; / To err is human, to forgive, divine.

Essay, 524–525

And stares, tremendous, with a threat'ning eye, / Like some fierce Tyrant in old tapestry.

Essay, 586–587
