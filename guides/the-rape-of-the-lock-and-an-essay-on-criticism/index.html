Alexander Pope

The Rape of the Lock and An Essay on Criticism

A study guide

Two poems by the young Alexander Pope. The Rape of the Lock began with a real quarrel: in 1711 Lord Petre cut off a lock of Arabella Fermor’s hair, and a friend of both Catholic families asked Pope for a poem that might laugh them out of it. Pope told the story as a mock epic. Belinda wakes from a dream sent by her guardian sylph, arms herself at her dressing table, sails up the Thames to Hampton Court and wins a game of ombre before the Baron cuts the curl with scissors lent by Clarissa. A gnome brings sighs and tears from the Cave of Spleen, the beaux and belles fight a battle with fans and snuff, and the lock rises to the stars. An Essay on Criticism, published in 1711, argues in verse about how poetry should be judged: follow Nature, learn from the ancients, beware of pride and of a little learning, and be a critic who is learned, candid and humane. Many of its lines have become proverbs.

Written 1709–1717; first published 1711.
