The Rape of the Lock and An Essay on Criticism · Alexander Pope

Form and language

Mock-heroic

Telling a trivial story in the grand style, with the conventions of epic, so that the mismatch is comic.

Pope called the Rape an heroi-comical poem, and it uses nearly every convention of epic: a statement of the subject and an invocation, an arming (the toilet), a sacrifice (the Baron’s altar), supernatural guardians, a council of spirits, a formal contest fought as a battle, a descent to the underworld, a hero’s speech, a general battle, divine scales and a final transformation. The distance between the grand form and the small occasion makes the comedy.

What dire offence from am'rous causes springs, / What mighty contests rise from trivial things,

Canto I, 1–2

Epic machinery

The gods, angels or spirits who take part in the action of an epic, called its machinery by the critics of Pope’s day.

None appears in the first version of 1712. For 1714 Pope added the sylphs and gnomes, taking them from Le Comte de Gabalis, a French satire on Rosicrucian ideas about spirits of the elements, and explained the term in his dedication. The sylphs are the souls of dead coquettes, and their duties, guarding fans, powder and petticoats, are the work of epic gods shrunk to the size of a lady’s dressing room. Pope thought that fitting the new machinery to what he had already published was one of his greatest proofs of judgement.

'Tis but their Sylph, the wise Celestials know, / Tho' Honour is the word with Men below.

Ariel, Canto I, 77–78

The heroic couplet

Rhyming pairs of ten-syllable lines, usually closed, so that each pair completes a thought.

Both poems are in heroic couplets, with an occasional triplet. Pope usually closes the sense at the end of each pair and divides the line at a pause, so that the halves can balance or collide. The Essay praises Denham’s strength and Waller’s sweetness, the poets who made the closed couplet fashionable, and shows how much the form can hold: an argument, a portrait, a joke or a proverb in twenty syllables.

What dire offence from am'rous causes springs, / What mighty contests rise from trivial things,

Canto I, 1–2

Zeugma

One verb or phrase governing two words in different senses, often one serious and one trivial.

Zeugma is the Rape’s favourite comic device: to stain her honour or her new brocade, to lose her heart or necklace at a ball, to take counsel and sometimes tea. The grammar treats the two objects as equal, and the reader has to decide whether the characters do too.

Or some frail China jar receive a flaw; / Or stain her honour or her new brocade; / Forget her pray'rs, or miss a masquerade; / Or lose her heart, or necklace, at a ball;

Ariel, Canto II, 106–109

Bathos

A sudden drop from the lofty to the ridiculous.

Lists that sink as they go are one of the Rape’s habits: puffs, powders, patches, Bibles, billet-doux; husbands or lapdogs; the towers of Troy and a curl of hair. Pope later made the word the title of a prose satire on bad poets, the Bathos, published in 1728.

Here files of pins extend their shining rows, / Puffs, Powders, Patches, Bibles, Billet-doux.

Canto I, 137–138

Antithesis

Opposed ideas set against each other in balanced phrases.

The closed couplet invites balance, and Pope uses it to weigh one thing against another: charms strike the sight, but merit wins the soul; to err is human, to forgive, divine; still pleased to praise, yet not afraid to blame. In the Essay the balance expresses judgement itself, faults weighed against merits.

'Tis but their Sylph, the wise Celestials know, / Tho' Honour is the word with Men below.

Ariel, Canto I, 77–78

Epic simile

An extended comparison that develops its second term at length.

Epic similes are parodied in the Rape, which compares the battle of the beaux and belles to Homer’s gods at war and a dying beau to Ovid’s swan, singing its death-song on the banks of the Maeander. The Essay’s best-known simile is serious: a young scholar climbing the Alps of learning sees hills peep over hills, and Alps on Alps arise.

Allusion

A passing reference to another text, person or event that the reader is expected to recognise.

Pope assumes a reader who knows Homer, Virgil and Milton. Clarissa’s speech parodies Sarpedon’s in the Iliad, Jove’s scales come from Homer, Belinda’s bodkin has a pedigree like Agamemnon’s sceptre, Umbriel’s spleenwort recalls the golden bough, and the lock rises to the stars like the lock of Queen Berenice. The Essay alludes to Horace’s nodding Homer, Virgil’s swift Camilla and Dryden’s Alexander’s Feast.

But chiefly Love—to Love an Altar built, / Of twelve vast French Romances, neatly gilt.

Canto II, 37–38

Epigram

A short balanced sentence that sounds like settled wisdom and usually turns a received idea inside out.

Many lines of the Essay have become proverbs: a little learning is a dangerous thing; to err is human, to forgive, divine; fools rush in where angels fear to tread. Pope compresses a general truth into a line or a couplet, which is why readers remember the poem in pieces.

'Tis with our judgments as our watches, none / Go just alike, yet each believes his own.

Essay, 9–10

Sound echoing sense

Making the sound and movement of a line imitate what it describes.

In the passage on versification in the Essay, Pope performs what he describes: ten low words creep in one dull line, a needless Alexandrine drags its slow length along, the line labours when Ajax heaves a rock, and a line of twelve syllables skims along the main with swift Camilla. The Rape puts the same skill to comic use: fans clap, silks rustle, and tough whalebones crack.

All side in parties, and begin th' attack; / Fans clap, silks rustle, and tough whalebones crack;

Canto V, 39–40

Innuendo

A suggestion, usually sexual, left for the reader to catch.

The Rape is full of double meanings, beginning with its title, in which rape still meant a seizure or theft. The nymph may break Diana’s law, beaux die in metaphor, and Belinda wishes the Baron had seized hairs less in sight. Pope never states the second meaning, which is how the poem stays polite.

Beware of all, but most beware of Man!

Ariel, Canto I, 114
