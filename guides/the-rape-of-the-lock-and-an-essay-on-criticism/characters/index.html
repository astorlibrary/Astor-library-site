The Rape of the Lock and An Essay on Criticism · Alexander Pope

Characters

Belinda

The belle whose lock is cut; first appears Canto I, 4

Admired by every eye on the Thames, Belinda is a young beauty of Queen Anne’s London, with a sparkling cross on her breast and two curls on her neck. At her dressing table she is the chief priestess, and the goddess she worships is her own reflection. She wins at ombre, loses the lock, rages, weeps, throws snuff in the Baron’s face and demands her hair back. In his dedication Pope told Arabella Fermor that Belinda resembled her in nothing but beauty.

The Baron

The lord who cuts the lock; first appears Canto II, 29

An adventurous young peer who sees Belinda’s curls, wants one, and prays for it at dawn at an altar made of French romances. He plays ombre against her and nearly wins, then, with coffee in his brain and Clarissa’s scissors in his hand, cuts the lock and claims it as a conqueror. He refuses Sir Plume, swears by the lock to wear it for ever, and is brought down by a pinch of snuff. Pope based him on Robert, Lord Petre, who was about twenty when he cut a lock of Arabella Fermor’s hair in 1711.

Ariel

Belinda’s guardian sylph; first appears Canto I, 20

The chief of the sylphs, the airy spirits Pope borrowed from the Rosicrucian lore of Le Comte de Gabalis. He sends Belinda her morning dream, warns her to beware of man, commands the sylphs from the mast of her boat, and perches on a matadore at ombre. His power lasts only while she rejects mankind, and when he finds an earthly lover in her heart he withdraws with a sigh. After the lock is cut he flies from her weeping.

Umbriel

The gnome who brings Belinda sorrow; first appears Canto IV, 13

Umbriel is a dusky, melancholy sprite, one of the gnomes into which the souls of prudes sink after death. When the sylphs withdraw he flies down to the Cave of Spleen with a sprig of spleenwort, as Aeneas carried the golden bough into the underworld, flatters its goddess, and brings back a bag of sighs and quarrels and a vial of tears, which he empties over Belinda and Thalestris. He watches the battle in triumph from a sconce.

Clarissa

The lady who lends the scissors and speaks for good sense; first appears Canto III, 127

A lady of the party who draws a two-edged weapon from her case and hands it to the Baron, as ladies in romance arm their knights. In the fifth canto, in a speech Pope added in 1717 to make the moral of the poem clearer, she tells Belinda that beauty fades and that good sense and good humour must keep what beauty wins. Her speech parodies Sarpedon’s speech to Glaucus in the Iliad. No one applauds, and in the battle Sir Plume pulls her down.

Thalestris

Belinda’s warlike friend; first appears Canto IV, 89

Named after a queen of the Amazons, she holds Belinda in her arms, fans her anger, and cries out that Belinda’s honour will be lost if the Baron keeps the lock. She sends her beau, Sir Plume, to demand it back, calls Clarissa a prude, raises the call to arms, and scatters death around her with her eyes in the battle. She is said to have been drawn from a Mrs Morley, sister of Sir George Brown.

Sir Plume

Thalestris’s beau; first appears Canto IV, 121

Sir Plume is vain of his amber snuff-box and of the nice conduct of his clouded cane, and has earnest eyes and a round, unthinking face. Sent to demand the lock, he opens his snuff-box and bursts into a string of oaths that ends with an order to give her the hair. In the battle he pulls Clarissa down, is killed by a frown from Chloe, and revives at her smile. He was taken to be Sir George Brown, who, Johnson reports, complained bitterly that he had been made to talk nonsense.

The Queen of Spleen

Goddess of melancholy and ill humour; first appears Canto IV, 16

The goddess of low spirits and the vapours, who lies sighing on her bed in a grotto where only the east wind blows, with Pain at her side and Megrim, or headache, at her head. Umbriel hails her as the ruler of women from fifteen to fifty and the parent of vapours and of female wit. She seems to reject his prayer, but grants it, filling a bag and a vial with the passions and tears he pours on Belinda. The spleen was a fashionable complaint in Pope’s day, called in The Spectator the distemper of the great and the polite.

The speaker

The young poet who addresses critics in the Essay; first appears Essay, 1

The voice of the Essay belongs to a young poet advising would-be critics: confident, epigrammatic and ready to mock fools, but careful to present himself as modest, the last and meanest of the ancients’ sons, who glows while he reads and trembles as he writes. At the close he honours his dead friend William Walsh and describes his own Muse as not free from faults, nor too vain to mend them. The Essay first appeared without Pope’s name.

Homer

The model poet of the Essay; first appears Essay, 124

Homer, the poet of the Iliad and the Odyssey, is the Essay’s standard of excellence. Critics are told to read him by day and meditate on him by night, Virgil finds that Nature and Homer are the same, and Aristotle steers by the light of the Maeonian star. When Homer seems to nod, Pope suggests, it is the reader who is dreaming. The Rape of the Lock borrows his gods, his battles, his golden scales and his heroes’ speeches.

Virgil

The Roman poet who imitated Homer; first appears Essay, 130

Called young Maro in the Essay, from his full name, Publius Vergilius Maro. Pope imagines him planning the Aeneid as if he were above the critic’s law, until he examines every part and finds that Nature and Homer are the same; he then confines his work to rules as strict as Aristotle’s. The Aeneid lies behind many of the Rape’s jokes, from its opening question to the Baron’s half-granted prayer and Umbriel’s sprig of spleenwort.

Aristotle

The first critic, called the Stagirite; first appears Essay, 138

Aristotle is called the Stagirite, from his birthplace at Stagira. In the Essay his rules were drawn from the practice of great poets, so that to imitate Homer is to obey them. In Part III he is the first explorer of criticism, who spread his sails and steered by the light of Homer, and poets accepted his laws. Pope also laughs at critics who quote his rules without understanding them, like the Don Quixote of a spurious sequel who reveres Aristotle but will not let a play leave out its combat.

William Walsh

Pope’s friend and early adviser; first appears Essay, 729

Pope’s early adviser was a poet and critic who died in 1708, before the Essay appeared, and whom Pope praises at its close as the Muse’s judge and friend, mild to failings and zealous for merit. Walsh met Pope when he was a teenager and advised him to aim at correctness, which no great English poet had yet achieved. Pope showed him his early work, including a draft of the Essay, and Dryden is said to have called him the best critic in the nation.

Appius

An angry critic, drawn from John Dennis; first appears Essay, 585

A critic who reddens at every word you speak and stares, tremendous, like a tyrant in old tapestry. The name came from John Dennis’s tragedy Appius and Virginia (1709), and tremendous was one of Dennis’s favourite words. Dennis answered with an angry pamphlet that attacked Pope’s person as well as his poem, and, as Johnson put it, the hostility between them was never appeased.
