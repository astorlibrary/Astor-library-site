The Cricket on the Hearth · Charles Dickens

What happens

Chirp the First A kettle, a cricket and a passenger left till called for

An evening at the Peerybingles’ fireside is built out of sounds before anyone speaks: kettle, Dutch clock and cricket compete while Dot waits for John’s cart. John brings home parcels, a wedding cake for Tackleton and an old man he found sitting by the roadside. When the stranger comes near the fire Dot cries out and falls into laughing and weeping, and John’s evening ends in a vision of Dots at every age, with one young man at her stool who does not belong there.

The kettle and the cricket
The narrator argues that the kettle started first, and the two sing a race until their song reaches John coming down the lane.
The parcels
Dot finds Tackleton’s wedding cake among the parcels, falls silent, and the cricket stops.
The stranger
A white-haired passenger with a stick that folds into a chair asks to be left till called for, and quizzes John about his ‘very young’ wife.
Tackleton’s invitation
Tackleton says the two couples are just alike, asks John whether his wife truly dotes on him, and says he kills crickets.
The fireside vision
While Dot fills his pipe, the Cricket calls up a lifetime of Dots for John, and a young man’s figure at her stool repeats that she married someone else.

Chirp the Second: Caleb’s house Caleb’s house, and the one he describes to Bertha

The narrator admits that Caleb and his blind daughter live in two different houses: the cracked wooden shed stuck to Tackleton’s premises, and the comfortable home Caleb has described to Bertha for years. Tackleton calls to announce that he is marrying May Fielding and invites himself to Dot’s picnic. Bertha takes the news calmly, then asks her father to be her eyes, and questions him about May and about the kind benefactor she believes Tackleton to be.

The toymaker’s room
Dolls sorted by class from wax to matchsticks, Noah’s arks with knockers, and figures that do foolish things when a handle is turned.
The blue coat
Caleb describes his sackcloth coat to Bertha as a fashionable bright blue one, and walks with a light step he has practised for her ear.
Tackleton’s call
Tackleton calls Bertha an idiot under his breath, takes her thanks for a rose-tree Caleb bought, and announces his marriage.
Bertha’s questions
She asks Caleb to describe the room, May and their benefactor, and weeps on his shoulder.

Chirp the Second: the picnic A picnic, a confession and a lit window

The Peerybingles drive through the January fog to their fortnightly picnic at Caleb’s, where May, her mother and Tackleton have arrived. Dot’s talk of the husbands they chose at school brings May to tears, and once John has driven on, Bertha falls at May’s feet and confesses that the coming marriage has almost broken her heart. In the evening John returns with the old stranger, and Tackleton takes him across a dark yard to a window through which he sees Dot fixing the stranger’s white hair back on a young man’s head.

The road
Boxer runs ahead, every house has a parcel or a greeting, and John is proud to have his wife admired from the cart.
Mrs Fielding at table
May’s mother mentions the Indigo Trade and says the marriages with least love in them are the happiest.
Bertha at May’s feet
Bertha blesses May and admits her grief, and Caleb understands at one blow what his inventions have done.
The window
Tackleton warns John against violence, then shows him Dot and the stranger in the lamplit ware-room.

Chirp the Third: the night A gun, a cricket and a night of thinking

Alone by his cold hearth at ten o’clock, John takes a loaded gun from the wall and goes to the stranger’s door. The cricket chirps, he puts the gun aside, and the Cricket appears in fairy shape with the Household Fairies, who show him Dot’s conduct in a glass all night. In the morning the stranger is gone through an open window, and John tells Tackleton that Dot is innocent of everything except concealment, that he never considered her enough, and that he will send her home to her parents.

The gun
Rage grows until John raises the gun to beat in the door, and the fire flares as the cricket begins.
The Fairy Cricket
The Cricket repeats Dot’s words about loving it, corrects John’s past tense, and calls the hearth the altar of his home.
The glass of fairies
Household Fairies show Dot turning down a dance, caring for Bertha and weeping on her stool, and rub out the stranger’s shadow whenever it falls.
John’s speech to Tackleton
John says he took a young woman from her friends to a dull house and will release her with his blessing; Dot overhears.

Chirp the Third: the wedding morning Edward Plummer returns, and everything is explained

Caleb brings Bertha to the Carrier’s house and tells her the truth about their home and their master, and Dot, asked what she sees, describes Caleb as he really is. Wheels are heard, Edward Plummer comes in without his disguise, and Dot explains that she kept his secret so that he could marry May, which he did at church that morning. Tackleton throws his ring in the fire, then sends the cake and toys to the celebration and comes himself. The book ends with a dance, after which the narrator finds himself alone with a cricket and a broken toy.

Caleb’s confession
Caleb admits the world he described does not exist; Bertha asks Dot to describe her father and calls the answer her sight restored.
Edward returns
Dot covers Caleb’s eyes as a sunburnt young man runs in, and the cuckoo strikes twelve.
Dot’s account
Speaking of herself as ‘she’, Dot tells how she carried messages between Edward and May, then asks John not to love her until he has heard it all.
Tackleton’s change
Tackleton asks Tilly to throw his ring in the fire, then sends the wedding cake and asks to join the party.
The dance and after
Everyone dances, including Tackleton with Mrs Fielding, and then the whole company vanishes from the narrator’s sight.
