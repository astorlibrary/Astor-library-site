The Cricket on the Hearth · Charles Dickens

Characters

John Peerybingle

The Carrier, Dot’s husband; first appears Chirp the First

A slow, heavy, good-natured carrier who is always very near a joke and never gets to it. Dickens gives him the book’s hardest night: shown what looks like proof of his wife’s betrayal, he reaches for a gun, puts it down when the cricket chirps, and by morning has turned the charge on himself for marrying a woman so much younger.

Dot Peerybingle

John’s young wife; first appears Chirp the First

Mary Peerybingle, called Dot by her husband, is quick, domestic and good at managing other people’s feelings. For most of the book the narrator lets her behave like a guilty wife and even tells the reader they may learn to hate her. Her secret turns out to be a kindness to May and Edward, and her last speech answers the fear she voiced in the first Chirp about an ill-assorted marriage.

Caleb Plummer

A poor toymaker working for Gruff and Tackleton; first appears Chirp the First

A little, meagre man in a coat made from sacking with the firm’s initials stencilled on the back, who has spent years describing that coat to his blind daughter as bright blue. His lies are all kindnesses, and Dickens lets them do damage: Bertha falls in love with the generous master Caleb invented, and her father is left asking whether he deceived her from her cradle only to break her heart.

Bertha Plummer

Caleb’s blind daughter, a doll’s dressmaker; first appears Chirp the Second

She lives in the comfortable house her father describes and loves the kind benefactor he has made out of their employer. Dickens gives her the sharpest ear in the book, since she hears a young man’s tread behind the old stranger’s disguise. When the truth comes she takes it as a gain, holding her father’s grey head and saying she is not blind any longer.

Tackleton

Toy merchant, trading as Gruff and Tackleton; first appears Chirp the First

A man who despises toys and makes his living from them, adding grim faces and goblins to his stock to frighten children. He is about to marry May Fielding, a much younger woman, and says obedience will be enough for him. He also sets out to show that John’s marriage is no better than his own. His change at the end is quick: he sends his wedding cake to the party and asks to be let in because there is no cricket left on his hearth.

Edward Plummer

Caleb’s son, back from South America; first appears Chirp the First

Believed dead in what Caleb calls the Golden South Americas, he comes home to hear that his sweetheart is about to marry a wealthier man, and disguises himself as a deaf old man so that he can watch her without being known. He explains the disguise himself in the last Chirp, and his only defence is that John never used to judge a man unheard.

May Fielding

Dot’s schoolfellow, engaged to Tackleton; first appears Chirp the Second

Pretty, quiet and almost silent, May has been persuaded by her mother into an engagement she had for some time refused. She says little at the picnic, but her colour and her tears at Dot’s talk of school-day sweethearts tell the reader what she will not say. She marries Edward on the morning she was due at church with Tackleton.

Tilly Slowboy

The Peerybingles’ nursemaid; first appears Chirp the First

A foundling in her early teens, brought up by public charity, who cannot cross a room without knocking the baby’s head against something. She repeats scraps of adult talk to the baby with the nouns turned plural, and one of these garbled repetitions is the broken hint about Bertha that Tackleton mentions when he comes to apologise.

Mrs Fielding

May’s mother; first appears Chirp the Second

A small, peevish old lady who believes her family would have been wealthy but for something in the Indigo Trade that she will not describe. She pushes May towards Tackleton on the principle that marriages with least love in them are the happiest, and when the plan collapses she has to be fetched to the celebration by an expedition.

The Cricket

The cricket on the Peerybingles’ hearth; first appears Chirp the First

The title character chirps in competition with the kettle, falls silent when Dot is troubled, and starts again just as John lifts the gun. The narrator calls it the Genius of the Carrier’s Hearth and Home. In fairy form it speaks to John in Dot’s own words and calls up Household Fairies to show him her conduct, and the next morning it appears behind Bertha during Caleb’s confession, pointing to her father.
