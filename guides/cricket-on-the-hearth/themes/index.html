The Cricket on the Hearth · Charles Dickens

Themes

Hearth and home

Home in this book is a set of sounds and habits, kettle, clock, pipe and stool, and Dickens shows how quickly the same objects change meaning once John suspects his wife. Tackleton’s definition, four walls and a ceiling, is the view the book sets out to disprove.

Chirp the First builds the home out of noise; Chirp the Second sets Caleb’s invented home beside the real shed; in Chirp the Third the Cricket calls the hearth an altar, Dot calls John’s heart her home, and Tackleton admits there is no cricket on his.

The kettle began it! Don’t tell me what Mrs. Peerybingle said. I know better.

Chirp the First

Oh, Mother Nature, give thy children the true poetry of heart that hid itself in this poor Carrier’s breast—he was but a Carrier, by the way—and we can bear to have them talking prose, and leading lives of prose

Chirp the First

Age, choice and marriage

Three unequal matches are set side by side: John and Dot, Tackleton and May, and the one Mrs Fielding approves of because it has least love in it. John’s decision to release Dot rests on the fear Dot herself voiced in the first Chirp, that a husband more like a guardian might never be loved.

Tackleton claims in Chirp the First that the two couples are just alike. Chirp the Third tests the claim, finds it false for the Peerybingles, and lets May out of her match by marrying Edward first.

I did fear once, John; I was very young, you know—that ours might prove to be an ill-assorted marriage, I being such a child, and you more like my guardian than my husband

Chirp the First

Otherwise I never could have loved her as I did … The Voice, correcting him, said “do.” The Carrier repeated “as I did.” But not firmly.

Chirp the Third

Jealousy

John’s jealousy arrives through evidence arranged by somebody else, and it runs from rage to self-reproach in a single night. Dickens borrows the outline of a tragic jealousy plot, with a trusted informant, a sight through a window and a weapon at a bedroom door, and turns it aside at the last moment.

Tackleton plants the idea in Chirp the First and stages the proof in Chirp the Second. In Chirp the Third he finds himself lectured by a man who has thought his way out of it.

Oh, Dot! Oh, failing Dot! There is no place for it in all your husband’s visions. Why has its shadow fallen on his hearth?

Chirp the First

He saw them stop, and saw her turn—to have the face, the face he loved so, so presented to his view!—and saw her, with her own hands, adjust the lie upon his head, laughing, as she did it, at his unsuspicious nature!

Chirp the Second

Seeing and knowing

The blind character hears what the sighted ones miss, and the one scene of plain looking, John at the window, produces a false conclusion. Caleb’s word-pictures for Bertha and Tackleton’s arranged view for John are versions of the same act: telling someone else what they are looking at.

Bertha hears the young man’s step in Chirp the Second, and John sees Dot at the window the same evening. In Chirp the Third Bertha asks Dot to say what she sees, and calls the answer her sight restored.

The kettle began it! Don’t tell me what Mrs. Peerybingle said. I know better.

Chirp the First

For years and years he had never once crossed that threshold at his own slow pace, but with a footfall counterfeited for her ear; and never had he, when his heart was heaviest, forgotten the light tread that was to render hers so cheerful and courageous!

Chirp the Second

Kind lies and kept secrets

Caleb lies to Bertha out of love and Dot hides Edward’s return out of loyalty to May, and both concealments do harm before they do any good. The book forgives both, but only after each has been confessed in full.

Caleb’s question about deceiving Bertha from her cradle begins in Chirp the Second; Dot’s secret nearly ends her marriage; both are told on the same morning, one straight after the other.

I did fear once, John; I was very young, you know—that ours might prove to be an ill-assorted marriage, I being such a child, and you more like my guardian than my husband

Chirp the First

Toys, trade and work

Almost everyone here works for a living: a carrier with two rounds, a toymaker nailing harness onto wooden horses, a doll’s dressmaker who cannot see her dolls. Tackleton’s business makes childhood into stock, and Caleb’s room, with dolls graded by class and figures worked by little handles, is a small model of the adult world.

The toys are described at length in Chirp the Second, watch the picnic like an audience, and stand run down and staring at the end of that Chirp. The last sentence of the book leaves a single broken toy on the ground.

Oh, Mother Nature, give thy children the true poetry of heart that hid itself in this poor Carrier’s breast—he was but a Carrier, by the way—and we can bear to have them talking prose, and leading lives of prose

Chirp the First

A Cricket sings upon the Hearth; a broken child’s toy lies upon the ground: and nothing else remains.

Chirp the Third

Forgiveness

John forgives Dot before he knows she is innocent: he takes the blame on himself and offers her freedom with his blessing. The later reconciliations, Bertha with her father and everyone with Tackleton, come more quickly and cost the forgiven party less.

John’s speech to Tackleton is the turning point; Dot asks him not to love her again until he has heard everything; Tackleton is forgiven within five minutes of walking in.

Otherwise I never could have loved her as I did … The Voice, correcting him, said “do.” The Carrier repeated “as I did.” But not firmly.

Chirp the Third

I had studied myself; I had had experience of myself; I knew how much I loved her, and how happy I should be … But I had not—I feel it now—sufficiently considered her.

Chirp the Third
