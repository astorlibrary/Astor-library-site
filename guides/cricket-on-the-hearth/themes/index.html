Hearth and home

Home in this book is a set of sounds and habits, kettle, clock, pipe and stool, and Dickens shows how quickly the same objects change meaning once John suspects his wife. Tackleton’s definition, four walls and a ceiling, is the view the book sets out to disprove.

Through the book Chirp the First builds the home out of noise; Chirp the Second sets Caleb’s invented home beside the real shed; in Chirp the Third the Cricket calls the hearth an altar, Dot calls John’s heart her home, and Tackleton admits there is no cricket on his.

In the text

Oh, Mother Nature, give thy children the true poetry of heart that hid itself in this poor Carrier’s breast—he was but a Carrier, by the way—and we can bear to have them talking prose, and leading lives of prose

Chirp the First

Dickens uses the old division between poetry and prose to make a point about class: the carrier talks prose and lives a life of prose, and the aside ‘he was but a Carrier, by the way’ mimics a speaker who would look down on him. The sentence is itself an appeal to Nature, so the narrator supplies the poetry that John never gets to the end of a joke to reach.

When suddenly, the struggling fire illuminated the whole chimney with a glow of light; and the Cricket on the Hearth began to Chirp!

Chirp the Third

The rescue comes from the fire and a household insect, and the sentence stages it like lighting and sound arriving on cue. The broken-off sentence before ‘When suddenly’ leaves John’s arm in the air, and the capital on ‘Chirp’ gives the smallest sound in the house the weight of a verdict.

Age, choice and marriage

Three unequal matches are set side by side: John and Dot, Tackleton and May, and the one Mrs Fielding approves of because it has least love in it. John’s decision to release Dot rests on the fear Dot herself voiced in the first Chirp, that a husband more like a guardian might never be loved.

Through the book Tackleton claims in Chirp the First that the two couples are just alike. Chirp the Third tests the claim, finds it false for the Peerybingles, and lets May out of her match by marrying Edward first.

In the text

I did fear once, John; I was very young, you know—that ours might prove to be an ill-assorted marriage, I being such a child, and you more like my guardian than my husband

Chirp the First

Dot introduces the fear as something she once had, and the dash that breaks the sentence suggests it is not quite over. ‘More like my guardian than my husband’ is the charge John brings against himself in Chirp the Third, so when he decides the marriage was unequal he is finishing a sentence she began here.

Otherwise I never could have loved her as I did … The Voice, correcting him, said “do.” The Carrier repeated “as I did.” But not firmly.

Chirp the Third

The argument of the whole night is conducted over a verb’s tense. John puts his love in the past to protect himself, the Cricket moves it into the present, and when John repeats the past tense ‘not firmly’ the grammar shows the change before he admits it.

Jealousy

John’s jealousy arrives through evidence arranged by somebody else, and it runs from rage to self-reproach in a single night. Dickens borrows the outline of a tragic jealousy plot, with a trusted informant, a sight through a window and a weapon at a bedroom door, and turns it aside at the last moment.

Through the book Tackleton plants the idea in Chirp the First and stages the proof in Chirp the Second. In Chirp the Third he finds himself lectured by a man who has thought his way out of it.

In the text

Oh, Dot! Oh, failing Dot! There is no place for it in all your husband’s visions. Why has its shadow fallen on his hearth?

Chirp the First

Speaking to Dot as if she were already guilty, the narrator calls her ‘failing’, a word that prepares the reader to take the next Chirp John’s way. The charge comes as questions about a shadow, and a shadow is close to what the young man is: a lover the others believe dead, returned in disguise.

He saw them stop, and saw her turn—to have the face, the face he loved so, so presented to his view!—and saw her, with her own hands, adjust the lie upon his head, laughing, as she did it, at his unsuspicious nature!

Chirp the Second

John’s evidence is laid out as a series of things ‘he saw’, and one noun gives the method away: Dot adjusts ‘the lie’ on the stranger’s head. The word is the narrator’s, a pun on the false hair and on deceit, and it leaves open, as John cannot, the question of whose lie it is.

Seeing and knowing

The blind character hears what the sighted ones miss, and the one scene of plain looking, John at the window, produces a false conclusion. Caleb’s word-pictures for Bertha and Tackleton’s arranged view for John are versions of the same act: telling someone else what they are looking at.

Through the book Bertha hears the young man’s step in Chirp the Second, and John sees Dot at the window the same evening. In Chirp the Third Bertha asks Dot to say what she sees, and calls the answer her sight restored.

In the text

The kettle began it! Don’t tell me what Mrs. Peerybingle said. I know better.

Chirp the First

The narrator starts with a quarrel over which household noise came first, and conducts it like a witness who will not be contradicted. His ‘I know better’ comes back in Tackleton’s mouth, ‘We know better, you know’, when he hints that the Peerybingles’ happiness is for show, and John’s reply is to refuse the phrase.

Father, I am lonely in the dark. I want my eyes, my patient, willing eyes.

Chirp the Second

Bertha calls her father her eyes and gives the eyes two virtues that belong to a person, patience and willingness. What she wants is description, and Caleb’s answer, that the shed is ‘Homely, but very snug’, shows him lying to the owner of the eyes in the act of lending them.

Kind lies and kept secrets

Caleb lies to Bertha out of love and Dot hides Edward’s return out of loyalty to May, and both concealments do harm before they do any good. The book forgives both, but only after each has been confessed in full.

Through the book Caleb’s question about deceiving Bertha from her cradle begins in Chirp the Second; Dot’s secret nearly ends her marriage; both are told on the same morning, one straight after the other.

In the text

I should have said that Caleb lived here, and his poor Blind Daughter somewhere else—in an enchanted home of Caleb’s furnishing, where scarcity and shabbiness were not, and trouble never entered.

Chirp the Second

Dickens revises himself in mid-paragraph, and the revision splits one address into two houses. The enchanted home is defined by what it lacks, scarcity, shabbiness, trouble, and the next paragraph fills the gap with what Bertha ‘never knew’: discoloured ceilings, rotting wood, a father going grey in front of her.

Have I deceived her from her cradle, but to break her heart at last?

Chirp the Second

Caleb’s question has the grammar of a prayer, addressed to ‘Great Power’, and it holds his whole history with Bertha in two clauses. Dickens repeats it twice more as the narrator reads it in Caleb’s face, and then Tilly Slowboy chants it to the baby with the nouns made plural, which is how it reaches Tackleton.

Toys, trade and work

Almost everyone here works for a living: a carrier with two rounds, a toymaker nailing harness onto wooden horses, a doll’s dressmaker who cannot see her dolls. Tackleton’s business makes childhood into stock, and Caleb’s room, with dolls graded by class and figures worked by little handles, is a small model of the adult world.

Through the book The toys are described at length in Chirp the Second, watch the picnic like an audience, and stand run down and staring at the end of that Chirp. The last sentence of the book leaves a single broken toy on the ground.

In the text

A Cricket sings upon the Hearth; a broken child’s toy lies upon the ground: and nothing else remains.

Chirp the Third

Having been at the party all along, the narrator is left alone in the present tense with two objects. The cricket is the one sound left over from the first page, and setting a broken toy beside it, in a book full of Caleb’s toys, lets the ending read as the close of a children’s game as well as of a story.

Forgiveness

John forgives Dot before he knows she is innocent: he takes the blame on himself and offers her freedom with his blessing. The later reconciliations, Bertha with her father and everyone with Tackleton, come more quickly and cost the forgiven party less.

Through the book John’s speech to Tackleton is the turning point; Dot asks him not to love her again until he has heard everything; Tackleton is forgiven within five minutes of walking in.

In the text

I had studied myself; I had had experience of myself; I knew how much I loved her, and how happy I should be … But I had not—I feel it now—sufficiently considered her.

Chirp the Third

John’s three clauses about himself are balanced and practised, and the fourth breaks at a dash, which is where the night’s thinking shows. ‘Sufficiently considered her’ is mild wording for what follows: he blames himself for taking a young woman from her friends to a dull house, and offers to set her free.

It is my sight restored. It is my sight! … I have been blind, and now my eyes are open. I never knew him!

Chirp the Third

Bertha uses the language of a miraculous cure for an event in which nothing happens to her eyes. What she has gained is an accurate description, supplied by Dot, and Dickens lets her count it as sight; the short last sentence, ‘I never knew him’, admits what the years of protection cost her.
