The Cricket on the Hearth · Charles Dickens

Form and language

Direct address

A narrator who talks to the reader, arguing, teasing and admitting to opinions of his own.

The first page is an argument with the reader about whether the kettle or the cricket began, and the narrator keeps stepping in: he defends Dot’s figure, warns that the reader may learn to hate her, and in the last paragraph turns out to have been in the room watching the dance.

The kettle began it! Don’t tell me what Mrs. Peerybingle said. I know better.

Chirp the First

Personification

Giving human feelings, speech or motives to objects and animals.

The kettle sulks, dribbles like a drunk and sings a song about the weather; the Hay-maker on the Dutch clock suffers every time it strikes; Boxer is credited with tact towards a blind girl. The house is animated so thoroughly that the Cricket’s speaking part in Chirp the Third reads as an extension of the style.

The kettle began it! Don’t tell me what Mrs. Peerybingle said. I know better.

Chirp the First

Idiolect

A speech habit belonging to one character alone, so that they can be recognised before they are named.

John is always very near a joke, Tilly makes every noun plural when she repeats adult talk to the baby, Mrs Fielding goes back to the Indigo Trade, and Caleb falls back on a song about a Sparkling Bowl whenever a question gets too close.

Bah! what’s home? … Four walls and a ceiling! (Why don’t you kill that Cricket? I would! I always do. I hate their noise.)

Chirp the First

Refrain

A phrase brought back at intervals, so that its meaning shifts with what has happened since.

Caleb’s question, whether he deceived Bertha from her cradle only to break her heart, is spoken once and then read twice more in his face, and Tilly chants a pluralised version of it to the baby. The chirp works the same way, meaning welcome, then warning, then mercy.

Have I deceived her from her cradle, but to break her heart at last?

Chirp the Second

Disguise

A character concealing their identity, so that some people in the story know more than others.

Edward’s white wig and pretended deafness keep the reader as much in the dark as John. Dot knows, and Bertha half hears through it, and the narrator’s comments are arranged so that the reader shares John’s reading of the window.

For years and years he had never once crossed that threshold at his own slow pace, but with a footfall counterfeited for her ear; and never had he, when his heart was heaviest, forgotten the light tread that was to render hers so cheerful and courageous!

Chirp the Second

Symbolic objects

An object the narrative comes back to until it stands for the argument itself.

Dot’s little stool, the pipe she fills, the Dutch clock and Tackleton’s wedding cake are introduced as household furniture and later carry the state of the marriage. Dot bungles the pipe when she is frightened, John cannot bear to look at the empty stool, and the cake ends up on the Peerybingles’ table.

I did fear once, John; I was very young, you know—that ours might prove to be an ill-assorted marriage, I being such a child, and you more like my guardian than my husband

Chirp the First

Allegory

A story or figure in which characters stand for ideas and act them out.

In Chirp the Third the Cricket becomes the Genius of the Hearth, and Household Fairies act out Dot’s virtues in a glass, rubbing out the Stranger’s shadow whenever it falls. The fairy-tale subtitle is made literal here, and the allegory is kept to John’s night of thinking.

Otherwise I never could have loved her as I did … The Voice, correcting him, said “do.” The Carrier repeated “as I did.” But not firmly.

Chirp the Third
