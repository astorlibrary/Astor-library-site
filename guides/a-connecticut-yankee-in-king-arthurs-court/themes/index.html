Technology and power

Hank’s gift is manufacture: guns, boilers, engines, and the machines that make them. The novel follows that gift from soap and matches through telephones to dynamite mines and Gatling guns, without ever changing its opinion that the gift is good.

Through the book The inventions are comic for twenty chapters, useful for ten, and by the sand-belt they are a system that lets fifty-four operators kill twenty-five thousand men between one dawn and the next.

In the text

So I am a Yankee of the Yankees—and practical; yes, and nearly barren of sentiment, I suppose—or poetry, in other words.

A Word of Explanation

He offers his lack of poetry as a qualification. Everything the novel goes on to test is in that boast: the man who is proud of having no sentiment will be the one deciding what a life is worth on the last afternoon.

the very first official thing I did, in my administration—and it was on the very first day of it, too—was to start a patent office

Chapter IX

Before schools, before medicine, before food: the machinery for owning ideas. It is a joke about American priorities and a serious statement of Twain’s own, made while he was bankrupting himself on a patented typesetter.

Kings, bosses and obedience

Twain’s target is the trained reflex that makes a population accept whoever stands above it, whatever the king is like. The Boss deplores it and then relies on it: his own power rests on exactly the awe he came to abolish.

Through the book He takes the country with a magic trick, rules it as the Boss, and discovers at the end that the people will obey the Church’s interdict the same way they obeyed him.

In the text

Any kind of royalty, howsoever modified

Chapter VIII

The opening of the novel’s plainest political statement, and it is made by a man who has just accepted the second-highest office in the kingdom. Twain lets the contradiction stand without comment for another thirty chapters.

unlimited power is the ideal thing when it is in safe hands

Chapter X

The clause that gives the book away. He means his own hands, and the last chapters answer the sentence with a body count.

The Established Church

The novel’s declared villain is an institution: a church that owns the land, blesses the arrangement and can switch the nation off with an interdict.

Through the book It is a grumble in the early chapters, a rival in the middle, and in the last five the decisive power in the book, the one force whose technology beats his.

Freemen, serfs and wages

The sharpest chapters are about economics: what a wage buys, who owns the land, and why men who are being robbed defend the system that robs them. Twain gives the arguments properly, with a blacksmith who has an answer for everything except the arithmetic.

Through the book The Boss starts by finding the serfs comic, learns to be angry on their behalf, and ends by discovering that they will march against him when told to.

In the text

a man is a man, at bottom

Chapter XXX

The democratic premise stated in five words, and it is the belief that makes his schools worth building. The novel gives it the best evidence it has, the king in the smallpox hut, and then lets the reader decide whether it survives the sand-belt.

I was become a stockholder in a corporation where nine hundred and ninety-four of the members furnished all the money and did all the work

Chapter XIII

Twain writes social analysis as a company report: nine hundred and ninety-four workers, six shareholders. Twain’s arithmetic here is the model for the economic chapters later, where the same point is made to a blacksmith who cannot bear it.

Slavery

The king and the Boss are sold as slaves and walk in a chain gang, and the novel stops being funny. Twain is writing about Britain in 528 and about the American South he grew up in, and he does not pretend otherwise.

Through the book The theme arrives as a detail of the setting, becomes an argument in the middle chapters, and is the reason the book’s comedy cannot be recovered after chapter thirty-five.

In the text

The blunting effects of slavery upon the slaveholder’s moral perceptions are known and conceded

Chapter XXV

Written by a man who grew up in a slaveholding town in Missouri, about a country thirteen centuries away. The sentence is careful and general, and the chapters around it are neither.

we were in a trap

Chapter XLIV

In four words the victory becomes the ending. The fifty-four cannot leave because of what they have made, and the plague that kills them comes from the men they killed. It is the plainest statement in Twain of what a decisive weapon does to the man holding it.

Education

Hank’s schools, his man-factory and his newspaper all rest on the belief that people are taught into their stations. It is the novel’s most generous idea, and the one it tries hardest to break.

Through the book The boys he trains are the only people who stand with him at the end, which proves the theory and destroys everything it was supposed to build.

In the text

Fifty-four what? Men? No, minds—the capablest in the world

Chapter XLIII

The confidence is technical and entirely justified: fifty-four trained people with electricity and machine guns can beat thirty thousand. What the sentence cannot see is that being right about this is the disaster.

How empty is theory in presence of fact!

Chapter XLIII

He means the flag of truce. The line applies far better to everything else he has built: the schools, the newspapers and the republic have all just been shown to be theory, and the fact arriving over the hill is armed.

Staged miracles

The eclipse, the exploding tower, the restored fountain and the electric fence are all theatre: effects produced for an audience that must not understand them. The Boss is a showman who despises the other showmen.

Through the book Each performance is larger than the last and each requires a bigger secret, until the final effect is a battlefield and the audience is dead.

In the text

It was in Warwick Castle that I came across the curious stranger whom I am going to talk about.

A Word of Explanation

A tourist’s sentence, flat and unhurried, before anything strange has been allowed in. The frame is doing quiet work: it lets the impossible story arrive as somebody else’s manuscript, so the reader can decide for himself what to believe.

I will blot out the sun, and he shall never shine again

Chapter V

Knowledge used as a threat, in the voice of the magicians he has been deriding. The sentence wins him the kingdom and sets the terms of his rule: he will govern by doing things nobody around him can explain.
