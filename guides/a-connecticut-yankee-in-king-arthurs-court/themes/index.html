A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur’s Court · Mark Twain

Themes

Technology and power

Hank’s gift is manufacture: guns, boilers, engines, and the machines that make them. The novel follows that gift from soap and matches through telephones to dynamite mines and Gatling guns, without ever changing its opinion that the gift is good.

The inventions are comic for twenty chapters, useful for ten, and by the sand-belt they are a system that lets fifty-four operators kill twenty-five thousand men between one dawn and the next.

So I am a Yankee of the Yankees—and practical; yes, and nearly barren of sentiment, I suppose—or poetry, in other words.

A Word of Explanation

Why, I could make anything a body wanted—anything in the world, it didn’t make any difference what

Kings, bosses and obedience

Twain’s target is the trained reflex that makes a population accept whoever stands above it, whatever the king is like. The Boss deplores it and then relies on it: his own power rests on exactly the awe he came to abolish.

He takes the country with a magic trick, rules it as the Boss, and discovers at the end that the people will obey the Church’s interdict the same way they obeyed him.

I will blot out the sun, and he shall never shine again

Chapter VIII

The Established Church

The novel’s declared villain is an institution: a church that owns the land, blesses the arrangement and can switch the nation off with an interdict.

It is a grumble in the early chapters, a rival in the middle, and in the last five the decisive power in the book, the one force whose technology beats his.

Freemen, serfs and wages

The sharpest chapters are about economics: what a wage buys, who owns the land, and why men who are being robbed defend the system that robs them. Twain gives the arguments properly, with a blacksmith who has an answer for everything except the arithmetic.

The Boss starts by finding the serfs comic, learns to be angry on their behalf, and ends by discovering that they will march against him when told to.

the very first official thing I did, in my administration—and it was on the very first day of it, too—was to start a patent office

Chapter IX

a man is a man, at bottom

Chapter XXX

Slavery

The king and the Boss are sold as slaves and walk in a chain gang, and the novel stops being funny. Twain is writing about Britain in 528 and about the American South he grew up in, and he does not pretend otherwise.

The theme arrives as a detail of the setting, becomes an argument in the middle chapters, and is the reason the book’s comedy cannot be recovered after chapter thirty-five.

The blunting effects of slavery upon the slaveholder’s moral perceptions are known and conceded

Chapter XXV

we were in a trap

Chapter XLIV

Education

Hank’s schools, his man-factory and his newspaper all rest on the belief that people are taught into their stations. It is the novel’s most generous idea, and the one it tries hardest to break.

The boys he trains are the only people who stand with him at the end, which proves the theory and destroys everything it was supposed to build.

a man is a man, at bottom

Chapter XXX

Fifty-four what? Men? No, minds—the capablest in the world

Chapter XLIII

Staged miracles

The eclipse, the exploding tower, the restored fountain and the electric fence are all theatre: effects produced for an audience that must not understand them. The Boss is a showman who despises the other showmen.

Each performance is larger than the last and each requires a bigger secret, until the final effect is a battlefield and the audience is dead.

It was in Warwick Castle that I came across the curious stranger whom I am going to talk about.
