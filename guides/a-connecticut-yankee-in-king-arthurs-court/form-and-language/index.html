A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur’s Court · Mark Twain

Form and language

Frame narrative

A story presented inside another story, with a narrator who is handed it.

The Warwick frame lets Twain end the book twice: once with the Boss unconscious in a cave, and once in the present with a dying man calling for a wife thirteen centuries away.

It was in Warwick Castle that I came across the curious stranger whom I am going to talk about.

A Word of Explanation

Anachronism

Placing the objects and language of one age inside another, deliberately and visibly.

Telephone wires in the forest, soap advertisements on knights, stove polish, a patent office in 528. The joke works in both directions: the sixth century looks absurd, and so does the nineteenth when it arrives in armour.

Deadpan

Describing something extraordinary in the tone used for routine business.

The account of the sand-belt is written like a works report: fences, mines, dispositions, a count of the dead. Twain does not change register, and that is why the last chapters feel so cold.

So I am a Yankee of the Yankees—and practical; yes, and nearly barren of sentiment, I suppose—or poetry, in other words.

A Word of Explanation

Escalation

Exaggeration pushed until it changes what it is describing.

One man against a kingdom, one eclipse to win a country, one afternoon against the whole of chivalry. Twain lets the scale grow by stages so that the reader accepts the final figure of twenty-five thousand.

Fifty-four what? Men? No, minds—the capablest in the world

Chapter XLIII

Irony

A gap between what the teller believes he is showing and what the reader sees.

Hank denounces men who rule by mystification while ruling by mystification, and reports his massacre as a technical success. Twain never steps in to correct him.

Any kind of royalty, howsoever modified

Chapter VIII

The public demonstration

An extended scene staged in front of a crowd, in which the plot turns.

The eclipse, the blowing up of Merlin’s tower, the restored fountain, the duel with Sagramor and the sand-belt are the same scene five times, each with a larger audience and a higher cost.

I will blot out the sun, and he shall never shine again

Chapter V

Lists

Piling up items so that the accumulation itself makes the point.

Schools, a patent office, a newspaper, matches, soap, insurance, a stock exchange: the lists are funny, and they are also the novel showing how much of a civilisation rests on one man’s memory.

Why, I could make anything a body wanted—anything in the world, it didn’t make any difference what
