A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur’s Court · Mark Twain

Context

Dates

1835
Twain born in Missouri In a slaveholding state, thirty years before emancipation; the slave chapters of this novel are written out of that childhood.
1884
George Washington Cable gives Twain Malory’s Le Morte d’Arthur George Washington Cable handed him Le Morte d’Arthur on tour, and the notebook joke about an itching knight followed within days.
1886
First chapters read aloud Twain read the opening to an audience on Governors Island and described the book as a contrast between two civilisations.
1886
Twain pours money into the Paige typesetter He was pouring money into a machine of eighteen thousand parts that never worked; the novel’s faith in engineering was written by a man being ruined by it.
1888
Later chapters written, including the slave gang and the sand-belt The later chapters, including the slave gang and the sand-belt, were written after the manuscript had been set aside twice.
1889
First edition published by Charles L. Webster & Co. Charles L. Webster & Co. issued it in December with 220 illustrations by Dan Beard, whose drawings pushed the politics further than the text.
1889
British edition published as A Yankee at the Court of King Arthur Chatto & Windus published it as A Yankee at the Court of King Arthur; English reviewers took the satire personally.
1890
William Dean Howells praises the book in Harper’s William Dean Howells praised the book’s anger at privilege in Harper’s, setting the terms for its American reception.
1894
Twain’s bankruptcy The typesetter failed and took his publishing house with it; he spent the next years on a lecture tour paying creditors.
1921
First film version A silent adaptation began a century of screen versions that keep the eclipse and drop the sand-belt.
1949
Film musical starring Bing Crosby The best-known adaptation makes the novel a romance and leaves out the slaughter at the end of the book.

Places

Hartford, Connecticut
Hank’s birthplace, the Colt arms factory he trained in, and the house where Twain wrote the book.
Warwick Castle
Where the frame narrator meets the stranger and is given the manuscript.
Winchester
Malory’s own identification of Camelot, and as close as the novel’s capital can be placed on a map.
Glastonbury
The abbey traditionally tied to Arthur’s grave, and the kind of holy site the Valley of Holiness satirises.
Cadbury Castle, Somerset
The hill fort local tradition has called Camelot since the sixteenth century.
