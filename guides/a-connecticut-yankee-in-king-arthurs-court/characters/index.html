A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur’s Court · Mark Twain

Characters

Hank Morgan

The Boss: arms-factory superintendent, and perpetual minister to King Arthur; first appears Chapter I

Practical, funny, humane in his opinions and dangerous in his methods. He can make anything, and the novel’s argument is that a man who can make anything will eventually make the thing that settles the argument for him. He never doubts that he is on the side of progress, including on the last afternoon.

Clarence

A page at Camelot, then the Boss’s chief of staff; first appears Chapter IV

Picked up in the first chapters as a cheerful boy and turned into the one person who understands the whole operation. He runs the newspaper, the telegraph and finally the war, and he is the only character who sees the ending coming: he says so, in the flattest possible terms, and is right.

King Arthur

The king, and later a man in peasant clothes; first appears Chapter III

Absurd when he is on the throne and impressive the moment he is off it. Twain gives him the novel’s one unqualified act of courage in the smallpox hut, and then has him sold in a slave market because he cannot stop walking like a monarch. The satire makes an exception for him, and means it.

Sandy

Demoiselle Alisande la Carteloise, who takes the Boss on a quest; first appears Chapter XI

She talks without pausing for chapters at a time, in sentences borrowed from Malory, and the Boss treats her as a nuisance for half the book. When her castle of captive princesses turns out to be a pigsty she is unembarrassed, and by the end she has become the person he is trying to get home to.

Merlin

The court magician, displaced; first appears Chapter III

A bore with a reputation, whose tricks stop working the moment somebody arrives with blasting powder. He spends the novel being humiliated and the last page winning: the spell that puts the Boss to sleep for thirteen centuries is the only piece of magic in the book that does what it claims.

Morgan le Fay

Arthur’s sister, and the keeper of a private dungeon; first appears Chapter XVI

Beautiful, gracious and casually murderous, she is the novel’s clearest picture of the manners absolute power produces. Her prisoners have been forgotten so thoroughly that some of them cannot say what they were charged with.

Sir Sagramor le Desirous

The knight who challenges the Boss to a duel; first appears Chapter IX

He takes offence at an overheard remark in chapter nine and comes back for satisfaction years later, which is how the tournament system works. His death in the lists is the beginning of the end: it makes the Boss’s methods public, and it puts the whole of chivalry on the other side.

Dowley

A blacksmith in a free village; first appears Chapter XXXI

Prosperous, proud of his wages and unable to follow the Boss’s argument about what those wages buy. Their evening together is the novel’s best scene of political economy, and it ends with a lecture that terrifies the listeners, because talking like that gets men hanged.

Hello-Central

The Boss’s daughter; first appears Chapter XL

Named after the only phrase of his own century he still hears, which is the novel’s saddest joke. Her illness takes her parents to the coast at the moment the Church moves, so the child who exists to show that the Boss has made a life in the sixth century is also the reason he is not there to save it.
