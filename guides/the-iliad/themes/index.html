The anger of Achilles

The poem announces its subject in its first word, and the word means a lasting, almost divine rage, far more than a burst of temper. It is justified at first: Achilles has been publicly robbed. The poem’s interest is in what happens when a just grievance outlives its cause, becomes a pleasure in itself, and is transferred, when Patroclus dies, from a commander to an enemy.

Through the poem Provoked in Book 1, hardened into refusal in Book 9, released on Hector from Book 18, glutted at the river in Book 21 and on Hector’s corpse in Book 22, and exhausted, though never conquered, in Book 24.

In the text

Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought countless ills upon the Achaeans. Many a brave soul did it send hurrying down to Hades, and many a hero did it yield a prey to dogs and vultures, for so were the counsels of Jove fulfilled.

Book 1

The first noun is anger, and the sentence is interested in what it caused: souls sent to Hades, bodies left for dogs and vultures. That last threat matters. Whether a corpse is buried or eaten becomes the question the whole poem turns on, up to the ransoming of Hector. And ‘the counsels of Jove’ are fulfilled through this anger: from the first sentence, a god’s plan and a man’s temper work together.

Therefore, perish strife both from among gods and men, and anger, wherein even a righteous man will harden his heart—which rises up in the soul of a man like smoke, and the taste thereof is sweeter than drops of honey.

Achilles, Book 18

This is the nearest the poem comes to analysing its own subject. Anger is described from the inside as a pleasure (it rises ‘like smoke’ and tastes ‘sweeter than drops of honey’), which is why it is so hard to give up. Achilles renounces his anger against Agamemnon in the same breath as he takes up a far worse one against Hector. He sees the mechanism clearly and cannot step out of it.

Honour, prizes and glory

Honour in the Iliad is concrete. It is a captive, a tripod, the best cut of meat, and a name that lives in songs after the body is gone. That makes it measurable and so confiscable, and the quarrel exposes the system’s weakness. Once Achilles sees that a worse man can take his prize, he starts to ask what glory is worth to someone who will die for it.

Through the poem Taken from Achilles in Book 1, offered back with interest in Book 9 and refused, redefined by Sarpedon in Book 12, and sought by Hector at the last in the only form left to him: a death worth telling.

In the text

I came not warring here for any ill the Trojans had done me. I have no quarrel with them. They have not raided my cattle nor my horses, nor cut down my harvests on the rich plains of Phthia.

Achilles, Book 1

The most subversive thing anyone says in the poem is said in its first book. Achilles has no grievance against Troy; he is there to win satisfaction for Agamemnon and Menelaus, and his reward comes in the form of prizes. Once a prize can be confiscated, the whole contract is exposed. His refusal of the embassy in Book 9 grows out of this speech: if the war is not his quarrel, only honour kept him in it.

Him do I hate even as the gates of hell who says one thing while he hides another in his heart; therefore I will say what I mean.

Achilles, Book 9

Whether or not Achilles guesses what has been left out, he names the fault exactly, and he names it to the one man in the camp who most deserves it. Odysseus’s own poem will celebrate the skill of saying one thing and hiding another. Achilles’ refusal to do so is his integrity, and also his isolation: a man who says only what he means has no way to climb down.

Death and the body

The gods cannot die and so cannot be heroes; men can, and that is the only reason courage means anything. The poem gives every minor warrior a father, a home and a moment of dying, and its great speeches about death come from men who know how little time they have. It cares just as much about what happens afterwards: a body stripped, fought over, left for dogs, or washed and given back for burial.

Through the poem A ‘little season’ in Book 1, leaves on the trees in Book 6, a choice of two deaths in Book 9, a whole book fought over Patroclus’s corpse in Book 17, Hector’s body dragged in Book 22 and ransomed in Book 24.

In the text

you bore me doomed to live but for a little season; surely Jove, who thunders from Olympus, might have made that little glorious. It is not so.

Achilles, Book 1

Achilles’ bargain is a short life in exchange for glory, and here he complains that he is being cheated on the glory. The phrasing is exact: the ‘little season’ is accepted, only the ‘glorious’ is in dispute. It is also the first time the most feared man in the army weeps, and he does it at the water’s edge like a child calling for its mother. The poem never treats his tears as weakness.

Men come and go as leaves year by year upon the trees. Those of autumn the wind sheds upon the ground, but when spring returns the forest buds forth with fresh vines. Even so is it with the generations of mankind, the new spring up as the old are passing away.

Glaucus, Book 6

A warrior asked for his pedigree replies that pedigrees are like leaves, and then gives his at great length. The simile is the poem’s most famous statement of mortality, and a comment on heroic ancestry: what lasts is the kind, and the individual falls. Yet the genealogy that follows saves his life, because his grandfather turns out to have been the guest of Diomedes’ grandfather. The leaves fall; the obligation survives.

Fate and the will of Zeus

The poem opens by saying that the counsels of Zeus were being fulfilled, and it is full of foreknowledge: Thetis knows, Achilles knows, even the horses know. Yet nothing is excused by it. Men choose inside a frame they cannot alter, and the gods, who could sometimes alter it, weigh the fates in golden scales and let them fall.

Through the poem Set in motion by Zeus’s nod in Book 1, weighed in the scales in Books 8 and 22, argued over when Zeus considers saving Sarpedon and Hector, and blamed by Agamemnon for everything in Book 19.

In the text

My mother Thetis tells me that there are two ways in which I may meet my end. If I stay here and fight, I shall not return alive but my name will live for ever: whereas if I go home my name will die, but it will be long ere death shall take me.

Achilles, Book 9

The choice that defines Achilles is set out like a sum, and on this night he chooses the long life. He reverses the choice only when Patroclus dies. The speech shocks because it doubts the arithmetic heroes live by: glory is a name, and a name is worth less than a life that, as he has just said, ‘can neither be bought nor harried back again’. He is the only man at Troy who knows the price for certain.

but it was not I that did it: Jove, and Fate, and Erinys that walks in darkness struck me mad when we were assembled on the day that I took from Achilles the meed that had been awarded to him.

Agamemnon, Book 19

Agamemnon apologises by explaining that he is not to blame, and goes on to tell a long story about Folly deceiving Zeus himself. In the poem’s theology this is more than an excuse: gods do cloud men’s minds. But it is convenient, and it is addressed to an army that has paid for his mistake with its dead. The Odyssey opens with Zeus complaining about exactly this kind of speech.

Home and the city

The Greeks are far from home and the Trojans are fighting in front of theirs, and again and again the poem shows what the war is being fought across: washing-troughs where women did laundry in peacetime, a baby frightened by a helmet, a bath heating for a man already dead. Achilles gives up his homecoming for glory. Hector dies within sight of his.

Through the poem Hector’s visit to the city in Book 6, Achilles’ choice between Phthia and fame in Book 9, the lost peace of the springs in Book 22, and the body brought home through the gates in Book 24.

In the text

He stretched his arms towards his child, but the boy cried and nestled in his nurse’s bosom, scared at the sight of his father’s armour, and at the horse-hair plume that nodded fiercely from his helmet.

Book 6

The one warrior in the poem shown with a child is undone by his own equipment. Everything that makes Hector a hero, the bronze and the plume that ‘nodded fiercely’, is exactly what a baby cannot read. The parents laugh, and Hector takes the helmet off: for a moment the poem shows a man choosing between his two roles and choosing the smaller one. A few lines later he picks the helmet up again.

She told her maids to set a large tripod on the fire, so as to have a warm bath ready for Hector when he came out of battle; poor woman, she knew not that he was now beyond the reach of baths.

Book 22

The poem cuts from the killing to a woman weaving flowers into a purple cloth, and the ordinary kindness of heating water for a tired husband becomes unbearable because we know more than she does. ‘Poor woman’ is the narrator stepping in. The bath matters for another reason: washing the body is the first rite of a funeral, and this body is being dragged through the dust towards the ships.

Hosts, guests and strangers

The war began as a crime against hospitality (Paris was Menelaus’s guest when he took Helen), and the poem’s two moments of peace between enemies both rest on the same code. Glaucus and Diomedes stop fighting when they find their grandfathers were host and guest; Priam comes to Achilles as a suppliant, and Achilles feeds him.

Through the poem Broken before the poem begins, honoured on the battlefield in Book 6, refused at Hector’s death in Book 22, and restored in the tent in Book 24, where the killer becomes the host.

In the text

As when some cruel spite has befallen a man that he should have killed some one in his own country, and must fly to a great man’s protection in a land of strangers, and all marvel who see him, even so did Achilles marvel as he beheld Priam.

Book 24

The simile makes the wrong man a killer. It is Achilles who has killed and Priam who has lost, yet the comparison casts the old king as a murderer seeking refuge in a stranger’s house. It fits someone else exactly: Patroclus, who as a boy killed a playmate over dice and was taken in by Peleus, as his ghost recalls in Book 23. By reversing the roles, the poem makes Achilles see a suppliant, and prepares the recognition between the two men.

Fear, O Achilles, the wrath of heaven; think on your own father and have compassion upon me, who am the more pitiable, for I have steeled myself as no man yet has ever steeled himself before me, and have raised to my lips the hand of him who slew my son.

Priam, Book 24

Priam rests his claim to pity on an act: he has done what no man has done before. The last clause holds the whole horror of the scene: a father’s lips on the hand that killed his son. It also mirrors the poem’s opening, where another old father, Chryses, came to the Greek camp with ransom for his child and was sent roughly away. This time the ransom is taken, and the father is fed.

Prizes, captives and mourners

In a poem about men at war, women are the prizes the quarrel is about, the captives the sack of a city produces, and the mourners who have the last word. The heroes send their wives back to the loom, and the poem follows them there. Andromache, Hecuba, Helen and Briseis speak its most searching lines about what the fighting is for.

Through the poem Briseis led away in Book 1, Helen weaving the war in Book 3, Andromache at the gate in Book 6, the captive women’s double mourning in Book 19, and the three formal laments that end Book 24.

In the text

Small wonder that Trojans and Achaeans should endure so much and so long, for the sake of a woman so marvellously and divinely lovely. Still, fair though she be, let them take her and go, or she will breed sorrow for us and for our children after us.

The elders of Troy, Book 3

The elders say two opposite things in two sentences and mean both. Her beauty explains ten years of war; she should be sent back anyway. It is the most generous verdict on Helen in the poem and it is spoken softly, among themselves. Notice who is judging: men past fighting, sitting on the tower ‘like cicales’, who will see their city burn for a decision nobody on the wall made.

Go, then, within the house, and busy yourself with your daily duties, your loom, your distaff, and the ordering of your servants; for war is man’s matter, and mine above all others of them that have been born in Ilius.

Hector, Book 6

Andromache has just given him sound advice about the weak point in the wall, and he answers by sending her to her loom. The sentence is a formula: the Odyssey gives it almost word for word to Telemachus, with ‘war’ changed to ‘speech’. Here it is tender, since he has just told her not to grieve before her time, but it draws the line the poem holds to throughout: men decide, and women mourn the decisions. In Book 24 the women’s laments are the poem’s last great speeches.
