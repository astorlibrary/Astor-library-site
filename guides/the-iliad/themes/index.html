The Iliad · Homer

Themes

The anger of Achilles

The poem announces its subject in its first word, and the word means a lasting, almost divine rage, far more than a burst of temper. It is justified at first: Achilles has been publicly robbed. The poem’s interest is in what happens when a just grievance outlives its cause, becomes a pleasure in itself, and is transferred, when Patroclus dies, from a commander to an enemy.

Provoked in Book 1, hardened into refusal in Book 9, released on Hector from Book 18, glutted at the river in Book 21 and on Hector’s corpse in Book 22, and exhausted, though never conquered, in Book 24.

Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought countless ills upon the Achaeans. Many a brave soul did it send hurrying down to Hades, and many a hero did it yield a prey to dogs and vultures, for so were the counsels of Jove fulfilled.

Book 1

I came not warring here for any ill the Trojans had done me. I have no quarrel with them. They have not raided my cattle nor my horses, nor cut down my harvests on the rich plains of Phthia.

Achilles, Book 1

Honour, prizes and glory

Honour in the Iliad is concrete. It is a captive, a tripod, the best cut of meat, and a name that lives in songs after the body is gone. That makes it measurable and so confiscable, and the quarrel exposes the system’s weakness. Once Achilles sees that a worse man can take his prize, he starts to ask what glory is worth to someone who will die for it.

Taken from Achilles in Book 1, offered back with interest in Book 9 and refused, redefined by Sarpedon in Book 12, and sought by Hector at the last in the only form left to him: a death worth telling.

I came not warring here for any ill the Trojans had done me. I have no quarrel with them. They have not raided my cattle nor my horses, nor cut down my harvests on the rich plains of Phthia.

Achilles, Book 1

you bore me doomed to live but for a little season; surely Jove, who thunders from Olympus, might have made that little glorious. It is not so.

Death and the body

The gods cannot die and so cannot be heroes; men can, and that is the only reason courage means anything. The poem gives every minor warrior a father, a home and a moment of dying, and its great speeches about death come from men who know how little time they have. It cares just as much about what happens afterwards: a body stripped, fought over, left for dogs, or washed and given back for burial.

A ‘little season’ in Book 1, leaves on the trees in Book 6, a choice of two deaths in Book 9, a whole book fought over Patroclus’s corpse in Book 17, Hector’s body dragged in Book 22 and ransomed in Book 24.

Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought countless ills upon the Achaeans. Many a brave soul did it send hurrying down to Hades, and many a hero did it yield a prey to dogs and vultures, for so were the counsels of Jove fulfilled.

Fate and the will of Zeus

The poem opens by saying that the counsels of Zeus were being fulfilled, and it is full of foreknowledge: Thetis knows, Achilles knows, even the horses know. Yet nothing is excused by it. Men choose inside a frame they cannot alter, and the gods, who could sometimes alter it, weigh the fates in golden scales and let them fall.

Set in motion by Zeus’s nod in Book 1, weighed in the scales in Books 8 and 22, argued over when Zeus considers saving Sarpedon and Hector, and blamed by Agamemnon for everything in Book 19.

Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought countless ills upon the Achaeans. Many a brave soul did it send hurrying down to Hades, and many a hero did it yield a prey to dogs and vultures, for so were the counsels of Jove fulfilled.

Book 1

it is you who bear the brunt of that toil that has been caused by my hateful self and by the sin of Alexandrus—both of whom Jove has doomed to be a theme of song among those that shall be born hereafter.

Helen, Book 6

Home and the city

The Greeks are far from home and the Trojans are fighting in front of theirs, and again and again the poem shows what the war is being fought across: washing-troughs where women did laundry in peacetime, a baby frightened by a helmet, a bath heating for a man already dead. Achilles gives up his homecoming for glory. Hector dies within sight of his.

Hector’s visit to the city in Book 6, Achilles’ choice between Phthia and fame in Book 9, the lost peace of the springs in Book 22, and the body brought home through the gates in Book 24.

Small wonder that Trojans and Achaeans should endure so much and so long, for the sake of a woman so marvellously and divinely lovely. Still, fair though she be, let them take her and go, or she will breed sorrow for us and for our children after us.

The elders of Troy, Book 3

He stretched his arms towards his child, but the boy cried and nestled in his nurse’s bosom, scared at the sight of his father’s armour, and at the horse-hair plume that nodded fiercely from his helmet.

Book 6

Hosts, guests and strangers

The war began as a crime against hospitality (Paris was Menelaus’s guest when he took Helen), and the poem’s two moments of peace between enemies both rest on the same code. Glaucus and Diomedes stop fighting when they find their grandfathers were host and guest; Priam comes to Achilles as a suppliant, and Achilles feeds him.

Broken before the poem begins, honoured on the battlefield in Book 6, refused at Hector’s death in Book 22, and restored in the tent in Book 24, where the killer becomes the host.

Men come and go as leaves year by year upon the trees. Those of autumn the wind sheds upon the ground, but when spring returns the forest buds forth with fresh vines. Even so is it with the generations of mankind, the new spring up as the old are passing away.

Glaucus, Book 6

As when some cruel spite has befallen a man that he should have killed some one in his own country, and must fly to a great man’s protection in a land of strangers, and all marvel who see him, even so did Achilles marvel as he beheld Priam.

Book 24

Prizes, captives and mourners

In a poem about men at war, women are the prizes the quarrel is about, the captives the sack of a city produces, and the mourners who have the last word. The heroes send their wives back to the loom, and the poem follows them there. Andromache, Hecuba, Helen and Briseis speak its most searching lines about what the fighting is for.

Briseis led away in Book 1, Helen weaving the war in Book 3, Andromache at the gate in Book 6, the captive women’s double mourning in Book 19, and the three formal laments that end Book 24.
