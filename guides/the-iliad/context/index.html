The Iliad · Homer

Context

Dates

1184 BC
The traditional date for the fall of Troy Calculated centuries later by the Greek scholar Eratosthenes from the genealogies of legend. It is not a record, though it falls close to a destruction that archaeologists date to around 1180 BCE at Hisarlik. The poem foresees the fall and never narrates it.
725 BC
The Iliad takes something like its present form A scholarly estimate: probably the second half of the eighth century BCE, perhaps a little later, and probably somewhat before the Odyssey. It grew out of generations of sung poetry about the war.
334 BC
Alexander the Great at Troy Crossing into Asia, Alexander is said by his ancient biographers to have sacrificed at Ilium and honoured the tomb of Achilles, and to have carried a copy of the Iliad on campaign.
19 BC
Virgil dies, leaving the Aeneid The second half of Rome’s epic replays the Iliad in Italy, with a Trojan now in the invader’s role and a local prince, Turnus, dying at the end as Hector did.
1488
Homer printed for the first time The first printed edition of the Greek text of both epics appeared in Florence, edited by Demetrius Chalcondyles.
1611
Chapman’s Iliad completed George Chapman published seven books of the Iliad in 1598 and the whole poem in English verse around 1611. It is the translation Keats would read two centuries later.
1720
Pope’s Iliad completed Alexander Pope’s version in heroic couplets appeared in six volumes from 1715 and made his fortune. For a century it was the Iliad most English readers knew.
1816
Keats’s sonnet on Chapman’s Homer ‘On First Looking into Chapman’s Homer’, written in October 1816, compares first reading Homer in Chapman’s English to sighting a new planet or an unknown ocean.
1835
Samuel Butler, translator of this edition, born The translator was born at Langar in Nottinghamshire, a clergyman’s son. Before he turned to Homer he farmed sheep in New Zealand, painted, wrote the satire Erewhon and argued in print against Darwin’s account of evolution.
1871
Schliemann digs at Hisarlik Heinrich Schliemann began large-scale excavation of the mound that Frank Calvert, who owned part of it, had already identified as Troy. The site holds many cities one above another, and which of them, if any, is Homer’s is still argued.
1898
Butler’s prose Iliad published Rendered, as the title page says, ‘for the use of those who cannot read the original’, in the plain prose of Butler’s own day. His Odyssey followed in 1900, and he died in 1902.
1924
Scholars link Hittite Wilusa with Troy In the mid-1920s scholars reading Hittite diplomatic texts proposed that Wilusa, a kingdom in western Anatolia, was Homer’s Ilios, and that its ruler Alaksandu bore the name Alexandros, which is Paris’s other name. The identification is widely accepted; what it proves about a Trojan war is not.
1928
Milman Parry’s study of the Homeric epithet Parry showed that phrases like ‘Hector tamer of horses’ form a system fitted to the metre, of the kind a poet composing aloud would need. Fieldwork among singers in Yugoslavia in the 1930s supported the case that Homeric verse grew out of oral performance.

Places

Troy (Hisarlik)
The mound at Hisarlik in north-west Turkey, generally accepted as the site of Troy, a city rebuilt layer upon layer over more than three thousand years. The poem rarely goes inside the walls, but when it does (to Priam’s palace, to the Scaean gate) it is the only city we are shown from within.
The Hellespont (Dardanelles)
The strait between Europe and Asia, a few miles from Troy. The Greek ships are drawn up on the shore near its mouth; Achilles threatens to be rowing out on it by morning in Book 9, and Hector pictures a champion’s barrow beside it in Book 7.
Mount Ida (Kaz Dağı)
The mountain range south-east of Troy, ‘many-fountained Ida’ in Butler. Zeus watches the battle from its highest peak, Gargarus, and it is there that Hera lulls him to sleep in Book 14.
Samothrace
The mountainous island in the northern Aegean from whose heights Poseidon watches the battle in Book 13; from there, the poem says, he could see Ida, Priam’s city and the Greek ships.
Lemnos
The island where Hephaestus landed when Zeus threw him out of heaven, as he tells it in Book 1, and where Achilles sold Lycaon before meeting him again at the river in Book 21. Ships from Lemnos bring wine to the Greek camp.
Mycenae
Agamemnon’s citadel in the Argolid, ‘the strong city of Mycenae’ in the catalogue of ships. Schliemann dug there in 1876; the gold from its shaft graves is centuries older than any date proposed for a Trojan war.
Xanthos (Lycia)
The ancient city on the Lycian river Xanthus in south-west Turkey. Sarpedon and Glaucus lead the Lycians ‘by the eddying waters of the Xanthus’, and the estate Sarpedon describes in Book 12 lies on its banks. It is not to be confused with Troy’s own river, which the gods also call Xanthus.
