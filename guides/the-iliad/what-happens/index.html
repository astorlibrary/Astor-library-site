The Iliad · Homer

What happens

Books 1–4 The quarrel and the armies

The whole disaster begins in a public meeting, with a priest’s daughter, a plague and an insult. Achilles withdraws and his mother wins Zeus’s promise to punish the Greeks. The next three books show the war he has left: an army nearly talked into going home, a duel meant to settle everything that settles nothing, and a truce broken by a single arrow.

Book 1
Agamemnon must return Chryseis to end Apollo’s plague, takes Briseis from Achilles instead, and Achilles withdraws; Thetis wins Zeus’s nod.
Book 2
Zeus sends Agamemnon a false dream; a test of the army’s nerve becomes a stampede for the ships; Odysseus beats Thersites; the forces are listed.
Book 3
Helen’s two husbands fight a duel for her, watched from the wall by Priam and Helen; Aphrodite snatches Paris away to his bedroom.
Book 4
Athene persuades Pandarus to shoot Menelaus; Agamemnon goes round his captains, and the armies close.

Books 5–8 The war without Achilles

With the best fighter sitting out, the poem finds out what the others are made of. Diomedes wounds two gods in a day. Hector goes back into the city and meets, one after another, the women he is fighting for. Then Zeus begins to keep his promise, and by nightfall the Trojans are camped on the plain within sight of the ships.

Book 5
Athene fills Diomedes with strength; he wounds Aphrodite as she rescues Aeneas, and later wounds Ares himself.
Book 6
Glaucus and Diomedes find their grandfathers were guest-friends and exchange armour; Hector sees his mother, Helen and Paris, then Andromache and their son at the Scaean gate.
Book 7
A duel is broken off at nightfall with an exchange of gifts; both sides bury their dead, and the Greeks build a wall and trench round their ships.
Book 8
Zeus forbids the gods to interfere, weighs the two armies’ fates in his scales, and drives the Greeks behind their wall.

Books 9–12 The embassy and the wall

Agamemnon admits his mistake and offers a fortune to have Achilles back, and Achilles turns it down in a speech that questions the whole bargain of heroic life. Without him the Greek leaders are wounded one after another, Patroclus is sent on an errand that will lead to his death, and Hector smashes his way through the wall.

Book 9
Odysseus, Phoenix and Ajax carry Agamemnon’s offer of gifts; Achilles refuses and threatens to sail for home in the morning.
Book 10
Odysseus and Diomedes go out as spies, catch the Trojan scout Dolon, kill him, and steal the horses of the Thracian king Rhesus.
Book 11
Agamemnon, Diomedes and Odysseus are wounded in turn; Achilles sends Patroclus to ask after a wounded man, and Nestor urges Patroclus to fight in Achilles’ armour.
Book 12
Sarpedon tells Glaucus why princes must fight in front; Hector breaks the gate with a stone and the Trojans pour through.

Books 13–16 Battle at the ships

The fighting reaches the beach and the gods break Zeus’s orders to join it: Poseidon rallies the Greeks, and Hera puts Zeus to sleep by seducing him. When he wakes, the Trojans are driven on to the ships with fire. Only then does Achilles let Patroclus go out in his armour, and the substitute does too well.

Book 13
Poseidon, watching from Samothrace, comes down to rally the Greeks; Idomeneus leads the defence while Hector presses on.
Book 14
Hera borrows Aphrodite’s embroidered girdle, seduces Zeus on Mount Ida and sends him to sleep; Ajax fells Hector with a stone.
Book 15
Zeus sends Apollo to revive Hector; the Trojans reach the ships, and Ajax fights them off from the decks.
Book 16
Patroclus drives the Trojans back and kills Sarpedon, son of Zeus; Apollo strips him of his armour, and Euphorbus and Hector kill him.

Books 17–20 Grief, armour and return

The poem slows down around a body. Greeks and Trojans fight all day over Patroclus’s corpse; the news reaches Achilles, who pours dust over his head; his mother goes to Hephaestus for new armour, and the shield is described at length before a blow is struck with it. The reconciliation with Agamemnon, when it comes, is brisk, because nobody now cares about the gifts.

Book 17
Menelaus and Ajax defend Patroclus’s corpse; Achilles’ immortal horses stand weeping in the battle; Hector wears Achilles’ armour.
Book 18
Achilles hears the news and chooses death; his shout from the trench throws the Trojans into panic; Hephaestus makes the shield.
Book 19
Agamemnon blames Zeus and Folly for the quarrel; Briseis mourns Patroclus; Achilles’ horse Xanthus foretells his death.
Book 20
Zeus lets the gods join the fighting; Poseidon snatches Aeneas away from Achilles because he is fated to survive.

Books 21–24 Revenge and ransom

Achilles fills the river with bodies until the river itself fights him, then kills Hector under the walls and drags the corpse behind his chariot. Two whole books remain. The funeral games for Patroclus hand out prizes and settle disputes without bloodshed; then Priam crosses the Greek camp at night to ransom his son, and the poem ends with a funeral in Troy.

Book 21
Achilles kills Lycaon and chokes the Scamander with the dead; the river rises against him until Hephaestus burns it back; the gods fight one another.
Book 22
Hector waits outside the gates, runs three times round the walls, is tricked by Athene in the shape of his brother, and dies; Achilles drags the body away.
Book 23
Patroclus’s ghost asks for burial; twelve Trojan captives die on his pyre; Achilles presides over races and contests and keeps the peace among the winners.
Book 24
Hermes leads Priam to Achilles’ tent; the two men weep together, eat, and agree a truce for Hector’s funeral.
