The Iliad · Homer

Characters

Achilles

Son of Peleus and the sea-goddess Thetis, the best fighter at Troy; first appears Book 1

He knows two things the other heroes can only suspect: that he will die young if he stays, and that the honour he is dying for can be taken from him by a worse man with a bigger army. The poem is about what that knowledge does to him. His anger is justified in Book 1, excessive by Book 9, catastrophic in Book 16 and monstrous in Book 22, and it ends in a meal shared with his enemy’s father.

Hector

Eldest son of Priam and the chief defender of Troy; first appears Book 2

The poem’s most sympathetic fighter is on the losing side, and it gives him what it never gives Achilles: a wife, a baby and a city of people who depend on him. He knows Troy will fall and fights anyway, out of shame and without hope. His judgement slips as his success grows. He overrules sound advice, puts on the armour he stripped from Patroclus, and when Achilles finally comes for him, he runs.

Agamemnon

King of Mycenae and commander of the Greek army; first appears Book 1

He holds the largest command and the least authority, and knows it: many of his speeches are attempts to make others acknowledge a rank that ought to speak for itself. He takes Briseis to prove that he is the stronger and proves the opposite. His apology in Book 19 blames Zeus, Fate and Folly, which is true to the poem’s theology and convenient for him. On the battlefield, to be fair, he is brave.

Patroclus

Achilles’ companion and squire, son of Menoetius; first appears Book 1

He is the gentlest man in the Greek camp (Briseis remembers him as the one who told her not to weep), and he is the one who dies for Achilles’ anger. Sent out in borrowed armour to frighten the Trojans away from the ships, he forgets his orders and chases them to the walls. The narrator speaks to him directly as he goes, as if he could still be warned.

Priam

King of Troy, father of fifty sons; first appears Book 3

Too old to fight, he watches the war from the walls and loses most of his sons to it. He refuses to blame Helen. In the last book he does what nobody in the poem has dared to do: crosses the enemy camp at night, with only a herald and a disguised god, and kisses the hands that killed Hector. His courage is the poem’s last and least warlike kind.

Andromache

Wife of Hector, daughter of Eetion of Thebe; first appears Book 6

Everything she has lost, she has lost to Achilles, who killed her father and seven brothers in one day. She appears three times, and each time the poem stops for her. She tells Hector where the wall is weakest, and he sends her back to her loom; she heats his bath while he dies; she leads the lament over his body, and the lament is mostly about what will now happen to their son.

Helen

Wife of Menelaus, taken to Troy by Paris; first appears Book 3

The cause of the war speaks of herself with a disgust nobody else voices, wishes a whirlwind had carried her off at birth, and has no illusions about Paris. She alone seems to know that she is living inside a story that will be sung. She is also the poem’s last mourner: her lament for Hector remembers him as the one Trojan who was never unkind to her.

Paris (Butler's Alexandrus)

Son of Priam, whose seizure of Helen began the war; first appears Book 3

Beautiful, capable and absent whenever it matters. He challenges the best of the Greeks and then shrinks from Menelaus, and Aphrodite lifts him out of the duel to his bed. The poem is fairer to him than his reputation: he takes Hector’s rebukes gracefully, fights well enough when he chooses, and is named in Hector’s dying words as the man who will kill Achilles.

Thetis

A sea-goddess, mother of Achilles; first appears Book 1

She was married to a mortal ‘sorely against my will’, as she tells Hephaestus, and she knows her son must die young. Every time Achilles weeps she rises out of the sea to comfort him, and every time she can do nothing about what is really wrong. It is her plea that turns Zeus against the Greeks in Book 1, and her errand to Hephaestus that arms her son for the death she has just foretold.

Zeus (Butler's Jove)

King of the gods; first appears Book 1

He has promised Thetis to honour Achilles, and he keeps his word, though it costs thousands of lives. He also feels pity (for Hector, for his own son Sarpedon, for Achilles’ horses), and in each case lets the pity go. His golden scales weigh out deaths he could apparently prevent and chooses not to. The poem’s highest power is capable of sympathy and unwilling to spend it.

Odysseus (Butler's Ulysses)

King of Ithaca, the Greeks’ best speaker; first appears Book 1

Before he has a poem of his own he is already recognisably himself: he stops a mutiny with a sceptre across Thersites’ back, leads the embassy to Achilles, goes spying by night, and reminds the army that men must eat before they fight. Achilles’ reply to him in Book 9 (that he hates a man who says one thing and hides another in his heart) is aimed at exactly the skill the Odyssey will celebrate.

Briseis

A captive woman, Achilles’ prize, taken from him by Agamemnon; first appears Book 1

The woman the quarrel is about is led away ‘loth to go’ in Book 1 and speaks only once, in Book 19, over Patroclus’s body. Her lament tells us what the heroes never mention: that Achilles killed her husband and three brothers when he sacked her city, and that Patroclus promised to make Achilles marry her. She is a prize to the men and a widow to herself.
