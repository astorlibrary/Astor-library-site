The Iliad · Homer

Form and language

Invocation

An opening address to a muse or god, asking for the song and announcing its subject.

The goddess is asked to sing ‘the anger of Achilles’, and the invocation plunges straight into the tenth year of the war with the quarrel already under way. Nothing of the ten years before is narrated. The poem calls on the Muses again before the catalogue of ships in Book 2, and at later crises, when what it needs is names and numbers, more than inspiration.

Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought countless ills upon the Achaeans. Many a brave soul did it send hurrying down to Hades, and many a hero did it yield a prey to dogs and vultures, for so were the counsels of Jove fulfilled.

Book 1

Epithets and formulae

Fixed descriptive phrases attached to a person or thing and repeated whenever they recur, a working tool of poetry composed in performance.

Achilles is “son of Peleus”, Hector “tamer of horses”, Dawn “rosy-fingered”, and whole scenes (sacrifice, arming, a warrior’s soul leaving his body) recur almost word for word. The lines for a soul leaving the body, flying to Hades to mourn “its sad fate” and the youth it has lost, are used for Patroclus and again for Hector, so the second death is heard through the first.

Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles son of Peleus, that brought countless ills upon the Achaeans. Many a brave soul did it send hurrying down to Hades, and many a hero did it yield a prey to dogs and vultures, for so were the counsels of Jove fulfilled.

Book 1

Extended simile

A comparison developed over several lines until it becomes a small scene of its own, typically closed with ‘even so’.

The Iliad’s similes carry the reader out of the battle into the world the battle is not: shepherds, harvests, children, storms, a woman weighing wool to earn her children’s bread. They are also where the poem thinks hardest. Glaucus compares men to leaves; the arrival of Priam in Book 24 is compared, very strangely, to the arrival of a murderer.

Men come and go as leaves year by year upon the trees. Those of autumn the wind sheds upon the ground, but when spring returns the forest buds forth with fresh vines. Even so is it with the generations of mankind, the new spring up as the old are passing away.

Glaucus, Book 6

Apostrophe

A speaker or narrator turning away from the audience to address someone, usually absent or unable to answer, as ‘you’.

Homer’s narrator occasionally speaks to a character directly, and in Book 16 he does it again and again to Patroclus (“O knight Patroclus”) as the young man goes to his death. The effect is of a narrator who knows what is coming and cannot stop it, which is exactly the position of the audience.

but as he was coming on like a god for a time, then, O Patroclus, was the hour of your end approaching, for Phoebus fought you in fell earnest.

Book 16

Prophecy and foreknowledge

Statements about the future that shape how the present is read, whether they come from gods, seers or the characters’ own fears.

Almost nothing in the Iliad comes as a surprise. Achilles knows the terms of his life from Book 1; Hector knows Troy will fall; Patroclus, dying, predicts Hector’s death, and Hector, dying, predicts Achilles’. Suspense is replaced by dread, and attention moves from what will happen to how people behave knowing it will.

you bore me doomed to live but for a little season; surely Jove, who thunders from Olympus, might have made that little glorious. It is not so.

Achilles, Book 1

Dramatic irony

The audience knows something that a character speaking or acting does not.

The poem’s audience knows the ending of the war and most of its heroes’ deaths, and the characters mostly speak without that knowledge. Hector bargains in Book 7 for the return of a dead champion’s body, never imagining his own; Andromache heats water for a man already dead.

Then will one say hereafter as he sails his ship over the sea, ‘This is the monument of one who died long since a champion who was slain by mighty Hector.’ Thus will one say, and my fame shall not be lost.

Hector, Book 7

Symbolic objects

Physical things that the narrative returns to until they stand for a relationship or an idea.

Achilles’ armour passes from his body to Patroclus’s to Hector’s, so that in Book 22 Achilles is in effect fighting his own old self. The sceptre he swears by and throws down in Book 1, stripped of leaf and bark and never to sprout again, is the authority of kings he is about to defy. The new shield carries cities at peace into battle, and Hector’s helmet frightens his son.

He stretched his arms towards his child, but the boy cried and nestled in his nurse’s bosom, scared at the sight of his father’s armour, and at the horse-hair plume that nodded fiercely from his helmet.

Book 6

Mirrored scenes

Scenes built to echo one another, so that the second is read through the first and the difference between them carries the meaning.

The poem begins with an old father, Chryses, coming to the Greek camp with a ransom for his child and being sent roughly away, and ends with an old father, Priam, coming with a ransom for his son and being fed. The quarrel over a prize in Book 1 is answered by the fairly judged prizes of the funeral games in Book 23.

Then will one say hereafter as he sails his ship over the sea, ‘This is the monument of one who died long since a champion who was slain by mighty Hector.’ Thus will one say, and my fame shall not be lost.

Hector, Book 7
