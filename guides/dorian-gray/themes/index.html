The Picture of Dorian Gray · Oscar Wilde

Themes

Beauty and surface

The preface announces that those who go beneath the surface do so at their peril, and the plot then buries the truth under a cloth in a locked room. Everyone in the novel makes moral judgements from faces, and everyone is wrong. What the book shows is that people would rather appearances deceived them.

A face admired in a studio, a mouth that changes overnight, a murder prevented by looking twenty, and a body identified only by its rings.

All art is at once surface and symbol. Those who go beneath the surface do so at their peril. Those who read the symbol do so at their peril.

Preface

I know you will laugh at me,” he replied, “but I really can’t exhibit it. I have put too much of myself into it.

Basil Hallward, Chapter I

Influence

Lord Henry claims that all influence is immoral because it stops a person being themselves, and immediately sets about influencing a young man of twenty. The novel takes him seriously enough to test the claim: Dorian’s whole life afterwards is an echo of somebody else’s music, which is the objection Lord Henry himself raised.

A sermon in a garden in Chapter II, a book sent as a present in Chapter X, and a final conversation in which the teacher denies that anything he said could have had an effect.

There is no such thing as a good influence, Mr. Gray. All influence is immoral⁠—immoral from the scientific point of view.

Lord Henry Wotton, Chapter II

The aim of life is self-development. To realize one’s nature perfectly⁠—that is what each of us is here for.

Lord Henry Wotton, Chapter II

Art and morality

The preface insists that a book cannot be moral or immoral and that art has no ethical sympathies. The plot then hands Dorian a novel that changes his life and a painting that keeps his moral accounts. Wilde tells a story whose events contradict its own opening statement, and never settles the disagreement.

Aphorisms in the preface, a yellow book in Chapter X, a man poisoned by it in Chapter XI, and Lord Henry’s flat denial in Chapter XIX that such a thing is possible.

There is no such thing as a moral or an immoral book. Books are well written, or badly written. That is all.

Preface

All art is at once surface and symbol. Those who go beneath the surface do so at their peril. Those who read the symbol do so at their peril.

Preface

The double life

Dorian goes from a Mayfair dinner to a den near the docks in the same evening and is received in both. The doubling is spatial as much as moral (a house with a locked upper room, a city with a respectable west and an unwatched east), and he describes the arrangement itself as a pleasure.

A locked schoolroom in Chapter X, the calm demeanour noted in Chapter XV, and his own house at Selby, where the hunted man keeps to his room for two days.

I have grown to love secrecy. It seems to be the one thing that can make modern life mysterious or marvellous to us. The commonest thing is delightful if one only hides it.

Basil Hallward, Chapter I

Nothing can cure the soul but the senses, just as nothing can cure the senses but the soul.

Lord Henry Wotton, Chapter II

Youth and time

Lord Henry’s opening speech is about the shortness of youth, and Dorian’s wish is a refusal to be billed for time passing. What the novel shows is that the time still happens: eighteen years go by in one chapter of catalogues, and the man who stopped ageing has also stopped accumulating a life.

A warning in a garden, a wish beside an easel, an inventory in Chapter XI, and a withered dead man found in evening dress.

But beauty, real beauty, ends where an intellectual expression begins.

Lord Henry Wotton, Chapter I

If it were I who was to be always young, and the picture that was to grow old! For that⁠—for that⁠—I would give everything! Yes, there is nothing in the whole world I would not give! I would give my soul for that!

Dorian Gray, Chapter II

Conscience and confession

Dorian keeps trying to confess and keeps being prevented: by Lord Henry’s amusement, by Basil’s death, by his own vanity about how the story will sound. The portrait offers him an exact account of himself at any hour, which is what a conscience is, and he responds by locking it upstairs and visiting when the mood takes him.

A prayer in front of the canvas in Chapter XIII, a confession offered as gossip in Chapter XIX, and a last look for improvement that finds only cunning.

The aim of life is self-development. To realize one’s nature perfectly⁠—that is what each of us is here for.

Lord Henry Wotton, Chapter II

The only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it.

Lord Henry Wotton, Chapter II
