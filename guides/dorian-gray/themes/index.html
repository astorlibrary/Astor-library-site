Beauty and surface

The preface announces that those who go beneath the surface do so at their peril, and the plot then buries the truth under a cloth in a locked room. Everyone in the novel makes moral judgements from faces, and everyone is wrong. What the book shows is that people would rather appearances deceived them.

Through the book A face admired in a studio, a mouth that changes overnight, a murder prevented by looking twenty, and a body identified only by its rings.

In the text

But beauty, real beauty, ends where an intellectual expression begins.

Lord Henry Wotton, Chapter I

A joke about clever men’s faces becomes Dorian’s rule: to stay beautiful is to stay unmarked by thought.

It is the real Dorian Gray⁠—that is all.

Lord Henry Wotton, Chapter II

Lord Henry means it as a shrug about temperament, and it turns out to describe the painting on the easel.

Lying on the floor was a dead man, in evening dress, with a knife in his heart. He was withered, wrinkled, and loathsome of visage. It was not till they had examined the rings that they recognized who it was.

Chapter XX

The dead man is known by his rings, because the face everyone judged him by is no longer evidence of anything.

Influence

Lord Henry claims that all influence is immoral because it stops a person being themselves, and immediately sets about influencing a young man of twenty. The novel takes him seriously enough to test the claim: Dorian’s whole life afterwards is an echo of somebody else’s music, which is the objection Lord Henry himself raised.

Through the book A sermon in a garden in Chapter II, a book sent as a present in Chapter X, and a final conversation in which the teacher denies that anything he said could have had an effect.

In the text

There is no such thing as a good influence, Mr. Gray. All influence is immoral⁠—immoral from the scientific point of view.

Lord Henry Wotton, Chapter II

Lord Henry establishes his authority by disclaiming it, and then sets about influencing a young man of twenty.

The only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it.

Lord Henry Wotton, Chapter II

Built to sound like a cure, the sentence is a licence, and the novel spends twenty chapters testing it.

Yes, Dorian, you will always be fond of me. I represent to you all the sins you have never had the courage to commit.

Lord Henry Wotton, Chapter VI

Lord Henry boasts of being a spectator: Dorian will commit the sins, and he will hear about them as anecdotes.

Art and morality

The preface insists that a book cannot be moral or immoral and that art has no ethical sympathies. The plot then hands Dorian a novel that changes his life and a painting that keeps his moral accounts. Wilde tells a story whose events contradict its own opening statement, and never settles the disagreement.

Through the book Aphorisms in the preface, a yellow book in Chapter X, a man poisoned by it in Chapter XI, and Lord Henry’s flat denial in Chapter XIX that such a thing is possible.

In the text

There is no such thing as a moral or an immoral book. Books are well written, or badly written. That is all.

Preface

The preface says a book cannot be moral or immoral. In Chapter XI a book poisons the hero.

Dorian Gray had been poisoned by a book.

Chapter XI

Seven words flatly contradict the preface, and Wilde lets both stand.

I know you will laugh at me,” he replied, “but I really can’t exhibit it. I have put too much of myself into it.

Basil Hallward, Chapter I

Basil thinks he is confessing to bad art and is confessing to love: a portrait can hold someone other than the sitter.

The double life

Dorian goes from a Mayfair dinner to a den near the docks in the same evening and is received in both. The doubling is spatial as much as moral (a house with a locked upper room, a city with a respectable west and an unwatched east), and he describes the arrangement itself as a pleasure.

Through the book A locked schoolroom in Chapter X, the calm demeanour noted in Chapter XV, and his own house at Selby, where the hunted man keeps to his room for two days.

In the text

I have grown to love secrecy. It seems to be the one thing that can make modern life mysterious or marvellous to us. The commonest thing is delightful if one only hides it.

Basil Hallward, Chapter I

Concealment as a refinement of pleasure, stated in the first chapter by the man who will be killed for wanting things brought into the open.

Nothing can cure the soul but the senses, just as nothing can cure the senses but the soul.

Lord Henry Wotton, Chapter II

Dorian repeats Lord Henry’s balanced sentence to himself sixteen chapters later, on his way to an opium den.

He himself could not help wondering at the calm of his demeanour, and for a moment felt keenly the terrible pleasure of a double life.

Chapter XV

Dorian admires his own composure from outside, as the portrait lets him do. It is connoisseurship, not remorse.

Youth and time

Lord Henry’s opening speech is about the shortness of youth, and Dorian’s wish is a refusal to be billed for time passing. What the novel shows is that the time still happens: eighteen years go by in one chapter of catalogues, and the man who stopped ageing has also stopped accumulating a life.

Through the book A warning in a garden, a wish beside an easel, an inventory in Chapter XI, and a withered dead man found in evening dress.

In the text

If it were I who was to be always young, and the picture that was to grow old! For that⁠—for that⁠—I would give everything! Yes, there is nothing in the whole world I would not give! I would give my soul for that!

Dorian Gray, Chapter II

The most consequential sentence in the novel is a tantrum in front of a canvas: no devil, no contract, no witness who understands.

You are the type of what the age is searching for, and what it is afraid it has found.

Lord Henry Wotton, Chapter XIX

Lord Henry admits, without noticing, that the age which wants a life of sensation is afraid of what it has found.

Conscience and confession

Dorian keeps trying to confess and keeps being prevented: by Lord Henry’s amusement, by Basil’s death, by his own vanity about how the story will sound. The portrait offers him an exact account of himself at any hour, which is what a conscience is, and he responds by locking it upstairs and visiting when the mood takes him.

Through the book A prayer in front of the canvas in Chapter XIII, a confession offered as gossip in Chapter XIX, and a last look for improvement that finds only cunning.

In the text

It is the face of my soul.

Dorian Gray, Chapter XIII

Asked to deny the rumours, Dorian shows Basil the painting: a confession in the only terms he still understands.

Each of us has heaven and hell in him, Basil

Dorian Gray, Chapter XIII

The one thing Dorian says that Lord Henry could not have taught him, and within a page he has stabbed the man he said it to.

The soul is a terrible reality. It can be bought, and sold, and bartered away. It can be poisoned, or made perfect.

Dorian Gray, Chapter XIX

The pupil corrects the teacher with the commercial verbs he was taught, and Lord Henry laughs.
