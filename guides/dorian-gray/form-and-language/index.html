The Picture of Dorian Gray · Oscar Wilde

Form and language

Epigram and paradox

A short, polished statement that inverts an expected idea in order to be memorable.

Lord Henry speaks almost entirely in them, and the novel’s method is to let a line that works brilliantly in a drawing room be acted on by somebody who takes it literally.

There is no such thing as a moral or an immoral book. Books are well written, or badly written. That is all.

Preface

The portrait as symbol

An object made to carry the meaning that characters cannot state directly.

The canvas keeps the record: cruelty at the mouth, blood on the hands, cunning in the eyes. It is a conscience with a frame, and it is the only thing in the book Dorian cannot argue with.

I know you will laugh at me,” he replied, “but I really can’t exhibit it. I have put too much of myself into it.

Basil Hallward, Chapter I

The Faustian pact

A bargain in which something is granted at a price the taker does not fully understand.

Wilde gives no devil, no contract and no witness: only a wish said aloud in a studio, which is granted and then never explained by anybody.

If it were I who was to be always young, and the picture that was to grow old! For that⁠—for that⁠—I would give everything! Yes, there is nothing in the whole world I would not give! I would give my soul for that!

Dorian Gray, Chapter II

Catalogue

A long list of objects or names used in place of narrative.

Chapter XI replaces eighteen years with embroideries, jewels, church vestments and Dorian’s ancestors. There are no scenes, because a life spent collecting leaves nothing that can be told as a story.

Dorian Gray had been poisoned by a book.

Chapter XI

Free indirect style

Third-person narration that slips into a character’s own vocabulary and self-justification.

Wilde narrates from inside Dorian’s head and on his side, so that a reader has to notice for themselves when the language of exquisite sensation is describing something squalid.

Harry! Sibyl Vane is sacred!

Dorian Gray, Chapter IV

Aestheticist description

Dense, sensuous writing about surfaces, colours, scents and materials.

The novel opens on roses, lilac and thorn before a person is mentioned, and it returns to perfumes and fabrics whenever the moral pressure rises; the style itself is a way of looking away.

It was a poisonous book. The heavy odour of incense seemed to cling about its pages and to trouble the brain.

Chapter X
