The Picture of Dorian Gray · Oscar Wilde

Characters

Dorian Gray

A young man of fortune with an unusually beautiful face; first appears Chapter I

He is just over twenty, orphaned, rich and almost characterless when the book starts, which is exactly why Lord Henry can write on him. What he acquires over eighteen years is not wickedness so much as the habit of treating other people as experiences, and the one thing the novel never lets him have is a consequence he can see.

Lord Henry Wotton

A titled talker with no occupation; first appears Chapter I

He supplies the theory, takes none of the risk, and is the only major character alive and unmarked at the end. His paradoxes are made to be quoted and never acted on, and the novel’s joke is that the one person who lives by them is the one person Lord Henry never expected to listen.

Basil Hallward

The painter of the portrait; first appears Chapter I

He will not exhibit the picture because it shows the feeling of the man who made it more than the face of the man who sat for it, the most honest statement about art in the book. His worship is real and useless: he is the only person who tries to ask Dorian a direct moral question, and he is killed for it.

Sibyl Vane

An actress at a small theatre in Holborn; first appears Chapter IV

She is Imogen one night and Juliet the next, and what Dorian loves is the succession of heroines. When real feeling makes her a bad actress she becomes worthless to him, and the novel’s sharpest irony is that her sincerity is what he punishes.

James Vane

Sibyl’s brother, a sailor; first appears Chapter V

He leaves for Australia swearing to kill the gentleman if anything happens to his sister, and returns years later as the one force in the novel that cannot be talked round. He is beaten by the same thing that has protected Dorian throughout: a face too young to belong to the man he is looking for.

Alan Campbell

A chemist, once an intimate friend; first appears Chapter XIV

He arrives certain that nothing will make him help and leaves having agreed, after Dorian writes something on a piece of paper. Wilde never says what it was, and the blank is the most efficient thing in the novel: a reader is handed the mechanics of blackmail without a word of its content.

The portrait

Basil’s canvas, kept behind a locked door; first appears Chapter I

It changes first at the mouth, then the hands, then the whole visage, and Dorian visits it the way other people visit a conscience. It is the only character in the novel that keeps an accurate record, and the last two pages show it was also the only one telling the truth about what he looked like.
