The Picture of Dorian Gray · Oscar Wilde

What happens

Chapters I-II A portrait, a conversation and a wish

Basil Hallward will not exhibit his best painting because he has put too much of himself into it. Lord Henry meets the sitter anyway and spends an afternoon in a garden explaining that youth is the only thing worth having. By the end of the second chapter Dorian has wished the picture would take the years instead, and Basil’s studio has produced two things it cannot control.

Chapter I
Basil explains to Lord Henry why the portrait can never be shown; Dorian is announced.
Chapter II
The sermon in the garden, the finished picture, the wish, and Basil offering to destroy the canvas.

Chapters III-VIII Sibyl Vane

Dorian falls in love with a girl who plays Juliet in a shabby little theatre in Holborn, and loves her for her parts. When love makes her performance sincere and therefore bad, he tells her so in her dressing room and leaves. She kills herself that night, and the mouth of the portrait has changed by morning.

Chapters III-IV
Lord Henry’s inquiries into Dorian’s parentage; the engagement announced by telegram.
Chapter V
The Vanes at home: a mother acting grief, a brother leaving for Australia and a sworn threat.
Chapters VI-VII
Sibyl plays badly because she has stopped pretending; Dorian’s speech in the dressing room; a cruel line added to the painted mouth.
Chapter VIII
Lord Henry calls with the news of her death and has turned it into an aesthetic experience before he leaves.

Chapters IX-XI The picture goes upstairs and eighteen years go past

Dorian has the canvas carried to the disused schoolroom at the top of the house, locks it in and keeps the key. A yellow-bound French novel arrives from Lord Henry, and the narrative loosens into years of collecting perfumes, jewels, embroideries and portraits of ancestors, with rumours accumulating in the gaps between paragraphs.

Chapter IX
Basil calls to console him and is refused a sight of the portrait; Dorian asks him for a drawing of Sibyl but will never sit to him again.
Chapter X
The frame-maker’s men carry the picture upstairs; the book in the yellow paper cover arrives.
Chapter XI
Eighteen years of study, fashion, ritual and rumour, told almost entirely as inventory.

Chapters XII-XIV The murder of Basil Hallward

Basil comes on the eve of Dorian’s thirty-eighth birthday to repeat the rumours to his face and to ask him to deny them. Dorian takes him up to the schoolroom instead, shows him the thing he made, and stabs him behind the ear. The next morning a chemist he once knew is blackmailed into destroying the body.

Chapter XII
A meeting in the fog at Grosvenor Square; Basil lists the friends Dorian has ruined.
Chapter XIII
The lamp held up to the portrait, the prayer, and the knife on the chest behind the door.
Chapter XIV
A summons, Alan Campbell’s refusal, a threat written on a sheet of paper, and five hours in a locked room with nitric acid.

Chapters XV-XVIII James Vane’s revenge, and the shooting party

Dorian goes from a dinner party to an opium den near the docks, where a woman calls him Prince Charming and Sibyl’s brother hears it. James Vane holds a pistol to his head, is talked out of it by a face that looks twenty, and follows him to the country. A shot fired at a hare kills a man hiding in the thicket, and Dorian is terrified until he sees that the dead man is James Vane.

Chapters XV-XVI
Lady Narborough’s dinner, then Blue Gate Fields; the woman’s name for him nearly kills him.
Chapter XVII
Selby Royal, an argument about naming things, and a face at the conservatory window.
Chapter XVIII
Two days indoors, a shooting party, and a body brought in from the covert.

Chapters XIX-XX Dorian stabs the portrait

Dorian tells Lord Henry he intends to reform and offers a spared country girl as evidence; Lord Henry laughs and denies that a book can poison anybody. Alone that night, Dorian looks for a change in the picture, finds only cunning added to the face, and attacks the canvas with the knife that killed Basil.

Chapter XIX
The last conversation: a confession offered as a story and dismissed as impossible.
Chapter XX
A walk home, a mirror broken, the portrait no better, and a cry heard by the servants.
