The Man Who Was Thursday · G. K. Chesterton

Form and language

Allegory

A narrative whose figures stand for ideas, without a key that fits every lock.

Sunday plainly means something, and the book will not say what. Chesterton, writing just before his death in 1936, insisted that the book recounted only a nightmare.

a small blue card, on which was written, “The Last Crusade,” and a number, the sign of his official authority

Chapter IV

Argument by reversal

A statement that contradicts expectation and turns out, on the terms set, to be exactly right.

A poet of law, a conspiracy that hides by sitting in public, detectives who read sonnets to prevent crimes: the paradoxes are the plot.

An artist is identical with an anarchist… You might transpose the words anywhere. An anarchist is an artist.

Chapter I

The comic grotesque

Figures exaggerated past realism into something both funny and frightening.

A vast president, an old man who chases at a crawl, a doctor whose glasses are the only terrifying thing about him. The exaggeration is always double-edged, and the laugh arrives a second after the fear.

The suburb of Saffron Park lay on the sunset side of London, as red and ragged as a cloud of sunset.

Chapter I

The chase

An extended action sequence that organises a long stretch of narrative.

There are five, each faster and less plausible than the last, ending with a balloon. The escalation is the argument: pursuit goes on until pursuer and pursued change places.

“Yes, I’m in the force right enough,” and he flicked a blue card towards them lightly as a matter of form.

Chapter IX

Reversal of identities

A gap between what characters know and what the reader has worked out.

For five chapters the reader is ahead of every character in the book, so the terror becomes comic without being cancelled: the men are truly frightened of people who are just as frightened of them.

The ordinary detective goes to pot-houses to arrest thieves; we go to artistic tea-parties to detect pessimists.

Chapter IV

Epigram

A compressed, quotable formulation that carries an argument.

Chaos is dull; the rare, strange thing is to hit the mark; we go to artistic tea-parties to detect pessimists. The book’s ideas travel in sentences short enough to be remembered by people who have not read it.

The rare, strange thing is to hit the mark; the gross, obvious thing is to miss it.

Chapter I

Dream logic

Narrative that follows the rules of dreams: repetition, pursuit, impossible geography, faces that will not stay still.

The chase across France in which every stranger joins the mob, and the president whose face grows as you approach, belong to the logic of a bad night more than a plot, and the last chapter says so.

the large face of Sunday grew larger and larger; and Syme was gripped with a fear that when he was quite close the face would be too big to be possible, and that he would scream aloud.
