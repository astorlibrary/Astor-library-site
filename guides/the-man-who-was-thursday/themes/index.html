Order and anarchy

Syme’s position (that a train arriving at Victoria is more astonishing than a train going anywhere) is the book’s whole argument. In the novel order is the hardest thing to achieve and the rarest thing to notice, and it is an adventure in itself.

Through the book Stated as a joke in a suburban garden, tested at a table of bombers, and finally handed to a president who turns out to have been running both sides.

In the text

An artist is identical with an anarchist… You might transpose the words anywhere. An anarchist is an artist.

Chapter I

The position Chesterton had spent two books arguing against, given to a character who means it and can phrase it. Putting the best version of the opposing case first is what stops the novel being a pamphlet.

the most dangerous criminal now is the entirely lawless modern philosopher.

Chapter IV

The premise of the plot, stated as policy. It also explains the book’s structure: if the enemy is an idea, the detective has to be a man who can hold the idea and not be held by it, which is exactly Syme’s difficulty.

Masks

Every terrifying figure in the book is wearing something: a beard, black glasses, a false nose, a costume. The horror is always the covering, and the face beneath it is always a colleague.

Through the book The first unmasking is a relief, the second a joke, the fourth a pattern, and by the last chapter the pattern has become a question about whether anything in the world is what it appears.

In the text

“I am a policeman,” said the Professor with his first broad smile

Chapter VIII

The line arrives as the release of a long-held breath, and it is the second time the book has played this card. From here the reader stops fearing the conspiracy and starts wondering what is left to be afraid of.

“I am a detective from Scotland Yard,”

Chapter XII

By this point the sentence is a punchline. The Central Anarchist Council has turned out to consist entirely of the police, which leaves the book with a chase and nobody to chase but its own president.

Nightmare

The subtitle, A Nightmare, means what it says. Distances stretch, pursuers move at impossible speeds, the same face appears on every stranger, and nobody can say when the dream began.

Through the book Realistic satire in the first two chapters gives way to dream logic at the council table, and the ending does not say where Syme woke; he cannot remember coming to at all.

In the text

The suburb of Saffron Park lay on the sunset side of London, as red and ragged as a cloud of sunset.

Chapter I

Chesterton describes a real place (Bedford Park, where the aesthetes lived) as if it were weather. The simile makes the suburb look like something about to disperse, which is what happens to everything solid in the book.

the large face of Sunday grew larger and larger; and Syme was gripped with a fear that when he was quite close the face would be too big to be possible, and that he would scream aloud.

Chapter V

A childhood fear of a museum mask, used to introduce the one character the book never explains. The threat is scale, more than violence: something too large to be taken in, which is how the last chapter will describe God.

Theology

The chase turns into a question about suffering and a president who says he is the peace of God. The final chapters put the objections in the mouths of the sympathetic characters, which is why the book is still argued about.

Through the book The Secretary will not be reconciled, Gregory accuses everyone of never having suffered, and Sunday’s last words are a question from the Gospels, with no answer.

In the text

a small blue card, on which was written, “The Last Crusade,” and a number, the sign of his official authority

Chapter IV

The badge of a modern police force is given a crusader’s name, which is the book’s whole method in an object: a bureaucratic detail that turns out to be a religious claim. Four more of these cards appear before the plot is done.

“I am the Sabbath,” said the other without moving. “I am the peace of God.”

Chapter XV

The answer names the day and claims the theology. It is also the moment the thriller stops working as a thriller: no pursuit can continue once the quarry has said this, and the remaining pages go to the objection.

Divided identity

Syme is a poet, a policeman and an anarchist delegate at the same time. The council members hold two identities each, and the question of which is the disguise is never as simple as it looks.

Through the book The masks come off one by one, but the last chapter puts new ones on (the six are dressed as the days of creation), so the removal of disguise ends as another costume.

In the text

Syme had for a flash the sensation that the cosmos had turned exactly upside down

Chapter VIII

The reversal feels like a change in the universe, far bigger than a change in the facts, and that is the book’s characteristic scale. Nothing has happened except that a piece of card has appeared, and the world has inverted.

Appearances

Syme’s speech about the back of the world is the book’s own account of itself: everything looks brutal from behind, and the face may be entirely different.

Through the book The idea is introduced as consolation during the balloon chase and then dramatised in the garden, where the pursued enemy turns out to be the employer.

In the text

The ordinary detective goes to pot-houses to arrest thieves; we go to artistic tea-parties to detect pessimists.

Chapter IV

The funniest sentence in the novel and a complete job description. It also licenses the whole plot: if crimes can be detected in books of sonnets, then a poet is the right man to send.

Shall I tell you the secret of the whole world? It is that we have only known the back of the world. We see everything from behind, and it looks brutal.

Chapter XIV

The book’s own instruction for reading it. Everything that has seemed monstrous has been a rear view of something else, and with this sentence a chase novel becomes an argument about how appearances are arranged.

Ordinary things

Bradshaw’s railway timetable, a good breakfast in public, a bowler hat, lilac in a garden: Chesterton makes his case for the ordinary by putting it inside a nightmare and watching it survive.

Through the book The conspiracy is at its most sinister over coffee-pots, and the dream ends on a girl cutting flowers before breakfast.

In the text

he was a poet of respectability

Chapter I

He makes three claims in a row (poet of law, of order, of respectability), each more provoking to that audience than the last. The garden reacts as though he had fallen out of the sky, which tells you what kind of suburb it is.

There he saw the sister of Gregory, the girl with the gold-red hair, cutting lilac before breakfast, with the great unconscious gravity of a girl.

Chapter XV

After a president the size of the sky, a girl with a pair of scissors. The nightmare is dismissed by breakfast, with no argument, and the ordinary world is handed back with its gravity intact.
