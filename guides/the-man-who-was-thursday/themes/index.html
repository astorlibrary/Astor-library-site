The Man Who Was Thursday · G. K. Chesterton

Themes

Order and anarchy

Syme’s position (that a train arriving at Victoria is more astonishing than a train going anywhere) is the book’s whole argument. In the novel order is the hardest thing to achieve and the rarest thing to notice, and it is an adventure in itself.

Stated as a joke in a suburban garden, tested at a table of bombers, and finally handed to a president who turns out to have been running both sides.

An artist is identical with an anarchist… You might transpose the words anywhere. An anarchist is an artist.

Chapter I

The rare, strange thing is to hit the mark; the gross, obvious thing is to miss it.

Chapter I

Masks

Every terrifying figure in the book is wearing something: a beard, black glasses, a false nose, a costume. The horror is always the covering, and the face beneath it is always a colleague.

The first unmasking is a relief, the second a joke, the fourth a pattern, and by the last chapter the pattern has become a question about whether anything in the world is what it appears.

“I am a policeman,” said the Professor with his first broad smile

Chapter VIII

“Yes, I’m in the force right enough,” and he flicked a blue card towards them lightly as a matter of form.

Chapter IX

Nightmare

The subtitle, A Nightmare, means what it says. Distances stretch, pursuers move at impossible speeds, the same face appears on every stranger, and nobody can say when the dream began.

Realistic satire in the first two chapters gives way to dream logic at the council table, and the ending does not say where Syme woke; he cannot remember coming to at all.

The suburb of Saffron Park lay on the sunset side of London, as red and ragged as a cloud of sunset.

Chapter V

Theology

The chase turns into a question about suffering and a president who says he is the peace of God. The final chapters put the objections in the mouths of the sympathetic characters, which is why the book is still argued about.

The Secretary will not be reconciled, Gregory accuses everyone of never having suffered, and Sunday’s last words are a question from the Gospels, with no answer.

the large face of Sunday grew larger and larger; and Syme was gripped with a fear that when he was quite close the face would be too big to be possible, and that he would scream aloud.

Chapter IV

Divided identity

Syme is a poet, a policeman and an anarchist delegate at the same time. The council members hold two identities each, and the question of which is the disguise is never as simple as it looks.

The masks come off one by one, but the last chapter puts new ones on (the six are dressed as the days of creation), so the removal of disguise ends as another costume.

Chapter VIII

Appearances

Syme’s speech about the back of the world is the book’s own account of itself: everything looks brutal from behind, and the face may be entirely different.

The idea is introduced as consolation during the balloon chase and then dramatised in the garden, where the pursued enemy turns out to be the employer.

the most dangerous criminal now is the entirely lawless modern philosopher.

Chapter IV

The ordinary detective goes to pot-houses to arrest thieves; we go to artistic tea-parties to detect pessimists.

Chapter IV

Ordinary things

Bradshaw’s railway timetable, a good breakfast in public, a bowler hat, lilac in a garden: Chesterton makes his case for the ordinary by putting it inside a nightmare and watching it survive.

The conspiracy is at its most sinister over coffee-pots, and the dream ends on a girl cutting flowers before breakfast.

The rare, strange thing is to hit the mark; the gross, obvious thing is to miss it.
