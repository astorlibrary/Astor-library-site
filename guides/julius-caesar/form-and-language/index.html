Julius Caesar · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Classical rhetoric

The formal art of persuasion: arrangement, repetition, the pretence of reluctance.

Antony’s funeral speech is a set piece of paralipsis (claiming not to say the thing you are saying), and its refrain of “honourable” does the work an open accusation would have been stopped for.

Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world Like a Colossus, and we petty men Walk under his huge legs and peep about To find ourselves dishonourable graves.

Cassius, 1.2.139

Prose against verse

Shifting between metre and prose to mark register and audience.

Brutus addresses the citizens in balanced prose and Antony answers in verse; the crowd is persuaded by the one that sounds least like a lecture.

Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more.

Brutus, 3.2.21

Dramatic irony

The audience knows something a character does not.

The date itself does the work: every reference to the ides of March lands on an audience who knows what happens on it, so Caesar’s confidence is heard as countdown.

Beware the ides of March.

Soothsayer, 1.2.21

Portents and prophecy

Signs staged as events in front of the audience.

The storm of 1.3 is a whole scene: men walk about in it disagreeing about what it means, which is the play’s method for everything else as well.

Soliloquy

A character alone, reasoning aloud to the audience.

Brutus’s orchard soliloquy is where he argues himself into the murder, and it proceeds entirely by analogy (an adder, a ladder, an egg), because there is no evidence to reason from.

It must be by his death: and for my part, I know no personal cause to spurn at him, But for the general. He would be crown'd: How that might change his nature, there's the question.

Brutus, 2.1.10

The crowd

A stage mob given lines, so that its reactions are part of the script.

The citizens open the play, decide the middle of it and commit its most gratuitous murder; Shakespeare writes their interruptions into the funeral so that persuasion can be measured line by line.

Beware the ides of March.

Soothsayer, 1.2.21
