Julius Caesar · William Shakespeare

Characters

Julius Caesar

Dictator of Rome; first appears 1.2

He is on stage for a fraction of the play and dominates the rest of it. Shakespeare is careful to make him formidable and physically ordinary, deaf in one ear, prone to fits, superstitious enough to change his mind twice in one morning, so that whether he would have become a tyrant can never be settled.

Marcus Brutus

A praetor, and the conspiracy’s respectable face; first appears 1.2

He joins a murder on a hypothesis, having said outright that he knows no personal cause. Every subsequent decision he takes for honourable reasons is tactically ruinous: spare Antony, let him speak, refuse the money he needs, march to Philippi. The play never suggests he is insincere, and that makes him difficult.

Caius Cassius

The conspiracy’s organiser; first appears 1.2

He begins as the play’s cynic (envious, sharp about Caesar’s body, willing to forge letters) and ends as the one character who reads situations correctly and is overruled every time. Kill Antony, do not let him speak, wait at Sardis: he is right three times, defers to Brutus three times, and dies of a misreport.

Mark Antony

Caesar’s friend and political heir; first appears 1.2

Dismissed by Brutus as a limb of Caesar and a man given to sports, he turns out to be the most capable politician in the play. His funeral speech says the opposite of what it seems to say, and the next scene he appears in shows him trading his own nephew’s life across a table.

Portia

Brutus’s wife, daughter of Cato; first appears 2.1

She argues for the right to know what her husband is doing, and proves her constancy by wounding herself in the thigh. Having won the argument, she cannot hold the knowledge: her next scene shows somebody trying not to give something away, and her death is reported twice in one conversation.

Calpurnia

Caesar’s wife; first appears 1.2

She has one scene, is right in it, and is talked out of being right by a man who reinterprets her dream as a compliment. The play gives her the only correct prediction anybody makes about the morning of the Ides, and then shows exactly how such a warning gets set aside in a household that runs on prestige.

Casca

A senator, and the first to strike; first appears 1.2

His account of the crown scene in 1.2 is the play’s funniest piece of writing and its most useful: a bored, contemptuous eyewitness is worth more than a ceremony. He is terrified by the storm in the next scene, which makes his blunt prose a pose, and Cassius recruits him in about forty lines.

Octavius

Caesar’s adopted heir; first appears 4.1

He arrives in Act IV, contradicts Antony twice, and wins both exchanges without raising his voice. Shakespeare gives him very few lines and makes every one of them a decision, so that an audience who knows what he becomes can see it starting: the last words of the play are his, ordering Brutus’s burial and calling the field to rest.
