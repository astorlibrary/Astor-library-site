Julius Caesar · William Shakespeare

Context

Dates

44 BC
Caesar assassinated in Rome On the ides of March, 44 BCE, in the meeting place at Pompey’s theatre: the historical event behind Act III.
42 BC
The battles at Philippi Brutus and Cassius are defeated by Antony and Octavius in Macedonia, some two years after the killing.
1579
North’s translation of Plutarch’s Lives The English prose Shakespeare worked from, sometimes putting whole passages into verse with few changes.
1599
The Globe opens, and the play is written Thomas Platter records seeing a tragedy of Julius Caesar at a Bankside playhouse on 21 September, with a dance after it.
1623
First printed in the First Folio No quarto is known, which makes this one of the least textually disputed of the tragedies.
1937
Orson Welles’s modern-dress Caesar The Mercury Theatre staging in New York, cut and lit to read the play against contemporary European fascism.
1953
Joseph L. Mankiewicz’s film With Marlon Brando as Antony and John Gielgud as Cassius, and the funeral scene shot as a mass rally.
2012
Gregory Doran’s RSC production Set in a contemporary African state, with an all-Black cast, and later filmed.

Places

Rome
The city of the first three acts: a street, an orchard, a house and the meeting place where Caesar is killed.
The Tiber
The river Cassius says he pulled Caesar out of, in the story that makes a general into a man who can drown.
Sardis
The camp in Asia Minor where the two conspirators quarrel in a tent and Caesar’s ghost appears.
Philippi
The plain in Macedonia where the republican armies are destroyed and both leaders die by their own swords.
Bankside, London
Where Thomas Platter watched a Caesar play in September 1599, in the Globe’s first season.

Critics

Thomas Platter

Travel journal (1599)

Platter, a Swiss visitor, saw a play about Julius Caesar across the Thames on 21 September 1599, in a house with a thatched roof, with about fifteen characters and a dance at the end. It was very probably Shakespeare’s play at the new Globe, though that is not certain.

Ben Jonson

Timber, or Discoveries (1641)

Jonson remembered a line he said Caesar had spoken in the play, “Caesar did never wrong, but with just cause”, as an example of Shakespeare writing too fast. The line is not in the Folio text, which may have been revised after the joke.
