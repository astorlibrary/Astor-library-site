Sleepy Hollow and Other American Halloween Stories · Washington Irving, Edgar Allan Poe and others

What happens

Washington Irving A Dutch valley, a schoolmaster and a bargain with the devil

The collection opens with the two Irving tales that taught American writing how to be funny and frightening in the same paragraph. Sleepy Hollow spends most of its length on food, dancing and local gossip before it puts anything on the road behind Ichabod; The Devil and Tom Walker is a New England money story with a black man in the swamp keeping the accounts.

The Legend of Sleepy Hollow (1820)
A valley where a drowsy influence hangs over the land, and where the local ghost is a Hessian trooper looking for his head.
The Legend of Sleepy Hollow
Food, fiddling and war stories, and a schoolmaster refused by the heiress before he rides home.
The Devil and Tom Walker (1824)
A miserly Massachusetts man meets the devil among the ruins of an Indian fort and takes the usury business.

Nathaniel Hawthorne Young Goodman Brown and The Minister’s Black Veil

Hawthorne makes the New England past a moral problem with no way out. Young Goodman Brown goes into the woods on one night’s errand and never recovers from what he may only have dreamed; The Minister’s Black Veil puts a piece of crape between one man and his parish and lets the parish supply the meaning.

Young Goodman Brown (1835)
A night walk into the forest, a congregation at a fire, and a man who cannot afterwards look at his wife.
The Minister’s Black Veil (1836)
A minister covers his face and refuses to explain, and the explanation his people invent is the story.

Edgar Allan Poe Usher, The Black Cat and The Tell-Tale Heart

Three of the most quoted stories in American literature, all told by men explaining themselves. Usher is atmosphere and collapse; The Black Cat and The Tell-Tale Heart are confessions from narrators who begin by insisting on their sanity and end by producing the evidence against themselves.

The Fall of the House of Usher (1839)
A visitor arrives at a house with a fissure in it, and leaves as the fissure finishes its work.
The Tell-Tale Heart (1843)
A murder committed for an eye, and a sound the murderer alone can hear while the police sit talking.
The Black Cat (1843)
Drink, cruelty, a second cat, and a rap on the plaster that gives the whole thing away.

Bierce, Gilman and Freeman Stories from the 1890s

Four stories from the 1890s and after, written by people who had seen a civil war and a rest cure. Bierce’s horrors are reported flatly, by soldiers and coroners; Gilman’s room is a medical arrangement; Freeman’s haunting arrives as a domestic detail that the women of the house discuss over their work.

The Boarded Window (1891)
A frontier widower sits with his wife’s body, and the reason the window is boarded is given in the last sentence.
The Damned Thing (1893)
A coroner’s court hears about a creature of a colour outside human sight, and returns the verdict it prefers.
The Yellow Wall-Paper (1892)
A rest cure, a barred nursery and a pattern with something behind it.
The Shadows on the Wall (1903)
A shadow in a New England parlour that matches nobody present, and the sisters who keep looking at it.

Lovecraft, and the holiday The Outsider, and how the stories became Halloween reading

Lovecraft’s story closes the century of writing the book covers, and the introduction then does the other half of the job: how jack-o’-lanterns, postcards, shop decorations and repeated anthologies made a set of unrelated tales into Halloween reading.

The Outsider (1926)
A climb out of a castle with no stairs up, a lit house, and a reflection.
The introduction
Trick-or-treating, printing and habit: how the stories acquired an October association none of their authors intended.
