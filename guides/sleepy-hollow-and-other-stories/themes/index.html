Sleepy Hollow and Other American Halloween Stories · Washington Irving, Edgar Allan Poe and others

Themes

Village superstition

The collection’s hundred years runs from a community’s shared ghost to a single mind’s private one. Irving’s horseman belongs to everyone in the Hollow; Poe’s heart is audible to one man; Gilman’s woman is behind one room’s paper.

The fear moves steadily indoors and inward, and the later stories have no village left to consult.

A drowsy, dreamy influence seems to hang over the land, and to pervade the very atmosphere.

Narrators

Nearly every story arrives through a teller with an interest: a chronicler quoting a village, a murderer insisting on his sanity, a coroner’s court reading from a dead man’s diary, a woman writing where she has been told not to.

Irving’s narrator is amused and detached; by the 1890s the tellers are inside the events, and by The Outsider the teller does not know what he is.

Concealment

Hawthorne’s veil, Poe’s floorboards and plastered wall, Bierce’s boarded window: again and again the volume hides something and then listens to it. Concealment is the plot of at least half these stories.

In Hawthorne the guilt is general and unnameable; in Poe it is specific and buried; in Bierce it is a fact the narrator withholds until the final line.

Puritan inheritance

Salem, Mather, the meeting-house and the devil in the woods sit under the American stories the way old churches sit under English ones. Irving’s schoolmaster reads witchcraft history for entertainment; Hawthorne’s characters live in its consequences.

What begins as a comic superstition in Irving becomes, in Hawthorne, a religion that can ruin a man in one night.

American settings

A Dutch valley, a swamp, a forest, a tarn, a frontier clearing. The settings are local, named and recent, and the supernatural in them belongs to a particular soil, a long way from old castles.

Usher’s tarn and Lovecraft’s castle are the exceptions, unplaced and old, and even the castle turns out to be somewhere else.

Money and land

Irving’s ghost story is also a story about a poor schoolmaster’s designs on a rich man’s barns, and The Devil and Tom Walker is about usury. The supernatural keeps arriving where property is at stake.

The theme fades as the stories move indoors: by Gilman and Freeman the question is authority inside a household, no longer property.
