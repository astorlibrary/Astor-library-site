All's Well That Ends Well · William Shakespeare

Context

Dates

1353
Boccaccio finishes the Decameron The ninth tale of the third day tells of Giletta of Narbonne, a physician’s daughter who cures the King of France, is given Beltramo of Roussillon as her husband, and wins him back by meeting his conditions.
1566
Painter’s Palace of Pleasure William Painter’s collection of translated tales puts the Giletta story into English, the most likely route by which it reached Shakespeare.
1598
Meres lists a Love’s Labour’s Won Francis Meres names it among Shakespeare’s comedies in Palladis Tamia. No play of that title survives. All’s Well has been proposed as a candidate, with no evidence either way.
1604
Measure for Measure played at court The other comedy of these years that depends on a bed trick was performed at Whitehall on 26 December. Most editors date All’s Well near it.
1623
Printed in the First Folio The only early text. It appears among the comedies, and no earlier quarto is known.
1741
First recorded performance The play’s securely recorded stage history begins at Goodman’s Fields, more than a century after the Folio.
1765
Johnson cannot like Bertram In his edition of Shakespeare, Samuel Johnson set out Bertram’s offences and objected that the play dismisses him to happiness regardless.
1896
Boas names the problem plays Frederick Boas grouped All’s Well with Measure for Measure, Troilus and Cressida and Hamlet as plays that raise questions they do not resolve. The label has stuck to the comedies.
1959
Guthrie at Stratford Tyrone Guthrie’s Shakespeare Memorial Theatre production, designed by Tanya Moiseiwitsch, had Edith Evans as the Countess.
1967
John Barton’s RSC production Ian Richardson played Bertram, Lynn Farleigh Helena and Helen Mirren Diana. A television version followed in 1968.
1981
BBC Television Shakespeare Elijah Moshinsky directed Angela Down as Helena, Ian Charleson as Bertram and Celia Johnson as the Countess. In the same year Trevor Nunn’s RSC production cast Peggy Ashcroft as the Countess.
2009
Marianne Elliott at the National Theatre Michelle Terry played Helena and Conleth Hill Parolles, in a production recorded for broadcast and often discussed for its handling of the ending.

Places

Rossillon (Roussillon)
The Countess’s house, where the play opens and ends. The historical county of Roussillon lay around Perpignan, on the Mediterranean side of the Pyrenees.
Paris
The King’s court, where Helena cures him and chooses her husband in Act II.
Florence
The Duke’s city and camp, where Bertram commands the horse, Parolles loses his drum, and the Widow lodges pilgrims.
Siena
Home of the Senoys, Florence’s enemy in the war the young French lords go to join.
Marseilles
Where Helena expects to find the King in 4.4, and where she learns in 5.1 that he has already gone.
Santiago de Compostela
The shrine of Saint Jaques le Grand, to which Helena writes that she has gone barefoot. She is next seen in Florence.
