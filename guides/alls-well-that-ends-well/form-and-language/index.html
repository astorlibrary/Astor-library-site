All's Well That Ends Well · William Shakespeare

Form and language

The bed trick

A plot device in which one person secretly takes another’s place in a sexual encounter, usually in the dark, to bind or expose the partner.

Helena takes Diana’s place in Bertram’s bed on a night he has been told to keep silent and leave within the hour. The device comes from Boccaccio, and the play keeps it offstage, so the audience hears of it only through Helena’s plans and Diana’s riddles.

Is wicked meaning in a lawful deed, And lawful meaning in a lawful act, Where both not sin, and yet a sinful fact.

Helena, 3.7.45

Stage property

An object handled on stage that carries far more weight than its use in the scene explains.

Two rings drive the plot: Bertram’s ancestral ring, which Diana gets from him, and the King’s ring, which Helena puts on Bertram’s finger in the dark. The last scene is spent working out who gave which ring to whom.

When thou canst get the ring upon my finger, which never shall come off, and show me a child begotten of thy body that I am father to, then call me husband

Bertram (in a letter read by Helena), 3.2.50

Letters

Written messages read aloud or reported on stage, which carry action and information across distances the characters cannot cross.

Bertram’s two letters in 3.2 set the plot’s terms, Helena’s pilgrim sonnet in 3.4 covers her escape, Parolles’s letter to Diana convicts him in 4.3, and Diana’s petition in 5.3 brings Bertram’s lies to a stop.

When thou canst get the ring upon my finger, which never shall come off, and show me a child begotten of thy body that I am father to, then call me husband

Bertram (in a letter read by Helena), 3.2.50

The gulling scene

A comic episode in which a group plays an elaborate trick on a vain or foolish character to expose him.

The drum plot of 4.1 and 4.3 captures Parolles with nonsense words and a blindfold, and the questioning is conducted with Bertram present, so the victim’s betrayals land on the man he is betraying.

Simply the thing I am Shall make me live.

Parolles, 4.3.289

Rhymed couplets

Pairs of rhyming lines, which give speech the finality of a proverb or the formality of a contract.

The play shifts into rhyme at its moments of decision and bargaining: Helena’s resolve at the end of 1.1, her bargain with the King in 2.1, the King’s speech on honour in 2.3, and Bertram’s last promise. The Epilogue is in the same form.

Our remedies oft in ourselves do lie, Which we ascribe to heaven: the fated sky Gives us free scope

Helena, 1.1.191

Soliloquy

A speech delivered by a character alone on stage, giving the audience direct access to thoughts hidden from others.

Helena has two in 1.1, which take her from hopeless adoration to a plan, and a third in 3.2, where she decides to leave France so that Bertram can come home. Parolles’s short soliloquy after his exposure is the most honest thing he says.

'Twere all one That I should love a bright particular star, And think to wed it, he is so above me.

Helena, 1.1.75

Prose against verse

The switch between prose and verse within a play, used to mark changes of class, mood or seriousness.

Parolles, the Clown, Lafew in his quarrels and the soldiers of the camp speak prose, while the court and Helena speak verse. The lords’ sober judgement of Bertram in 4.3 comes in prose, which strips it of ceremony.

Virginity, by being once lost, may be ten times found; by being ever kept, it is ever lost.

Parolles, 1.1.118
