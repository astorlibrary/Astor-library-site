Birth and merit

Bertram refuses Helena for one reason, her father’s profession, and the King answers that honour comes from deeds and that he can supply the title himself. The play sets a count who inherited everything beside a physician’s daughter who earned her place at court.

Through the play The argument is won in words in 2.3 and settled by force, and Bertram’s conduct in Florence shows whether his birth has made him fit for the rank he defends.

In the text

’Twere all one That I should love a bright particular star, And think to wed it, he is so above me.

Helena, 1.1.75

Helena measures the social distance between them in astronomy. Bertram is a star, she may be comforted only by his collateral light, and marrying him would be as absurd as wedding a star. The image gives his rank the fixed order of the heavens, and by the end of the same scene she will argue that the sky leaves people free to act.

A poor physician’s daughter my wife! Disdain Rather corrupt me ever!

Bertram, 2.3.111

His objection is purely one of rank, and he makes it in front of the man she has just cured. Poor and physician’s are the insults; he says nothing against her looks or her honesty. He would rather be corrupted by disdain for ever than accept her, and the line hands the King the argument he answers for the next thirty lines.

Forced marriage

The central marriage is ordered by a king, performed within hours and abandoned the same night. For the rest of the play Helena works to make a legal bond into a real marriage, on the terms her husband wrote to be impossible.

Through the play The last scene is a second wedding in all but form, and it closes on Bertram’s conditional promise and the King’s offer to arrange another marriage for Diana.

In the text

Wars is no strife To the dark house and the detested wife.

Bertram, 2.3.273

The couplet weighs a marriage against a battlefield and finds the battlefield easier. A dark house suggests both mourning and confinement, and the line makes Helena a place to escape from. In 2.1 Bertram was kept from the war as too young; now the marriage gives him his excuse to go.

’Tis but the shadow of a wife you see, The name, and not the thing.

Helena, 5.3.306

Calling herself a shadow, as if she were a ghost, Helena then puts the whole history of her marriage in six words: she has been Bertram’s wife in title only. His answer, Both, both, grants her the thing as well as the name. How much he means by it the text leaves to the actor, since it gives him two words and no stage direction.

Honour

Bertram’s ring is the honour of his house, Diana’s chastity is hers, Parolles’s lost drum is an instrument of honour, and the King says honour grows from acts. Each version is tested and several are traded away.

Through the play Bertram wins honour in the field and loses it in bed and at court, and the First Lord’s mingled yarn describes the mix.

In the text

Love all, trust a few, Do wrong to none.

Countess, 1.1.53

Nine words of a mother’s advice, and the rest of the play shows her son getting every clause wrong. He trusts Parolles, the one companion everyone warns him about, and before the end he has wronged his wife and lied to the King. The same speech hopes his goodness will share with his birthright, which is what Bertram fails to manage.

Mine honour’s such a ring; My chastity’s the jewel of our house, Bequeathed down from many ancestors

Diana, 4.2.46

Diana repeats Bertram’s own sentence back to him with one noun changed, so that his reason for keeping the ring becomes her reason for keeping her chastity. He has no answer that does not give way, and six lines later he hands the ring over. The exchange states the bargain the plot depends on: a man’s heirloom against a woman’s body, and both of them called honour.

Words, clothes and courage

Parolles lives by scarves, garters and big talk, and Lafew says the soul of the man is his clothes. His exposure in 4.3 strips off each layer until only a frightened man who wants to live is left.

Through the play Bertram trusts the performance and learns what it covers only when he hears himself described by it, and he goes on performing at court in the last act.

In the text

there can be no kernal in this light nut; the soul of this man is his clothes

Lafew, 2.5.38

Lafew has spent two scenes mocking Parolles’s scarves and garters, and here he reduces the man to an empty shell and a wardrobe. The drum plot proves him right in 4.3, when the blindfold comes off and nothing is left under the costume. Bertram hears the warning and ignores it, which tells the audience as much about his judgement as about Parolles.

Simply the thing I am Shall make me live.

Parolles, 4.3.289

Exposed as a coward, a liar and a traitor, Parolles neither apologises nor collapses. He gives up the title of captain and resolves to eat, drink and sleep as the thing he is. The line is comic and oddly dignified, and it gives the plain account of himself that Bertram never gives, even in the last scene.

Conditions, tokens and proof

Bertram’s refusal takes the form of two conditions, the ring and the child, and the plot is Helena’s way of satisfying them. Rings, letters and a pregnancy carry the final scene, where the King can only believe what he is shown.

Through the play Two rings cross in the dark in Florence and have to be traced back to their owners in front of the court, while Diana’s riddles keep the proof back until Helena can deliver it.

In the text

When thou canst get the ring upon my finger, which never shall come off, and show me a child begotten of thy body that I am father to, then call me husband

Bertram (in a letter read by Helena), 3.2.50

Bertram writes his refusal as a pair of conditions he believes impossible, and signs it off with a pun, in such a then I write a never. Without meaning to he has drafted a contract. Helena reads it as one and meets each clause to the letter, which is how his refusal becomes the plan for the rest of the plot.

If she, my liege, can make me know this clearly, I’ll love her dearly, ever, ever dearly.

Bertram, 5.3.314

His promise begins with If. He addresses the King and speaks of Helena in the third person, as though the marriage were still a case awaiting proof. The chime of clearly and dearly makes the couplet sound settled, and the repeated ever sounds eager, yet the condition stands, and Helena immediately offers ‘deadly divorce’ if her proof fails.

Inheritance

Helena inherits her father’s skill and a paper of prescriptions, Bertram inherits his father’s face, title and ring. The Countess prays that Bertram will succeed his father in manners as well as shape, and the King looks for the father’s moral parts in the son.

Through the play The inherited remedy makes Helena’s fortune, the inherited ring catches Bertram out, and the play ends with an heir already on the way.

In the text

He that of greatest works is finisher Oft does them by the weakest minister.

Helena, 2.1.134

A proverb about Providence is Helena’s answer to the whole college of physicians, and her lack of standing becomes the evidence for her claim. A young woman carrying a dead father’s prescription becomes, in this couplet, the kind of instrument heaven prefers. The King had just refused to prostitute his malady to empirics, and within fifty lines he agrees to try her.

He was my son, But I do wash his name out of my blood, And thou art all my child.

Countess, 3.2.59

Disowning her son in favour of her ward, the Countess completes the adoption she offered in 1.3. Wash and blood treat kinship as a stain that can be cleaned away, a startling claim from the character who cares most about family in the play. The past tense of He was my son does the disowning before the rest of the sentence explains it.

Pursuit in love

Helena chooses and pursues, which reverses the usual pattern of comedy, and Bertram flees her while pursuing another woman. The play makes both kinds of wanting uncomfortable to watch.

Through the play Helena says in 4.4 that men can make sweet use of what they hate. The bed trick joins his lust and her love in one act that neither of them sees in the same way.

In the text

Virginity, by being once lost, may be ten times found; by being ever kept, it is ever lost.

Parolles, 1.1.118

Parolles talks about virginity the way a merchant talks about hoarding: goods kept lose value and goods spent multiply. The paradox is glib, and the play then carries it out in earnest, since Helena’s virginity is given up in a dark room in Florence and comes back as a child and a husband. His prose joke turns into her method.

Is wicked meaning in a lawful deed, And lawful meaning in a lawful act, Where both not sin, and yet a sinful fact.

Helena, 3.7.45

In a riddle, Helena works through the ethics of the bed trick. Bertram intends adultery and will in fact sleep with his wife; she intends something lawful and still takes part in a deception. The balanced clauses keep both sides in view without settling the question, and the last phrase, a sinful fact, concedes that something about the deed remains wrong.
