All's Well That Ends Well · William Shakespeare

Themes

Birth and merit

Bertram refuses Helena for one reason, her father’s profession, and the King answers that honour comes from deeds and that he can supply the title himself. The play sets a count who inherited everything beside a physician’s daughter who earned her place at court.

The argument is won in words in 2.3 and settled by force, and Bertram’s conduct in Florence shows whether his birth has made him fit for the rank he defends.

'Twere all one That I should love a bright particular star, And think to wed it, he is so above me.

Helena, 1.1.75

Our remedies oft in ourselves do lie, Which we ascribe to heaven: the fated sky Gives us free scope

Helena, 1.1.191

Forced marriage

The central marriage is ordered by a king, performed within hours and abandoned the same night. For the rest of the play Helena works to make a legal bond into a real marriage, on the terms her husband wrote to be impossible.

The last scene is a second wedding in all but form, and it closes on Bertram’s conditional promise and the King’s offer to arrange another marriage for Diana.

A poor physician's daughter my wife! Disdain Rather corrupt me ever!

Bertram, 2.3.111

Wars is no strife To the dark house and the detested wife.

Bertram, 2.3.273

Honour

Bertram’s ring is the honour of his house, Diana’s chastity is hers, Parolles’s lost drum is an instrument of honour, and the King says honour grows from acts. Each version is tested and several are traded away.

Bertram wins honour in the field and loses it in bed and at court, and the First Lord’s mingled yarn describes the mix.

Love all, trust a few, Do wrong to none.

Countess, 1.1.53

From lowest place whence virtuous things proceed, The place is dignified by th’ doer’s deed.

King, 2.3.121

Words, clothes and courage

Parolles lives by scarves, garters and big talk, and Lafew says the soul of the man is his clothes. His exposure in 4.3 strips off each layer until only a frightened man who wants to live is left.

Bertram trusts the performance and learns what it covers only when he hears himself described by it, and he goes on performing at court in the last act.

there can be no kernal in this light nut; the soul of this man is his clothes

Lafew, 2.5.38

Simply the thing I am Shall make me live.

Parolles, 4.3.289

Conditions, tokens and proof

Bertram’s refusal takes the form of two conditions, the ring and the child, and the plot is Helena’s way of satisfying them. Rings, letters and a pregnancy carry the final scene, where the King can only believe what he is shown.

Two rings cross in the dark in Florence and have to be traced back to their owners in front of the court, while Diana’s riddles keep the proof back until Helena can deliver it.

When thou canst get the ring upon my finger, which never shall come off, and show me a child begotten of thy body that I am father to, then call me husband

Bertram (in a letter read by Helena), 3.2.50

Mine honour's such a ring; My chastity's the jewel of our house, Bequeathed down from many ancestors

Diana, 4.2.46

Inheritance

Helena inherits her father’s skill and a paper of prescriptions, Bertram inherits his father’s face, title and ring. The Countess prays that Bertram will succeed his father in manners as well as shape, and the King looks for the father’s moral parts in the son.

The inherited remedy makes Helena’s fortune, the inherited ring catches Bertram out, and the play ends with an heir already on the way.

Love all, trust a few, Do wrong to none.

Countess, 1.1.53

He that of greatest works is finisher Oft does them by the weakest minister.

Helena, 2.1.134

Pursuit in love

Helena chooses and pursues, which reverses the usual pattern of comedy, and Bertram flees her while pursuing another woman. The play makes both kinds of wanting uncomfortable to watch.

Helena says in 4.4 that men can make sweet use of what they hate. The bed trick joins his lust and her love in one act that neither of them sees in the same way.

'Twere all one That I should love a bright particular star, And think to wed it, he is so above me.

Helena, 1.1.75

Virginity, by being once lost, may be ten times found; by being ever kept, it is ever lost.

Parolles, 1.1.118
