All's Well That Ends Well · William Shakespeare

Characters

Helena

Daughter of the physician Gerard de Narbon, brought up by the Countess; first appears 1.1

She drives every turn of the plot, and she keeps describing herself as low, poor and unworthy while she does it. She stakes her life on a cure the King’s doctors have abandoned, chooses her husband in front of the court, obeys his order to go home, and then slips away from it by night. The bed trick is her design, paid for with her own gold. She speaks the play’s title twice, each time as a way of carrying on.

Bertram

Count of Rossillon, the Countess’s son; first appears 1.1

He is young, newly fatherless and a ward of the King, and he wants to be at the war. Ordered to marry a woman he calls a poor physician’s daughter, he gives in to a threat, leaves on the wedding night and wins honour as a cavalry commander in Florence. He courts Diana with music and oaths, hands over the ring of his house to get her into bed, and at court tells lie after lie about the ring and about her. His last lines promise love on a condition.

The Countess of Rossillon

Bertram’s mother, and Helena’s guardian; first appears 1.1

She opens the play in black for her husband and ends it asking the King to forgive her son. Her first response to Helena’s love is alarm, God shield you mean it not, and within a hundred lines she is giving her means and attendants for the trip to Paris. On reading Bertram’s letter she takes Helena’s side against him without hesitation, and in the last scene she identifies the ring Diana holds as the heirloom of her house.

The King of France

Ruler of the court at Paris, dying when the play begins; first appears 1.2

He has given up hope of a cure and at first will not let an unknown young woman try, then agrees on condition that she dies if she fails. Cured, he keeps his promise by force, making a fine speech on virtue against title and following it with threats when Bertram is unpersuaded. In the last scene he forgives Bertram, suspects him of murder, arrests him, loses patience with Diana’s riddles, and at the end offers Diana a husband of her choosing and a dowry, repeating the bargain that started the trouble.

Parolles

Bertram’s companion, a self-styled captain; first appears 1.1

His name is French for words, and he is mostly made of them. Helena knows him for a liar and a coward in the first scene, and Lafew sees through his scarves at their first meeting. He urges Bertram to the war, swears he will recover a lost drum, and when his own comrades take him blindfold he betrays the army and slanders everyone in it. Stripped of his captaincy, he decides to live as the thing he is, and the man who first exposed him ends up feeding him.

Lafew

An old lord of the French court; first appears 1.1

He is the play’s plain judge of character. He brings Helena to the King with a joke about being Cressida’s uncle, marvels at the cure, and is the first to see that Parolles is a window of lattice he can look straight through. After Helena’s supposed death he proposes his own daughter for Bertram, withdraws the offer as soon as Diana’s petition is read, and ends the play in tears, borrowing Parolles’s handkerchief and telling him to wait on him at home.

Diana

Daughter of a widow of Florence, courted by Bertram; first appears 3.5

In her first scene Mariana warns her against the French count and his go-between, and she never wavers. In her one scene alone with Bertram she takes his oaths apart and gets his ring by turning his own argument about honour against him. At court she plays the witness who will not give a straight answer, claims to be married and a maid in the same breath, and risks prison to hold the truth back until Helena comes in.

The Widow of Florence

Diana’s mother, who lodges pilgrims; first appears 3.5

She says she was well born and has come down in the world, and she hesitates to put her reputation at risk. Helena’s proofs of rank and a purse of gold persuade her, and Diana later says her mother told her exactly how Bertram would woo. She travels with Helena to Marseilles and Rossillon and stands beside Diana in front of the King.

The Clown (Lavache)

The Countess’s fool; first appears 1.3

He wants to marry Isbel because the flesh drives him, loses interest after a trip to court, and entertains the Countess with an answer for every question at court. His jokes about cuckolds, the devil and the broad gate to the great fire run close to the play’s darker matter. Parolles alone gives his name, Lavache, when he comes begging in the last act and the Clown will not stand downwind of him.

The two French lords (the Dumaine brothers)

Brothers serving with Bertram in the Florentine war; first appears 2.1

They leave the court for the war in 2.1, carry letters between Florence and Rossillon, and set the trap for Parolles because they know him better than Bertram does. One leads the ambush in a language of his own invention. Waiting for Bertram in 4.3 they judge his seduction of Diana with a frankness nobody uses to his face, and one of them sums the whole play up in a sentence about a mingled yarn.
