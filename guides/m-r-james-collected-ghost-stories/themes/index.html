Curiosity

A few of the dead in these stories earn it, like Mr Abney or Karswell, but more are ruined for doing their jobs well: cataloguing, translating, excavating, buying at the right price. The learning is the fault.

Through the book Dennistoun buys the scrap-book in the first story, Parkins pockets the whistle, Wraxall reads up on Count Magnus, and by A Warning to the Curious the title says it outright.

In the text

As for the fragments of ostensible erudition which are scattered about my pages, hardly anything in them is not pure invention

Preface, 1904

Here a cataloguer of medieval manuscripts admits that his learned apparatus is fiction, and the double negative keeps a scholar’s caution even so. The admission matters because the erudition is what persuades a reader to accept everything else.

It ought to mean, ‘Who is this who is coming?’ Well, the best way to find out is evidently to whistle for him.

“Oh, Whistle, and I’ll Come to You, My Lad”

He reads the warning correctly and treats it as a research question. That single sentence is James’s whole argument about scholarship: the translation is right, the inference is professional, and it is the worst decision in the book.

Documents

A manuscript, a diary, a Latin inscription, a plan of a well: the plot usually runs through a piece of paper that the scholar can read and the reader is shown.

Through the book James prints the evidence (ciphers, abbreviations, bad Latin) so that the reader does the research alongside the victim and reaches the answer at about the same time.

In the text

St Bertrand de Comminges is a decayed town on the spurs of the Pyrenees, not very far from Toulouse, and still nearer to Bagnères-de-Luchon.

Canon Alberic’s Scrap-book

James writes it like a guidebook: a real town, two real reference points, a distance. James begins in the register of somebody who has been there, and the whole method of the collection is in that choice.

there never was, naturally, any such book as that which I quote in “The Treasure of Abbot Thomas”

Preface, 1904

The important word is naturally: he expects you to have believed in the book, and finds it obvious that you should not have. It is the closest thing in the preface to a conjuror explaining a trick.

Touch

Many of his frights are tactile: a mouth under the pillow, hair, damp linen, something that is the wrong shape in the dark. Seeing it whole is nearly always withheld.

Through the book The mouth with teeth that Dunning finds under his pillow in Casting the Runes is the purest case; later stories also work through sound and presence, and A Warning to the Curious ends in a chase along the shingle.

In the text

most of which were read to friends at Christmas-time at King’s College, Cambridge

Preface, 1904

These were written to be spoken, in a room, at night, to people who knew the author. The sentence explains the shape of every story in the book: short, spoken-paced, and building to one moment a listener could react to aloud.

coarse black hairs, longer than ever grew on a human hand; nails rising from the ends of the fingers and curving sharply down and forward, grey, horny and wrinkled

Canon Alberic’s Scrap-book

Every detail is one a naturalist could record (length, colour, curvature, texture), and the horror is that the list is so competent. James frightens by cataloguing, which is what he did for a living.

Churches

Cathedrals, abbeys, prayer-books and Templar graves fill the stories, and the church in them is an archive, never a refuge. Consecrated ground is where the dangerous things were put, on purpose.

Through the book The clergy in the stories are scholars first; when something is finally stopped, it is by a rule of the thing’s own, and prayer has little to do with it.

Belief

The servants and villagers are right about the house, and the educated men in the armchairs are wrong. James was a conservative who gave the people without Latin the best information in story after story.

Through the book Their warnings are ignored in the early stories and acted on far too late in the later ones, which is the only real moral progress in the book.

East Anglia

Flat coast, shingle, martello towers, sea fog, gorse, a golf links. The landscapes are real and specific, and the horror is put down in them like a fence post.

Through the book The named places thicken as the stories go on, until A Warning to the Curious is effectively a walking route along the Suffolk coast with something behind you on it.

In the text

in “Oh, Whistle, and I’ll Come to You” I had Felixstowe in mind

Preface, 1904

He names the seaside town, and the story’s terror is thereby attached to a place with a railway station and a hotel. James understood that a reader frightened at the right coordinates stays frightened on holiday.
