M. R. James: Collected Ghost Stories · M. R. James

What happens

Ghost Stories of an Antiquary (1904) Canon Alberic, the whistle and the other first stories

The first volume sets the method: a scholar, an object, a piece of research, and something that arrives because the research succeeded. Canon Alberic’s scrap-book, the picture that moves between viewings, the whistle at Burnstow, the thing in Abbot Thomas’s well: all of it is here in the first eight.

Canon Alberic’s Scrap-book
An English scholar buys a manuscript from a terrified sacristan at St Bertrand de Comminges, and finds a drawing at the back of it.
Lost Hearts
An orphan arrives at a country house where his guardian has a scholarly interest in the transmigration of souls.
The Mezzotint
An engraving of a country house changes between one viewing and the next, and a group of university men watch it happen in instalments.
“Oh, Whistle, and I’ll Come to You, My Lad”
A professor of ontography digs an ancient whistle out of a Templar preceptory at Burnstow on the East Coast, and blows it.

More Ghost Stories (1911) Runes, cathedral stalls and a library card

In the second volume James’s machinery gets funnier and nastier together. Casting the Runes makes a scholarly grudge into an occult contract with a deadline; The Tractate Middoth puts the horror between library shelves; The Stalls of Barchester Cathedral brings the cathedral close into the centre of his imagination.

Casting the Runes
Karswell passes a slip of paper to the man who had his paper on alchemy turned down, and the recipient has three months to pass it back.
The Tractate Middoth
A library assistant is asked for a Hebrew volume and finds something with cobwebs on its face waiting in the stack.
The Stalls of Barchester Cathedral
An archdeacon’s diary records his discomfort with the woodwork of his own stall, and with what is under the cathedral.

A Thin Ghost (1919) and A Warning to the Curious (1925) Flat coast, cathedral history, a crown in a barrow

The later volumes are drier and in places bleaker. An Episode of Cathedral History puts the thing behind an altar tomb; A View from a Hill invents a pair of binoculars made from boiled bones; and A Warning to the Curious, the best of them, buries one of the three crowns that guard the coast of England and lets a man dig it up.

An Episode of Cathedral History
Victorian restorers take out a pulpit and find an altar tomb nobody has looked behind.
A Warning to the Curious
A man digs up one of the three crowns of East Anglia, and is followed home by whatever is left of its guardian.
A View from a Hill
Borrowed binoculars show a gallows and an abbey where neither now stands, because of what was boiled to make them.

The 1931 additions and the last stories Rats, playing fields, and a vignette

The collection closes with the pieces added when the stories were first gathered in 1931 and the three that came afterwards, including Wailing Well, written for a scout camp, and A Vignette, published in the year he died, which is barely a story and one of the most frightening things he wrote.

Rats
A guest alone on the upper floor of a Suffolk inn turns the key of a locked bedroom and looks in.
Wailing Well
A scout camp, a marked map, and a boy who goes where the map says not to.
A Vignette
A memory of a childhood garden, a hole in a gate, and something looking back through it.

James on the ghost story James’s rules for the ghost story

Seven short pieces set out the rules: a quiet, familiar setting, recognisable characters, no occult jargon, and a ghost that means harm. He also says the reader should be given a window of escape and then have it closed. They are the clearest statement anyone in the form has made about their own method.

Preface, 1904
The settings that are real, the books that are not, and the Christmas readings at King’s.
Some Remarks on Ghost Stories
Malevolence, reticence, ordinary surroundings, and no explanation of the mechanism.
