The Death of Ivan Ilyich · Leo Tolstoy

What happens

Chapter I The funeral

In a break at the Law Courts, Peter Ivanovich reads out a newspaper notice: Ivan Ilych Golovin, Member of the Court of Justice, has died. Each colleague thinks first of the transfers and promotions. Peter Ivanovich goes to the house, crosses himself uncertainly by the coffin, fights a springy pouffe while the widow asks how to get more money from the government, hears that Ivan Ilych screamed for three days, and escapes in time for a game of bridge. Only the servant Gerasim speaks plainly about death.

Chapter I
An interval in the Melvinski trial, the black-bordered announcement, and thoughts of eight hundred roubles more a year.
Chapter I
Schwartz’s wink, the corpse’s reproachful face, the pouffe, the price of a grave, and a pension.

Chapters II–III A life easy, pleasant and decorous

Ivan Ilych’s life, the narrator says, had been most simple and most ordinary and therefore most terrible. A capable, sociable graduate of the School of Law, he rises through the provinces and the new courts of 1864, marries a suitable girl, withdraws from her into his work when marriage becomes unpleasant, and in 1880, passed over and short of money, lands a better post through a change of minister. He furnishes a new house himself, and while showing an upholsterer how to hang the curtains he slips and knocks his side.

Chapter II
A medallion reading respice finem, affairs in the province, and the reformed courts.
Chapter II
Praskovya Fedorovna, the first pregnancy, and a separate, fenced-off world of official duties.
Chapter III
Five thousand roubles, a lucky conversation on a train, antiques, and a fall from a step-ladder.

Chapters IV–V The doctors

A queer taste in his mouth and a discomfort in his left side grow into constant pain and bad temper. A celebrated doctor treats him as he treats prisoners in court and will not say whether the case is serious. Other doctors disagree, his wife blames him, his colleagues joke, and at cards he misses a grand slam. When his brother-in-law’s face tells him how much he has changed, he lies awake thinking of the appendix, and suddenly sees that the question is life and death.

Chapter IV
Floating kidney, chronic catarrh or appendicitis, and one question the doctor will not answer.
Chapter V
A look in the glass, a conversation overheard, an evening of hope, and a night of terror.

Chapters VI–VIII It

He knows that all men are mortal but cannot believe it applies to him. Death, which he calls It, looks at him from behind the flowers in the drawing-room and through every screen he puts up. His family and doctors keep up the lie that he is only ill; the young peasant Gerasim alone tells the truth, carries out his excretions without disgust and holds his legs on his shoulders at night. The doctor calls, a specialist gives some hope, and his wife and daughter go to see Sarah Bernhardt.

Chapter VI
The syllogism from the logic book, the striped leather ball, and a life lost over a curtain.
Chapter VII
The commode, the legs held up, and the lie everyone else insists on.
Chapter VIII
Tea, morphine, the doctor warming his hands, the specialist, and the family dressed for the theatre.

Chapters IX–XI What if my whole life has been wrong?

In the night he weeps at the cruelty of God and hears an inner voice ask what he wants. Reviewing his life, he finds that everything good lies in childhood and that since then he has been going downhill while he thought he was rising. He lies facing the back of the sofa, living in memories. His daughter becomes engaged, he looks at Gerasim’s face and asks whether his whole life has been wrong, and after taking communion to please his wife he sends her away with a scream.

Chapter IX
The black sack, what do you want?, and the thought that he did not live as he ought, dismissed.
Chapter X
French plums of childhood, morocco leather, and a stone falling faster.
Chapter XI
The proposal, the question, the priest, the brief hope, and Go away!

Chapter XII The light

He screams for three days, struggling in the black sack and held back by his belief that his life was a good one. At the end of the third day he falls through and sees a light. His son kisses his hand; he feels sorry for the boy and for his wife, tries to ask forgiveness, and finds that his fear of death has gone. In place of death there is light. Someone says It is finished, and he dies two hours later.
