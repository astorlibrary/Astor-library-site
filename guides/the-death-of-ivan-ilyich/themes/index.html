Death and the body

Everyone knows that men are mortal, and nobody in the story believes it about himself. The colleagues feel it is he who is dead and not I; Peter Ivanovich’s moment of terror at the funeral passes as soon as he remembers it happened to Ivan Ilych; and Ivan Ilych himself, who learned the syllogism about Caius at school, finds it true of Caius and impossible for little Vanya. The story makes the reader live through the months in which an ordinary man is forced to believe it.

Through the book The notice in the Gazette; the corpse’s reproach; It behind the flowers; the black sack; In place of death there was light.

In the text

“Gentlemen,” he said, “Ivan Ilych has died!”

Peter Ivanovich, Chapter I

Death arrives as office news, read out from a newspaper in the middle of a legal argument. The next paragraphs show what the news means to the listeners: a vacancy, a chain of promotions and eight hundred roubles more a year. Tolstoy starts from the world’s view of the event, so that the rest of the book can show it from the inside.

hung a medallion inscribed respice finem on his watch-chain

Chapter II

Respice finem is Latin for look to the end, an old reminder of death, and he wears it as a fashionable ornament. It is the story’s most compact irony. The young man who carries the motto round with him will not look at his own end until it is staring at him from behind the drawing-room flowers.

The lie

What torments Ivan Ilych most is the deception, which everyone accepts, that he is not dying but only ill and needs to keep quiet and follow his treatment. The lie degrades his death to the level of their visits, curtains and sturgeon, and it is kept up by the decorum he has served all his life. He longs to shout at them to stop lying and never does. Only Gerasim and, silently, his son do not lie, and at the end he sees that his whole life has been a deception hiding life and death from him.

Through the book Believe me at the funeral; the doctors’ important air; You feel better, don’t you?; the conventional deception at the theatre party; all you have lived for is falsehood.

In the text

To Ivan Ilych only one question was important: was his case serious or not?

Chapter IV

The patient’s question and the doctor’s question are different questions, and only one of them gets asked. When Ivan Ilych finally puts his, apologising for it, the doctor looks at him over his spectacles as if to say that a prisoner must keep to the questions put to him. Much of the story turns on this gap between the professional’s problem and the sufferer’s.

Gerasim alone did not lie; everything showed that he alone understood the facts of the case and did not consider it necessary to disguise them, but simply felt sorry for his emaciated and enfeebled master.

Chapter VII

Tolstoy’s moral hero is a peasant lad who does the dirtiest job in the house. He is healthy, as the others are, but his health does not offend Ivan Ilych, because he does not pretend. The facts of the case is a lawyer’s phrase, and it is the servant who understands them.

Propriety

Ivan Ilych’s rule of life is that everything should be done easily, pleasantly and decorously, and done as the most highly placed people do it. His youthful sins were committed with clean hands, in clean linen and with French phrases. His house is furnished to be refined and resembles every other house of its class. Propriety governs the funeral too: Peter Ivanovich knows to cross himself and to press the widow’s hand. At the end the correctness of his whole life is exactly what holds him back from the light.

Through the book Il faut que jeunesse se passe; the drawing-room; the dinners that resemble all other dinners; I did everything properly; the justification of his life held him fast.

In the text

the springs of the pouffe, relieved of his weight, rose also and gave him a push.

Chapter I

Tolstoy stages the scene of condolence as farce. The pouffe rebels, creaks and quivers while the widow weeps and asks about money, and the comedy cools Peter Ivanovich’s feelings entirely. The furniture Ivan Ilych chose so carefully has become an obstacle to mourning him.

Ivan Ilych’s life had been most simple and most ordinary and therefore most terrible.

Chapter II

Therefore is the scandalous word. Tolstoy does not say the life was terrible in spite of being ordinary; he says ordinariness is what made it terrible. The verdict comes before the evidence, and the chapter that follows reads like the case for the prosecution, told in a calm, almost admiring voice.

The law and official life

A gifted lawyer, Ivan Ilych reduces every case to what can be written on stamped paper and keeps human relations out of official business; his work is the fenced-off world he retreats to from his marriage. The story turns his profession against him: the celebrated doctor treats him exactly as he treated prisoners, his colleagues treat his death as a vacancy, and in his last reckoning he hears an usher announce the judge and protests that he is not guilty.

Through the book The new courts of 1864; examining magistrate, prosecutor, member of the court; five thousand roubles; the doctor as judge; The judge is coming!

In the text

he considered his duty to be what was so considered by those in authority.

Chapter II

His conscience is borrowed from his superiors. The narrator adds that he was never a toady, only drawn to people of high station as a fly is to the light, and that things which once disgusted him stopped troubling him when he saw good people do them. The sentence explains how a decent man can live a false life without ever doing anything wrong.

The doctor put on just the same air towards him as he himself put on towards an accused person.

Chapter IV

Ivan Ilych recognises his own professional manner in the doctor, and for the first time he is on the wrong side of it. The doctor sums up brilliantly, weighs floating kidney against appendicitis, and ignores the only question that matters to the patient. The story turns the courtroom around, and he cannot appeal.

Marriage

Marriage gives Ivan Ilych satisfaction and the right people approve of it, so he marries, and within a year he decides that marriage must be managed like an official duty: dinner at home, a housewife and a bed, and propriety in public. Husband and wife live in an ocean of veiled hostility with occasional islets of amorousness. In his illness she makes his dying another of the annoyances he causes her, and he hates her with his whole soul, until at the very end he feels sorry for her.

Through the book Why shouldn’t I marry?; the first pregnancy; the forty-five-rouble quarrel over cakes; Jean, my dear; sorry for you too.

In the text

I consider it an affectation to say that my grief prevents my attending to practical affairs.

Praskovya Fedorovna, Chapter I

Her grief and her practicality are equally performed. She means to seem admirably sensible, and she does attend to everything: the plot, the choir, and above all how much can be got from the government. The line shows a widow whose attention was never really on her husband, and prepares us for the marriage the next chapter describes.

he at once retired into his separate fenced-off world of official duties, where he found satisfaction.

Chapter II

Work becomes a fortress against home. The phrase fenced-off world describes his whole method of living: keep each part of life in its compartment, admit only official relations, and let nothing fresh or vital disturb the arrangement. Illness is the one thing that cannot be kept outside the fence.

Pity

What Ivan Ilych wants most, and would be ashamed to confess, is to be pitied as a sick child is pitied. Nobody around him can give it except Gerasim, who pities him without lying, and his son, whose frightened look he recognises. At the end the direction reverses: it is Ivan Ilych who feels sorry for his son and his wife, and in acting for their sake he is released from his suffering and his fear.

Through the book Gerasim holding his legs; the wish to be petted and cried over; Shebek’s visit; the boy kissing his hand; sorry for him, sorry for you too.

In the text

he wished most of all (though he would have been ashamed to confess it) for someone to pity him as a sick child is pitied.

Chapter VII

The important functionary with a greying beard wants to be petted and cried over. When his colleague Shebek arrives, he puts on a severe face and gives an opinion on a decision of the Court of Cassation instead. The contrast between what he needs and what his dignity lets him show is the falsity within him, matching the falsity around him.

“Is it our fault?” Lisa said to her mother. “It’s as if we were to blame! I am sorry for papa, but why should we be tortured?”

Lisa, Chapter XI

Lisa is not cruel, only young, healthy and in love, and her father’s suffering interferes with her happiness. Her complaint shows how the family experiences his dying: as something being done to them. The reader has just seen, from his side, the look of animosity that prompted it.

Self-knowledge

For most of the story Ivan Ilych dismisses the thought that he did not live as he ought, because he did everything properly. The question returns in the night voices of chapter IX and in chapter XI becomes unavoidable: what if my whole life has been wrong? The light comes with the admission that it was not the right thing, and the question of what the right thing is. The story’s argument is that self-knowledge comes late and cannot be reached through correctness.

Through the book What do you want?; going downhill while I imagined I was going up; What if my whole life has been wrong?; it was not the right thing, but that’s no matter.

In the text

the mere fact of the death of a near acquaintance aroused, as usual, in all who heard of it the complacent feeling that, “it is he who is dead and not I.”

Chapter I

As usual is the sharpest word: this is the normal human reaction, and the narrator reports it without surprise. The complacency is the attitude the story will dismantle. Ivan Ilych will spend his last months discovering that he, too, had always assumed death was something that happened to other people.

It is as if I had been going downhill while I imagined I was going up.

Ivan Ilych, Chapter IX

His career has been one long ascent in rank, and he now reads it as a descent. The next sentence explains the paradox: he was going up in public opinion, but to the same extent life was ebbing away. The image anticipates the stone falling faster in chapter X and the railway carriage in chapter XII.
