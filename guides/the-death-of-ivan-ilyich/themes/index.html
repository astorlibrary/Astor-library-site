The Death of Ivan Ilyich · Leo Tolstoy

Themes

Death and the body

Everyone knows that men are mortal, and nobody in the story believes it about himself. The colleagues feel it is he who is dead and not I; Peter Ivanovich’s moment of terror at the funeral passes as soon as he remembers it happened to Ivan Ilych; and Ivan Ilych himself, who learned the syllogism about Caius at school, finds it true of Caius and impossible for little Vanya. The story makes the reader live through the months in which an ordinary man is forced to believe it.

The notice in the Gazette; the corpse’s reproach; It behind the flowers; the black sack; In place of death there was light.

“Gentlemen,” he said, “Ivan Ilych has died!”

Peter Ivanovich, Chapter I

the mere fact of the death of a near acquaintance aroused, as usual, in all who heard of it the complacent feeling that, “it is he who is dead and not I.”

Chapter I

The lie

What torments Ivan Ilych most is the deception, which everyone accepts, that he is not dying but only ill and needs to keep quiet and follow his treatment. The lie degrades his death to the level of their visits, curtains and sturgeon, and it is kept up by the decorum he has served all his life. He longs to shout at them to stop lying and never does. Only Gerasim and, silently, his son do not lie, and at the end he sees that his whole life has been a deception hiding life and death from him.

Believe me at the funeral; the doctors’ important air; You feel better, don’t you?; the conventional deception at the theatre party; all you have lived for is falsehood.

I consider it an affectation to say that my grief prevents my attending to practical affairs.

Praskovya Fedorovna, Chapter I

“It’s God’s will. We shall all come to it some day,” said Gerasim, displaying his teeth

Gerasim, Chapter I

Propriety

Ivan Ilych’s rule of life is that everything should be done easily, pleasantly and decorously, and done as the most highly placed people do it. His youthful sins were committed with clean hands, in clean linen and with French phrases. His house is furnished to be refined and resembles every other house of its class. Propriety governs the funeral too: Peter Ivanovich knows to cross himself and to press the widow’s hand. At the end the correctness of his whole life is exactly what holds him back from the light.

Il faut que jeunesse se passe; the drawing-room; the dinners that resemble all other dinners; I did everything properly; the justification of his life held him fast.

he stopped and winked at him, as if to say: “Ivan Ilych has made a mess of things—not like you and me.”

Chapter I

The law and official life

A gifted lawyer, Ivan Ilych reduces every case to what can be written on stamped paper and keeps human relations out of official business; his work is the fenced-off world he retreats to from his marriage. The story turns his profession against him: the celebrated doctor treats him exactly as he treated prisoners, his colleagues treat his death as a vacancy, and in his last reckoning he hears an usher announce the judge and protests that he is not guilty.

The new courts of 1864; examining magistrate, prosecutor, member of the court; five thousand roubles; the doctor as judge; The judge is coming!

“Gentlemen,” he said, “Ivan Ilych has died!”

Peter Ivanovich, Chapter I

he considered his duty to be what was so considered by those in authority.

Chapter II

Marriage

Marriage gives Ivan Ilych satisfaction and the right people approve of it, so he marries, and within a year he decides that marriage must be managed like an official duty: dinner at home, a housewife and a bed, and propriety in public. Husband and wife live in an ocean of veiled hostility with occasional islets of amorousness. In his illness she makes his dying another of the annoyances he causes her, and he hates her with his whole soul, until at the very end he feels sorry for her.

Why shouldn’t I marry?; the first pregnancy; the forty-five-rouble quarrel over cakes; Jean, my dear; sorry for you too.

I consider it an affectation to say that my grief prevents my attending to practical affairs.

Praskovya Fedorovna, Chapter I

he at once retired into his separate fenced-off world of official duties, where he found satisfaction.

Chapter II

Pity

What Ivan Ilych wants most, and would be ashamed to confess, is to be pitied as a sick child is pitied. Nobody around him can give it except Gerasim, who pities him without lying, and his son, whose frightened look he recognises. At the end the direction reverses: it is Ivan Ilych who feels sorry for his son and his wife, and in acting for their sake he is released from his suffering and his fear.

Gerasim holding his legs; the wish to be petted and cried over; Shebek’s visit; the boy kissing his hand; sorry for him, sorry for you too.

Gerasim alone did not lie; everything showed that he alone understood the facts of the case and did not consider it necessary to disguise them, but simply felt sorry for his emaciated and enfeebled master.

Chapter VII

he wished most of all (though he would have been ashamed to confess it) for someone to pity him as a sick child is pitied.

Chapter VII

Self-knowledge

For most of the story Ivan Ilych dismisses the thought that he did not live as he ought, because he did everything properly. The question returns in the night voices of chapter IX and in chapter XI becomes unavoidable: what if my whole life has been wrong? The light comes with the admission that it was not the right thing, and the question of what the right thing is. The story’s argument is that self-knowledge comes late and cannot be reached through correctness.

What do you want?; going downhill while I imagined I was going up; What if my whole life has been wrong?; it was not the right thing, but that’s no matter.
