The Death of Ivan Ilyich · Leo Tolstoy

Form and language

Beginning with the death

A narrative that shows how a life ends before telling how it was lived.

Chapter I is the funeral, seen by colleagues who are thinking about promotions and bridge. The title has already announced the death, so the reader follows Ivan Ilych’s life knowing what it is heading towards and watching how little the people around him care.

“Gentlemen,” he said, “Ivan Ilych has died!”

Peter Ivanovich, Chapter I

The intruding narrator

A narrator who interrupts the story to comment, digress or talk about the telling.

Tolstoy’s narrator passes sentence at the start of chapter II: most simple and most ordinary and therefore most terrible. He adds dry verdicts throughout, on the house that resembled all others and the pleasures that were those of vanity, and the comments hold the reader to a judgement Ivan Ilych reaches only at the end.

the mere fact of the death of a near acquaintance aroused, as usual, in all who heard of it the complacent feeling that, “it is he who is dead and not I.”

Chapter I

Free indirect style

Third-person narration that takes on a character’s own thoughts and phrasing without quotation marks.

The narration slips into Ivan Ilych’s mind with the Caius syllogism, the screens, It, and the thoughts about the kidney, so that the reader shares his evasions before seeing through them. In chapter VIII a single day is lived through with him, hour by hour.

To Ivan Ilych only one question was important: was his case serious or not?

Chapter IV

The repeated sentence

A phrase or image that comes back through a work and gathers meaning.

Easily, pleasantly and decorously describes his life again and again until it becomes a charge against it. It, with a capital letter, stands for death. The black sack, the screens and the light recur, and Gerasim’s we shall all come to it is said at the funeral and echoed at the sickbed.

“It’s God’s will. We shall all come to it some day,” said Gerasim, displaying his teeth

Gerasim, Chapter I

Analogy

An extended comparison that explains one thing by another.

Three comparisons carry the story: the doctor treats the patient as a judge treats a prisoner; Ivan Ilych’s decline is a stone falling faster as it nears the ground; his last discovery is like the feeling in a railway carriage when you think you are going backwards and suddenly realise you are going forwards.

The doctor put on just the same air towards him as he himself put on towards an accused person.

Chapter IV

Things in the house

Objects that carry meaning beyond their use in the plot.

The pouffe whose springs keep rising at the funeral, the pink cretonne Ivan Ilych chose, the curtains he died for, the scratched table and the album in the drawing-room, the medallion reading respice finem: the story’s things show a life spent on appearances.

the springs of the pouffe, relieved of his weight, rose also and gave him a push.

Chapter I

Light and dark imagery

Contrasts of light and darkness used to carry meaning.

There was light and now there is darkness, he thinks in chapter V; he struggles in a black sack and a black hole, and falls through to a light at the bottom. The contrast is simple and structural, and the last chapter resolves it.

It seemed to him that he and his pain were being thrust into a narrow, deep black sack, but though they were pushed further and further in they could not be pushed to the bottom.

Chapter IX

Irony

Saying one thing in words whose real meaning is different, or letting events contradict what characters expect.

He dies of a fall from a step-ladder while arranging a drawing-room to impress people; he wore respice finem, look to the end, on his watch-chain; he boasts that another man might have been killed; the doctor looks at him as if he were a prisoner who will not keep to the questions put to him.

he stopped and winked at him, as if to say: “Ivan Ilych has made a mess of things—not like you and me.”
