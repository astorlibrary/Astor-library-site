King Lear · William Shakespeare

Characters

King Lear

King of Britain; first appears 1.1

A man who has never in his life been contradicted and cannot tell the difference between love and the language of love. He gives his power away in the first scene; what the play takes from him is the assumptions that came with it, and what is left underneath turns out to be capable of pity for the first time at eighty.

Cordelia

Lear’s youngest daughter; first appears 1.1

She speaks about a hundred lines in the whole play and is absent from the middle three acts, which is part of the design: the play takes her away again and again. In the first scene she will not put a price on love, which is not the same as defiance, and it costs her a kingdom in under fifty lines.

Goneril

Lear’s eldest daughter, Duchess of Albany; first appears 1.1

She wins the first scene by saying what is wanted and spends the rest of the play being right about her father’s knights and wrong about everything else. The play gives her a real grievance and then lets her overrun it: by Act IV she is poisoning her sister and writing to a man about killing her husband.

Regan

Lear’s second daughter, Duchess of Cornwall; first appears 1.1

She follows her sister’s lead in Act I and outruns her afterwards. It is Regan who suggests plucking out the second eye, and Regan who kills the servant who objects. Widowed in the same scene, she immediately begins competing for Edmund, and dies of her sister’s poison without ever suspecting it.

Earl of Gloucester

Lear’s courtier, father of Edgar and Edmund; first appears 1.1

He jokes about his illegitimate son’s conception in the play’s opening exchange, with the son standing beside him. His plot rhymes with Lear’s without repeating it: Lear cannot read speech, Gloucester cannot read a document, and it is Gloucester who is punished physically for a loyalty he only just found the nerve to act on.

Edmund

Gloucester’s illegitimate son; first appears 1.1

The only character who tells the audience his plan and keeps to it. He argues that the law’s distinction between his brother and himself is arbitrary, which is true, and then uses the point to justify anything at all. His attempt to do one good thing arrives about ninety seconds too late to save Cordelia.

Edgar

Gloucester’s legitimate son; first appears 1.2

He is tricked out of his name in Act I and spends four acts wearing other people’s, of which Poor Tom is only the first. His care for his father is real and strangely cruel (he lets Gloucester believe in a miracle and does not tell him who is holding his arm), and he ends the play holding a kingdom nobody would want.

Earl of Kent

Lear’s banished servant, disguised as Caius; first appears 1.1

He contradicts the king to his face in the first scene, is banished for it, and comes back in disguise the same week to carry on doing the job. His loyalty is not sentimental: he trips Oswald, insults Cornwall and gets himself locked in the stocks. At the end he declines the kingdom because he has an appointment elsewhere.

The Fool

Lear’s licensed jester; first appears 1.4

He arrives after Cordelia leaves and vanishes after the mock trial, which has made some readers connect them. His jokes are all one joke, repeated until it stops being funny: you gave away the thing that made you worth obeying. He is the only person allowed to say it, and he keeps saying it through the worst of the weather.
