King Lear · William Shakespeare

Form and language

The double plot

Two parallel stories staged so that each comments on the other.

Shakespeare’s only tragedy with a fully developed subplot: Lear misreads speech, Gloucester misreads a document, and the two ruined fathers meet in 4.6, so the comparison happens on stage.

Animal imagery

Language that reduces people to beasts, appetite or prey.

Pelicans, kites, wolves, serpent’s teeth, the bare forked animal and a dog obeyed in office: the play measures how far a person can be stripped by how much animal vocabulary is used about them.

How sharper than a serpent's tooth it is To have a thankless child!

King Lear, 1.4.260

Disguise

A character adopting another identity in full view of the audience.

Kent and Edgar are both disguised for most of the play, and both keep the secret from the people it would comfort; Edgar’s silence to his father takes a convention of comedy and makes it a long, well-meant cruelty.

As flies to wanton boys, are we to the gods. They kill us for their sport.

Gloucester, 4.1.38

Prose against verse

Shifting between metre and prose to mark status, sanity or intimacy.

Lear’s verse breaks down into prose on the heath and in the Dover fields, so the collapse can be heard before it is stated; the Fool works almost entirely in prose and rhyme.

Thou art the thing itself: unaccommodated man is no more but such a poor bare, forked animal as thou art.

King Lear, 3.4.94

The storm

Weather staged as an active force.

Lear addresses the storm directly, commands it, and then concedes that it owes him nothing: a three-stage argument with the sky that no amount of thunder machinery can win for him.

Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks! rage! blow!

King Lear, 3.2.1

Riddle and aside

Speech aimed past its apparent hearer, at the audience or at nobody.

The Fool’s riddles say to the theatre what nobody at court may say to the king; Edgar’s asides during 4.1 let the audience hold a piece of knowledge that would change everything if it were spoken aloud.

Thou, nature, art my goddess; to thy law My services are bound. Wherefore should I Stand in the plague of custom, and permit The curiosity of nations to deprive me,

Edmund, 1.2.1
