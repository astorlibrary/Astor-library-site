King Lear · William Shakespeare

Context

Dates

1587
The second edition of Holinshed’s Chronicles Carries the story of King Leir and his three daughters, drawn from Geoffrey of Monmouth, with a restoration that Shakespeare removes.
1590
Sidney’s Arcadia Supplies the blinded father and the wronged son that Shakespeare builds into the Gloucester plot.
1594
The anonymous King Leir performed An older, more openly Christian play on the same story; printed in 1605, and probably known to Shakespeare.
1603
Harsnett’s Declaration of Egregious Popish Impostures The source of the fiends Poor Tom names, and evidence that the play was written after it appeared.
1606
Performed before King James at Whitehall The Stationers’ Register record and the quarto title page both point to St Stephen’s night in the Christmas holidays.
1608
The First Quarto printed Printed for Nathaniel Butter as a True Chronicle Historie, and linking the play to the King’s Men at the Globe.
1619
Second Quarto, falsely dated 1608 A reprint that carries the 1608 date on its title page; its true year was not established until the twentieth century.
1623
The First Folio text Printed as The Tragedie of King Lear, with over a hundred lines the quarto lacks and around three hundred fewer of the quarto’s.
1681
Nahum Tate’s History of King Lear Removes the Fool, marries Cordelia to Edgar and lets Lear live. It held the English stage for more than a century.
1838
Macready restores the Fool and the tragic ending Priscilla Horton played the restored Fool; Shakespeare’s ending returned to public stage memory.
1971
Peter Brook’s film Made from his 1962 Stratford production, with Paul Scofield as Lear and almost every trace of comfort stripped out.
2007
Trevor Nunn’s RSC production with Ian McKellen A stormy, thrust-stage staging that included an on-stage hanging of the Fool.

Places

Dover
The cliff Gloucester means to die from, and the coast where Cordelia’s army lands.
Gloucester
The earldom of the second family, and the title Edmund is given for betraying his father.
Cornwall
Regan’s dukedom, and the source of the play’s most casual cruelty.
Kent
The earldom of the man who is banished for honesty in the first scene and comes back disguised.
Albany
The old name for the land north of the Forth, and Goneril’s husband’s title: a duke who slowly changes sides.
Burgundy
The suitor who withdraws when Cordelia’s dowry disappears, leaving France to take her without one.

Critics

Samuel Johnson

The Plays of William Shakespeare (1765)

Johnson defended Nahum Tate’s version, in which Cordelia survives, on the ground that audiences preferred it. He admitted that he had been “so shocked by Cordelia’s death” that he did not know whether he had ever read the last scenes again until he had to revise them as editor.

Charles Lamb

“On the Tragedies of Shakspeare”, in The Reflector (1811)

Lamb argued that the play is “essentially impossible to be represented on a stage”: the storm and an old man tottering about in it are poor things to watch, while the greatness of Lear is in his mind and can only be met in reading.

A. C. Bradley

Shakespearean Tragedy (1904)

Bradley thought Lear the greatest of the plays but too large for the stage. He suggested that Lear dies believing that Cordelia is alive, and so dies in joy, and he read the play as a story of redemption through suffering.

Jan Kott

“King Lear, or Endgame”, in Shakespeare Our Contemporary (1964)

Kott read the play beside Samuel Beckett: a world without justice or providence, in which suffering has no meaning and the grotesque and the tragic meet. His reading influenced Peter Brook’s production of 1962 with Paul Scofield as Lear.

Stanley Cavell

“The Avoidance of Love: A Reading of King Lear”, in Must We Mean What We Say? (1969)

Cavell argues that Lear’s demand for public declarations of love is a way of avoiding being loved, because real love would expose him. Cordelia’s refusal threatens him for that reason, and the same avoidance runs through the play’s other relationships.
