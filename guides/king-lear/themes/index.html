King Lear · William Shakespeare

Themes

The word ‘nothing’

The word runs through both plots like a fault line: Cordelia’s answer, Lear’s reply, Edmund’s ‘nothing’ as he pockets a forged letter, the Fool’s arithmetic of noughts. The play sets speech that performs feeling against speech that measures it, and shows a state making irreversible decisions on the strength of the first.

A refusal in 1.1, a joke through Act I, a fact by Act III, when Lear has nothing left to compare with, and an answer in 5.3 where a father asks a dead daughter to speak.

Unhappy that I am, I cannot heave My heart into my mouth: I love your majesty According to my bond; no more nor less.

Cordelia, 1.1.90

Nothing will come of nothing: speak again.

King Lear, 1.1.89

Sight, blindness and judgement

Kent tells Lear to see better in the first scene, and the play spends five acts taking the metaphor literally. Gloucester says he stumbled when he saw; Lear, mad, tells a blind man that a man may see how this world goes with no eyes. Understanding in this play is consistently something that arrives after the organ of understanding has been destroyed.

Figurative in Act I, appallingly literal in 3.7, and reversed in 4.6, where the blind man and the mad king conduct the play’s clearest analysis of power.

See better, Lear; and let me still remain The true blank of thine eye.

Kent, 1.1.160

Lest it see more, prevent it. Out, vile jelly!

Cornwall, 3.7.84

Nature and the unnatural

Everyone in the play appeals to nature and they mean incompatible things by it. Lear calls on it to make his daughter sterile; Edmund makes it his goddess and means appetite without law; Gloucester reads it in eclipses. The storm is the argument brought on stage, and it answers nobody.

Invoked as authority in Acts I and II, unanswering in Act III, and reduced in 3.4 to a bare forked animal that owes the worm no silk.

Thou, nature, art my goddess; to thy law My services are bound. Wherefore should I Stand in the plague of custom, and permit The curiosity of nations to deprive me,

Edmund, 1.2.1

Now, gods, stand up for bastards!

Edmund, 1.2.22

Justice and authority

The play holds two trials. The first is a mock one in a farmhouse, where a mad king arraigns a joint-stool; the second is a duel between brothers decided by force. In between, Lear works out that a dog is obeyed in office and that the difference between the justice and the thief is a change of places, perhaps the most radical political speech Shakespeare ever gives a king.

Ceremonial in 1.1, parodied in 3.6, dismantled in 4.6, and finally left to a chivalric combat that settles a private quarrel and saves nobody.

Nothing will come of nothing: speak again.

King Lear, 1.1.89

Thou, nature, art my goddess; to thy law My services are bound. Wherefore should I Stand in the plague of custom, and permit The curiosity of nations to deprive me,

Edmund, 1.2.1

Age, need and dependence

The quarrel that drives Lear onto the heath is about a number: a hundred knights, then fifty, then twenty-five, then what need one. The daughters’ case is not obviously wrong, and the play knows it. What Lear finds in answer is that need is not the measure of a life, an argument he can only make once he has lost the argument.

Negotiated downwards through Act II, answered in 2.4, and turned outwards in 3.4, where the same reasoning is applied to people who never had anything to lose.

Unhappy that I am, I cannot heave My heart into my mouth: I love your majesty According to my bond; no more nor less.

Cordelia, 1.1.90

Who is it that can tell me who I am?

King Lear, 1.4.200

Service and loyalty

The play’s decency is almost entirely in its servants. Kent sits in the stocks for a master who banished him; an unnamed servant of Cornwall’s draws a sword to stop a blinding and dies for it; an old tenant leads Gloucester when his family cannot. Rank organises the tragedy, but obligation runs the other way.

Punished in 2.2, fatal in 3.7, and finally the only bond left standing in 5.3, where Kent follows his master out of the play.

See better, Lear; and let me still remain The true blank of thine eye.

Kent, 1.1.160
