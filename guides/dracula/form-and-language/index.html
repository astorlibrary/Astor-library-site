Dracula · Bram Stoker

Form and language

Documentary form

A narrative assembled entirely from papers, recordings and cuttings, with no outside narrator.

Journals, letters, telegrams, a ship’s log, a newspaper cutting, phonograph cylinders and a memorandum. Even the moment of the Count’s death is somebody’s diary entry written afterwards.

Welcome to my house! Enter freely and of your own will!

Count Dracula, Chapter II

Multiple narrators

Several first-person accounts, each limited to what that writer knew at the time.

Seven or eight hands write this novel, and their ignorance is the plot: Harker knows what the Demeter was carrying and cannot tell Seward, who is treating the result.

I am all in a sea of wonders. I doubt; I fear; I think strange things, which I dare not confess to my own soul.

Jonathan Harker, Chapter II

Dramatic irony

The reader knowing more than the characters, because of how the material is arranged.

Stoker prints the Demeter’s log immediately before Lucy’s sleepwalking, so a reader watches a diagnosis being missed for five chapters by people with all the information in separate drawers.

God and the Blessed Virgin and the saints help a poor ignorant soul trying to do his duty.

The captain of the Demeter, Chapter VII

Idiolect

Giving each character a distinct and consistent way of speaking.

Van Helsing’s broken syntax, Quincey’s Texan idiom, the Whitby fishermen’s Yorkshire and Renfield’s ledger-keeping precision make the documents identifiable before a heading names them.

Listen to them⁠—the children of the night. What music they make!

Count Dracula, Chapter II

Gothic setting

Landscape and architecture used to generate dread and to trap characters.

Stoker puts the castle in the first quarter and then relocates the Gothic to a ruined Essex estate, a Hampstead tomb and a Piccadilly house with the dust sheets still on.

Listen to them⁠—the children of the night. What music they make!

Count Dracula, Chapter II

Inventory and catalogue

Lists of objects, dates and quantities used to make the impossible feel administrative.

Fifty boxes counted and recounted, sailing dates, train timetables, Renfield’s tally of flies: the horror is tracked by arithmetic, and arithmetic is what eventually finds it.

We have on our side power of combination⁠—a power denied to the vampire kind; we have sources of science; we are free to act and think; and the hours of the day and the night are ours equally.

Professor Van Helsing, Chapter XVIII
