Blood and contagion

Blood in this novel is property, inheritance and infection. Dracula lectures Harker on the blood of his race before anyone has been bitten; Lucy receives blood from four men in under a fortnight; and the final horror at the Harkers’ bedside is a forced exchange: Dracula makes Mina drink his blood. Nothing here stays inside one body.

Through the book A boast about ancestry in Chapter III, a transfusion ward in Chapters IX to XII, and a scene in Chapter XXI that the novel’s own narrator can barely bring himself to write down.

In the text

Blood is too precious a thing in these days of dishonourable peace; and the glories of the great races are as a tale that is told.

Count Dracula, Chapter III

He has spent several pages using blood to mean lineage, and here he prices it. The double sense is set up before a single drop is taken, so that every later transfusion carries an argument about descent as well as a medical procedure. A tale that is told is from the Psalms, and he means it as a complaint about being finished.

The blood is the life! The blood is the life!

R. M. Renfield, Chapter XI

He is quoting Deuteronomy, and Seward files it as a symptom. Every element of the diagnosis the doctors need is in this room (an appetite for blood, a servant who answers to a master, a man who knows the neighbouring house is occupied), and it is all written down as a case note.

Modernity against the old world

Stoker arms his hunters with the newest equipment available in 1897: shorthand, a typewriter, a phonograph, telegrams, blood transfusion, Kodak photographs and railway timetables. The joke is that none of it detects anything. What finally works is the oldest office skill in the book, which is putting papers in date order.

Through the book Harker’s shorthand in Chapter I, Seward’s cylinders in Chapter V, and Mina’s typescript in Chapter XIV, which converts a pile of private records into a usable case.

In the text

I shall get my typewriter this very hour and begin transcribing. Then we shall be ready for other eyes if required.

Mina Harker, Chapter XIV

This is the sentence on which the second half of the novel turns, and it is about office equipment. Ready for other eyes is the whole problem stated in five words: private records held by separate people cannot be compared, and a hunt that has failed for three hundred pages becomes possible the moment somebody makes copies.

We were struck with the fact, that in all the mass of material of which the record is composed, there is hardly one authentic document; nothing but a mass of typewriting

Jonathan Harker, Note

The book’s last move is to withdraw its own evidence. Mina’s typing saved their lives by making the records comparable, and it destroyed them as proof by leaving no originals. The same act did both, and a novel that has spent six hundred pages insisting on documentation ends by admitting it has none.

Arrival and invasion

The threat enters England legally. A solicitor conveys the property, a shipping firm moves the cargo, and a carter delivers the boxes to Purfleet for a fee. Late-Victorian anxieties about what might come in through the docks are given a shape that is less an army than a change of address.

Through the book A purchase completed in Chapter II, a ship that reaches Whitby in Chapter VII, and a chase in the last four chapters that simply reverses the route.

In the text

Welcome to my house! Enter freely and of your own will!

Count Dracula, Chapter II

It sounds like hospitality and it is a legal condition: the folklore rule that a vampire must be invited is being satisfied in advance, and Harker signs up to it by walking through the door. The Count makes no move to meet him, and Harker notes it twice: the crossing has to be the guest’s own act.

God and the Blessed Virgin and the saints help a poor ignorant soul trying to do his duty.

The captain of the Demeter, Chapter VII

The log is the purest example of the novel’s method: a form designed for positions, weather and watches, kept up by a man whose crew is vanishing one at a time. He records the facts he is permitted to record and can name nothing else, and the empty ship reaches Whitby carrying both its cargo and an official document that proves something happened without being able to say what.

Women, desire and protection

The novel separates its two women and treats them very differently. Lucy’s appetite is punished in a tomb; Mina is useful and is therefore shielded from the work, which is how she is reached. The men’s protectiveness is presented as chivalry and shown, repeatedly, to be the tactical error that costs most.

Through the book Lucy’s question about marrying three men, the three women in the castle, and the decision in Chapter XVIII to keep Mina out of the council.

In the text

There are darknesses in life, and there are lights; you are one of the lights.

Professor Van Helsing, Chapter XIV

He says it to the person who has just done the filing, and four chapters later the men decide to keep her out of their council. The novel praises Mina in the language of light and then removes her from the room where the work is done.

Unclean! Unclean! Even the Almighty shuns my polluted flesh! I must bear this mark of shame upon my forehead until the Judgment Day.

Mina Harker, Chapter XXII

The cry is from Leviticus, where it is what a leper is required to shout to warn others off, and Mina applies it to herself before anyone else can. The novel has her take up the vocabulary of ritual pollution for something done to her while she was drugged and held down, and nobody in the room corrects her.

Faith and science

Seward can only see a case of anaemia because anaemia is what his training allows. Van Helsing’s method is to keep an open mind past the point where his profession would close it, and to use a communion wafer and a garlic flower as instruments. The book sets belief and evidence against each other and then makes them cooperate.

Through the book Seward’s scepticism in Chapters IX to XII, Van Helsing’s lecture on the limits of science in Chapter XIV, and his formal statement of the case in Chapter XVIII.

In the text

Ah, it is the fault of our science that it wants to explain all; and if it explain not, then it says there is nothing to explain.

Professor Van Helsing, Chapter XIV

The broken grammar makes the argument sound folkloric, but it is a precise objection: a method that treats the unexplained as the non-existent will always return the answer it started with. Stoker gives his most modern characters the newest instruments in the world and then has the oldest man in the room point out that instruments only find what they are built to find.

Now God be thanked that all has not been in vain! See! the snow is not more stainless than her forehead!

Professor Van Helsing, Chapter XXVII

The victory is announced as a fact about a woman’s complexion. Van Helsing checks Mina’s forehead before he checks anything else, and the novel closes its plot by restoring her to a state of visible purity, which tells a reader a good deal about what was thought to have been lost.

Records and proof

Everything the reader knows arrives as a document, and the last page points out that hardly one of those documents is authentic. Seven years after the events the survivors hold a bundle of typescript that would convince nobody, and the novel ends by not asking for belief at all.

Through the book Harker’s fear in Chapter IV that his journal will never be read, Mina’s transcriptions in the middle chapters, and the admission in the Note that there is hardly one authentic document among them.

In the text

The last I saw of Count Dracula was his kissing his hand to me; with a red light of triumph in his eyes, and with a smile that Judas in hell might be proud of.

Jonathan Harker, Chapter IV

The triumph is over paperwork: the Count has just obtained three post-dated letters that will keep anyone from looking for Harker until long after he is dead. Stoker gives the villain his most theatrical exit in the novel at the exact moment his weapon is a set of forged dates.

the consensus of their excuses is that they had been with a “bloofer lady.”

Chapter XIII

The newspaper reports the phrase as a childish fashion and prints it in inverted commas to keep its distance. ‘Bloofer’ is a small child’s attempt at ‘beautiful’, so the most frightening development in the novel reaches the reader as a piece of quaint local colour in a light news column, which is how it reaches the characters.
