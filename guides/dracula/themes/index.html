Dracula · Bram Stoker

Themes

Blood and contagion

Blood in this novel is property, inheritance and infection. Dracula lectures Harker on the blood of his race before anyone has been bitten; Lucy receives blood from four men in under a fortnight; and the final horror at the Harkers’ bedside is a forced exchange: Dracula makes Mina drink his blood. Nothing here stays inside one body.

A boast about ancestry in Chapter III, a transfusion ward in Chapters IX to XII, and a scene in Chapter XXI that the novel’s own narrator can barely bring himself to write down.

Blood is too precious a thing in these days of dishonourable peace; and the glories of the great races are as a tale that is told.

Count Dracula, Chapter III

This man belongs to me! Beware how you meddle with him, or you’ll have to deal with me.

Count Dracula, Chapter III

Modernity against the old world

Stoker arms his hunters with the newest equipment available in 1897: shorthand, a typewriter, a phonograph, telegrams, blood transfusion, Kodak photographs and railway timetables. The joke is that none of it detects anything. What finally works is the oldest office skill in the book, which is putting papers in date order.

Harker’s shorthand in Chapter I, Seward’s cylinders in Chapter V, and Mina’s typescript in Chapter XIV, which converts a pile of private records into a usable case.

I shall get my typewriter this very hour and begin transcribing. Then we shall be ready for other eyes if required.

Mina Harker, Chapter XIV

We have on our side power of combination⁠—a power denied to the vampire kind; we have sources of science; we are free to act and think; and the hours of the day and the night are ours equally.

Professor Van Helsing, Chapter XVIII

Arrival and invasion

The threat enters England legally. A solicitor conveys the property, a shipping firm moves the cargo, and a carter delivers the boxes to Purfleet for a fee. Late-Victorian anxieties about what might come in through the docks are given a shape that is less an army than a change of address.

A purchase completed in Chapter II, a ship that reaches Whitby in Chapter VII, and a chase in the last four chapters that simply reverses the route.

Welcome to my house! Enter freely and of your own will!

Count Dracula, Chapter II

Listen to them⁠—the children of the night. What music they make!

Count Dracula, Chapter II

Women, desire and protection

The novel separates its two women and treats them very differently. Lucy’s appetite is punished in a tomb; Mina is useful and is therefore shielded from the work, which is exactly how she is reached. The men’s protectiveness is presented as chivalry and shown, repeatedly, to be the tactical error that costs most.

Lucy’s question about marrying three men, the three women in the castle, and the decision in Chapter XVIII to keep Mina out of the council.

This man belongs to me! Beware how you meddle with him, or you’ll have to deal with me.

Count Dracula, Chapter III

There are darknesses in life, and there are lights; you are one of the lights.

Professor Van Helsing, Chapter XIV

Faith and science

Seward can only see a case of anaemia because anaemia is what his training allows. Van Helsing’s method is to keep an open mind past the point where his profession would close it, and to use a communion wafer and a garlic flower as instruments. The book sets belief and evidence against each other and then makes them cooperate.

Seward’s scepticism in Chapters IX to XII, Van Helsing’s lecture on the limits of science in Chapter XIV, and his formal statement of the case in Chapter XVIII.

The blood is the life! The blood is the life!

R. M. Renfield, Chapter XI

Ah, it is the fault of our science that it wants to explain all; and if it explain not, then it says there is nothing to explain.

Professor Van Helsing, Chapter XIV

Records and proof

Everything the reader knows arrives as a document, and the last page points out that hardly one of those documents is authentic. Seven years after the events the survivors hold a bundle of typescript that would convince nobody, and the novel ends by not asking for belief at all.

Harker’s fear in Chapter IV that his journal will never be read, Mina’s transcriptions in the middle chapters, and the admission in the Note that there is hardly one authentic document among them.

I am all in a sea of wonders. I doubt; I fear; I think strange things, which I dare not confess to my own soul.

Jonathan Harker, Chapter II

The last I saw of Count Dracula was his kissing his hand to me; with a red light of triumph in his eyes, and with a smile that Judas in hell might be proud of.

Jonathan Harker, Chapter IV
