Dracula · Bram Stoker

Context

Dates

1819
Polidori’s The Vampyre is published Begun during the same 1816 Geneva summer as Frankenstein, and the story that made the vampire an aristocrat instead of a peasant revenant.
1847
Bram Stoker is born in Dublin He was bedridden until about the age of seven, and later became an athlete at Trinity College.
1872
Sheridan Le Fanu’s Carmilla appears in In a Glass Darkly A fellow Dubliner’s vampire novella, and the closest predecessor to Stoker’s handling of desire and illness.
1878
Stoker becomes business manager of the Lyceum Theatre Twenty-seven years working for Henry Irving, whose stage presence is often heard behind the Count’s manners.
1890
Stoker begins his working notes The surviving papers show a planned vampire novel taking shape before the name Dracula or the Transylvanian setting arrives.
1890
Stoker spends the summer at Whitby The harbour, the abbey steps, the churchyard seat and the Russian schooner story all reached the novel from this visit.
1897
Dracula published by Archibald Constable and Company A staged reading was given at the Lyceum in the same year, chiefly to secure the dramatic copyright.
1912
Stoker dies in London He had written a dozen other novels, none of which is much read.
1922
Murnau’s Nosferatu An unauthorised adaptation with the names changed; Stoker’s widow sued, and the court ordered the negatives destroyed.
1924
Hamilton Deane’s authorised stage version opens At the Grand Theatre, Derby; the evening dress and the cloak with a standing collar come from the play, and the novel has neither.
1931
Universal’s Dracula with Bela Lugosi Adapted from the stage version, which is the reason most readers arrive expecting a very different Count.

Places

Bistritz
Where Harker sleeps before the last stage, and where the landlady presses a crucifix on him.
The Borgo Pass
The road where the Count’s calèche takes over from the public coach, exactly at midnight.
Whitby
The harbour the Demeter runs into, and the churchyard seat where Lucy is found.
Purfleet
Carfax, the estate bought by post, with Seward’s asylum on the other side of the wall.
Hampstead
The Westenra tomb, and the Heath where children come home with small wounds.
Galatz
Where the box is transferred to a river launch, and the chase becomes a matter of maps and timetables.

Critics

Franco Moretti

“The Dialectic of Fear”, in New Left Review (1982)

Moretti compared the Count to capital: a foreign monopolist who must keep growing, who hoards and does not spend, and whom the novel’s alliance of English gentlemen, an American and a Dutch professor unite to destroy.

Christopher Craft

““Kiss Me with Those Red Lips”: Gender and Inversion in Bram Stoker’s Dracula”, in Representations (1984)

Craft argued that the novel’s desire is displaced through women: the Count’s interest in Jonathan Harker is deflected on to Lucy and Mina, and the men’s blood transfusions and their staking of Lucy restore a threatened order of gender.

Stephen D. Arata

“The Occidental Tourist: Dracula and the Anxiety of Reverse Colonization”, in Victorian Studies (1990)

Arata read the novel as a fantasy of reverse colonisation: at the height of empire, a figure from the eastern edge of Europe invades London and threatens to turn the English into his own kind.
