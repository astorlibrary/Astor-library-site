White Nights · Fyodor Dostoevsky

What happens

First Night A girl at the canal railing

Petersburg has emptied for the summer and the narrator, who knows every face and house in it but no one to speak to, walks out beyond the city gates. Coming home late along the canal he sees a girl crying at the railing, rescues her with his knotted stick from a drunken gentleman, and walks her home. She agrees to meet him again the next night on one condition: he must not fall in love with her.

First Night
The old man on the Fontanka, the houses that talk to him, and the whole town leaving for its summer villas.
First Night
Tears at the railing, a staggering gentleman, the stick, and an appointment for ten o’clock the next night.

Second Night What a dreamer is

Nastenka asks for his history, and he says he has none. Instead, speaking of himself in the third person, he describes a dreamer: a creature who hides in a corner, lives on romances, finds real visitors unbearable and wakes from his reveries to find that his best years have gone. Nastenka, who has been listening wide-eyed, is moved, and offers her own story in return for his advice.

Second Night
The nooks of Petersburg, the dreamer like a snail in his shell, and a friend who calls once and never again.
Second Night
Hoffmann, Walter Scott’s heroines, Kazan and Danton; then the anniversaries he keeps for feelings that never happened.

Nastenka’s History The lodger upstairs

Nastenka is seventeen and lives with her blind grandmother, who has pinned their dresses together for two years to keep her out of mischief. A young lodger lent them Walter Scott and Pushkin and took them to The Barber of Seville. When he was leaving for Moscow she went up to his room with her clothes in a bundle, and he promised to come back for her in a year if he could. He has been back in Petersburg three days and has not come. The narrator proposes a letter, and she hands him one she has already written.

Nastenka’s History
The grandmother, the deaf charwoman who once took Nastenka’s place, and a new lodger who is pleasant looking.
Nastenka’s History
Novels, the opera, a year’s promise, and a letter ready and sealed like Rosina’s.

Third Night Waiting for the lodger

Written the next day, in rain. The narrator has delivered the letter, and on the third night Nastenka is happy, affectionate and full of plans; she likes him, she says, because he has not fallen in love with her. The lodger does not come. She wonders aloud why he cannot be more like the narrator, and why people cannot all be like brothers to one another, and she goes home promising to come back.

Third Night
A gloomy day, like the old age ahead of him, and the recollection of an evening spent waiting.
Third Night
A bell counted aloud, false comfort, and a comparison of the two men.

Fourth Night A proposal on the embankment

There is no letter, and Nastenka breaks down. The narrator tells her that he loves her; she says she will get over the lodger and accepts him, and they walk up and down planning to live together with her grandmother. Then a young man passes, stops and calls her name. She runs to him, runs back to kiss the narrator, and goes off with the lodger.

Fourth Night
Tears at the railing again, and the words it is impossible, but I love you.
Fourth Night
Plans for the room upstairs and another trip to the opera, cut short by a voice behind them.

Morning A letter and a clean ceiling

It is raining. Matrona brings a letter from Nastenka, who asks his forgiveness, calls him a brother and tells him she will be married next week. Matrona has swept away the cobwebs, and the room suddenly looks old; he sees himself fifteen years on, in the same room with the same servant. He blesses Nastenka, and asks whether a whole moment of happiness is too little for a lifetime.
