White Nights · Fyodor Dostoevsky

Form and language

Looking back

An account given after the event by a narrator who already knows how it ends.

From the first sentence the nights belong to youth, told by an older man to a dear reader. The Third Night and the Morning slip into the present tense, as if written on the day, so the reader waits for the lodger with the narrator without knowing that he will come.

It was a wonderful night, such a night as is only possible when we are young, dear reader.

First Night

Address to the reader

A speaker turning to address the reader, or someone within the story, directly.

Dear reader on the first page, and then Nastenka, dear Nastenka, over and over: much of the story is spoken to her, and the narrator admits he could never tire of saying her name. The last paragraph speaks to her directly, blessing her on her wedding day.

It was a wonderful night, such a night as is only possible when we are young, dear reader.

First Night

Personification

Giving human feelings, speech or action to things.

Petersburg’s houses greet the narrator, announce their repairs and cry out that they are being painted yellow. Nature in spring is a consumptive girl who is lovely for a moment. A man with no human friends makes friends of buildings, and the joke explains his loneliness.

when I looked at my friend I heard a plaintive, “They are painting me yellow!”

First Night

The dreamer’s monologue

A long passage built to stand out from the narrative around it.

Most of the second night is one speech, delivered as though read from a manuscript. It moves from comedy (the snail, the embarrassed visitor, the kitten under a chair) to fantasy and then to real grief about wasted years, and Nastenka’s response, a question about whether he has lived like that all his life, changes the story’s direction.

The dreamer—if you want an exact definition—is not a human being, but a creature of an intermediate sort.

The dreamer, Second Night

Third-person self-address

A speaker referring to himself as he or by a type-name instead of I.

Asked for his history, the narrator says he is a type and tells his life as our hero, because it is too shaming to say I. The distance lets him be exact about himself, and Nastenka sees through it immediately: this must have happened to you, word for word.

The dreamer—if you want an exact definition—is not a human being, but a creature of an intermediate sort.

The dreamer, Second Night

Allusion

A reference to another work, person or event that the reader is expected to recognise.

The dreamer’s fantasies name Hoffmann, Scott’s Diana Vernon, Clara Mowbray, Effie Deans and Minna and Brenda, Meyerbeer’s Robert the Devil, Pushkin’s Egyptian Nights and his little house in Kolomna. Rossini’s Barber of Seville runs through Nastenka’s story, and her ready-written letter is Rosina’s.

of friendship with Hoffmann, St. Bartholomew’s Night, of Diana Vernon, of playing the hero at the taking of Kazan by Ivan Vassilyevitch, of Clara Mowbray, of Effie Deans

The dreamer, Second Night

Letters

Letters written, carried or read within a story.

A letter is drafted by the narrator, Nastenka hands him one already sealed, he delivers it, and no reply comes. The story ends with her letter to him, which he reads over and over: loving, apologetic and announcing her wedding.

“R, o—Ro; s, i—si; n, a—na,” I began. “Rosina!” we both hummed together

Nastenka’s History

Weather

Weather used to set a mood or to mark a change in the story.

Starry, bright nights for the first meetings; a gloomy, rainy day like the old age before me for the third; a lovely blue sky and a passing yellow cloud just before the lodger appears; rain beating on the window on the last morning.

To-day was a gloomy, rainy day without a glimmer of sunlight, like the old age before me.

Third Night

Irony

Saying one thing in words whose real meaning is different, or letting events contradict what characters expect.

Nastenka likes him because he has not fallen in love with her; he argues at length that the lodger must be a man of delicate feeling; he points out a beautiful sky at the moment his happiness ends; Matrona cleans the ceiling for a wedding that is not his.

They, of course, do not know me, but I know them.
