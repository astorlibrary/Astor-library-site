White Nights · Fyodor Dostoevsky

Themes

Dreaming and real life

Most of the narrator’s life happens in his imagination, and he says so. On the second night he describes a dreamer who hides in his corner, builds whole romances out of books and wakes to find the room dark and his years gone, and he calls this life a crime and a sin. Nastenka gives him four nights of real life, and the story shows that real life hurts in ways daydreams do not. His last words ask whether one moment of happiness is enough for a lifetime, and the story leaves the question open.

A walk beyond the city gates; I make up regular romances in my dreams; the monologue of the second night; the anniversaries of feelings that never happened; the proposal; the cleaned ceiling.

Why, with no one, with an ideal, with the one I dream of in my sleep. I make up regular romances in my dreams.

The dreamer, First Night

The dreamer—if you want an exact definition—is not a human being, but a creature of an intermediate sort.

The dreamer, Second Night

Loneliness

Eight years in a great city and hardly an acquaintance: the narrator knows faces and houses, but no one knows him. Nastenka is lonely in another way, pinned to a blind old woman with nobody to advise her. Their four nights are a meeting of two lonely people, and she puts the problem into words when she asks why people cannot all be like brothers. At the end he pictures himself in the same room fifteen years later, as solitary as before.

The town leaving for its villas; the friend who visits once; the pinned dress; the bench by the canal; Matrona and the room grown old.

For though I had been living almost eight years in Petersburg I had hardly an acquaintance.

First Night

They, of course, do not know me, but I know them.

First Night

St Petersburg

The city is the narrator’s only real acquaintance. Its houses talk to him, its summer departures make him feel abandoned, and its canals and embankments are where the whole story happens. The white nights of the title are the weeks around midsummer when the northern sky does not go fully dark, and they give the meetings their unreal light. When the weather turns, so does the story: the third night is written on a rainy day and the morning is wet and grey.

The Nevsky, the Gardens and the Fontanka; villas on Kamenny Island and at Pargolovo; the canal railing; Nastenka’s side street; rain.

It was a wonderful night, such a night as is only possible when we are young, dear reader.

First Night

Love and friendship

Nastenka offers friendship on condition that there is no love, and the narrator agrees straight away; the rest of the story shows why the bargain cannot hold. The language of brothers and sisters runs all the way through: brotherly sympathy on the first night, brotherly advice on the second, more than brother and sister on the third, a friend and a brother in her final letter. It lets both of them name what they feel without naming it, until the fourth night, when he says it outright.

You mustn’t fall in love with me; the letter carried to a rival; I like you because you have not fallen in love with me; the confession; If only you were he.

But you mustn’t fall in love with me, I beg you!

Nastenka, First Night

Listen, Nastenka, I can’t do anything; I am a poor man, for I have nothing, not even a decent berth. How could we live, if I were to marry you?

The lodger, Nastenka’s History

The pinned dress

Nastenka’s grandmother pins the girl’s dress to her own so that she cannot go anywhere, and for two years the two sit together knitting, sewing and reading aloud. It is comic and it is a prison, and it explains why a girl of seventeen would climb the stairs to a lodger with her clothes in a bundle. The narrator is confined too, by habit and shyness to his corner, and the two confinements explain why they understand each other so quickly.

The pin; the deaf charwoman sitting in her place; Walter Scott through the binding; the bundle on the lodger’s bed; the plan for the empty room upstairs.

she took a pin and pinned my dress to hers, and said that we should sit like that for the rest of our lives if, of course, I did not become a better girl.

Nastenka, Nastenka’s History

But look under the binding; they sometimes stuff it under the bindings, the rascals!

The grandmother, Nastenka’s History

Reading and romance

Everyone in the story has learned about love from books. The narrator nearly says Madam! and stops because the word has been said a thousand times in society novels; his fantasies come from Scott, Hoffmann and Pushkin; Nastenka reads her way to the lodger through Ivanhoe; the grandmother searches novels for love letters; and the letter scene repeats Rosina’s in The Barber of Seville. Nastenka’s teasing, that he talks as if reading from a book, shows that she can see the difference between literature and life before he can.

A society-novel word held back; the catalogue of romantic fantasies; Walter Scott and Pushkin sent downstairs; Ro-si-na hummed over a sealed letter.

should certainly have pronounced the word “Madam!” if I had not known that that exclamation has been uttered a thousand times in every Russian society novel.

The dreamer, First Night

Anniversaries

Someone looking back on nights possible only when we are young tells this story, and the narrator is already a man who lives in remembrance. He keeps anniversaries of his own sensations, visits places where he was once happy in his own way, and expects to remember these four nights all his life. Nastenka promises the same in her letter. The ending makes memory a blessing: he will not reproach her, and one moment of happiness is what he keeps.

Such a night as is only possible when we are young; the anniversary of that which never existed; where have you buried your best days?; the memory of you in Nastenka’s letter; a whole moment of happiness.

It was a wonderful night, such a night as is only possible when we are young, dear reader.

First Night

Do you know that I am forced now to celebrate the anniversary of my own sensations, the anniversary of that which was once so sweet, which never existed in reality
