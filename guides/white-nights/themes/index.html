Dreaming and real life

Most of the narrator’s life happens in his imagination, and he says so. On the second night he describes a dreamer who hides in his corner, builds whole romances out of books and wakes to find the room dark and his years gone, and he calls this life a crime and a sin. Nastenka gives him four nights of real life, and the story shows that real life hurts in ways daydreams do not. His last words ask whether one moment of happiness is enough for a lifetime, and the story leaves the question open.

Through the book A walk beyond the city gates; I make up regular romances in my dreams; the monologue of the second night; the anniversaries of feelings that never happened; the proposal; the cleaned ceiling.

In the text

Why, with no one, with an ideal, with the one I dream of in my sleep. I make up regular romances in my dreams.

The dreamer, First Night

He answers with perfect candour, and the candour is his charm. Nastenka laughs, but kindly, and tells him he is his own enemy. The phrase regular romances is the key to his life: he writes novels in his head in which he is the hero, and has never had to test one against a real woman until tonight.

The dreamer—if you want an exact definition—is not a human being, but a creature of an intermediate sort.

The dreamer, Second Night

Dostoevsky had used almost the same words in a newspaper feuilleton of 1847, the Petersburg Chronicle, where the dreamer becomes a strange creature of the neuter gender. Here the definition is given with mock pedantry by a dreamer about himself. The comparisons that follow, a snail and a tortoise, are comic, and they also show a man who has made himself a shell with no door.

Loneliness

Eight years in a great city and hardly an acquaintance: the narrator knows faces and houses, but no one knows him. Nastenka is lonely in another way, pinned to a blind old woman with nobody to advise her. Their four nights are a meeting of two lonely people, and she puts the problem into words when she asks why people cannot all be like brothers. At the end he pictures himself in the same room fifteen years later, as solitary as before.

Through the book The town leaving for its villas; the friend who visits once; the pinned dress; the bench by the canal; Matrona and the room grown old.

In the text

For though I had been living almost eight years in Petersburg I had hardly an acquaintance.

First Night

His loneliness is stated as a fact and left there. The next sentence, what did I want with acquaintances?, tries to laugh it off, and then he explains that he knows all Petersburg anyway. The pattern of the whole story is here: a painful truth, a joke to cover it, and a fantasy to replace it.

I have lived, as they say, keeping myself to myself, that is, utterly alone—alone, entirely alone.

The dreamer, Second Night

He repeats the word as if hearing it for the first time. A man who has no history in the ordinary sense, no events and no people, has to explain himself some other way, and this admission leads straight into his great speech about the dreamer. Nastenka’s answer, that she too is shut in with a blind grandmother, begins their likeness.

St Petersburg

The city is the narrator’s only real acquaintance. Its houses talk to him, its summer departures make him feel abandoned, and its canals and embankments are where the whole story happens. The white nights of the title are the weeks around midsummer when the northern sky does not go fully dark, and they give the meetings their unreal light. When the weather turns, so does the story: the third night is written on a rainy day and the morning is wet and grey.

Through the book The Nevsky, the Gardens and the Fontanka; villas on Kamenny Island and at Pargolovo; the canal railing; Nastenka’s side street; rain.

In the text

when I looked at my friend I heard a plaintive, “They are painting me yellow!”

First Night

A pretty pink house cries out as it is repainted, and he cannot bear to visit his disfigured friend. The fantasy is funny and tender, and it shows how completely buildings have taken the place of people for him. Yellow, the colour he calls that of the Celestial Empire, returns in Nastenka’s yellow hat and the yellow cloud of the fourth night.

To-day was a gloomy, rainy day without a glimmer of sunlight, like the old age before me.

Third Night

Here the tense changes, and so does the weather. The story is now being written on the day, in the rain, by a man who has begun to guess the truth. At twenty-six he speaks of old age as if it had arrived, and the comparison shows how quickly hope has gone out of him since the evening before.

Love and friendship

Nastenka offers friendship on condition that there is no love, and the narrator agrees straight away; the rest of the story shows why the bargain cannot hold. The language of brothers and sisters runs all the way through: brotherly sympathy on the first night, brotherly advice on the second, more than brother and sister on the third, a friend and a brother in her final letter. It lets both of them name what they feel without naming it, until the fourth night, when he says it outright.

Through the book You mustn’t fall in love with me; the letter carried to a rival; I like you because you have not fallen in love with me; the confession; If only you were he.

In the text

But you mustn’t fall in love with me, I beg you!

Nastenka, First Night

Nastenka makes the rule, and the reader knows straight away that it will be broken. She is not being coy: she has her own secret, a man she is waiting for, and she needs a friend who wants nothing from her. The condition gives the story its tension, since every tender moment afterwards has to be passed off as friendship.

It’s impossible, but I love you, Nastenka! There it is! Now everything is told,

The dreamer, Fourth Night

After three nights of eloquence the confession is short and clumsy, accompanied by a wave of the hand. Nastenka’s answer is that she knew long ago. For a few pages the impossible becomes possible: she says she will get over the lodger, and they make plans for the empty room upstairs and another night at the opera.

The pinned dress

Nastenka’s grandmother pins the girl’s dress to her own so that she cannot go anywhere, and for two years the two sit together knitting, sewing and reading aloud. It is comic and it is a prison, and it explains why a girl of seventeen would climb the stairs to a lodger with her clothes in a bundle. The narrator is confined too, by habit and shyness to his corner, and the two confinements explain why they understand each other so quickly.

Through the book The pin; the deaf charwoman sitting in her place; Walter Scott through the binding; the bundle on the lodger’s bed; the plan for the empty room upstairs.

In the text

she took a pin and pinned my dress to hers, and said that we should sit like that for the rest of our lives if, of course, I did not become a better girl.

Nastenka, Nastenka’s History

Nothing in the book is funnier or sadder than the pin. A blind woman who cannot watch the girl fastens her to herself, and for two years they sit knitting and reading aloud. Nastenka tells it lightly and scolds the narrator for laughing at her grandmother. The picture of a young life held still explains everything she does next.

But look under the binding; they sometimes stuff it under the bindings, the rascals!

The grandmother, Nastenka’s History

She fears the right thing in the wrong place. There is no love letter in the binding; the books themselves are the courtship, and within a month Nastenka is reading Ivanhoe and Pushkin and has stopped dreaming of Chinese princes. The grandmother, who admits to reading such novels all night herself, is one of the story’s best comic turns.

Reading and romance

Everyone in the story has learned about love from books. The narrator nearly says Madam! and stops because the word has been said a thousand times in society novels; his fantasies come from Scott, Hoffmann and Pushkin; Nastenka reads her way to the lodger through Ivanhoe; the grandmother searches novels for love letters; and the letter scene repeats Rosina’s in The Barber of Seville. Nastenka’s teasing, that he talks as if reading from a book, shows that she can see the difference between literature and life before he can.

Through the book A society-novel word held back; the catalogue of romantic fantasies; Walter Scott and Pushkin sent downstairs; Ro-si-na hummed over a sealed letter.

In the text

should certainly have pronounced the word “Madam!” if I had not known that that exclamation has been uttered a thousand times in every Russian society novel.

First Night

Even at the moment of action he thinks in literature, and he is stopped by the fear of a cliché. It is a good joke against himself and against the novels of the day. It also costs him the chance to speak, and only the arrival of a drunken gentleman, a plot device of the same society novels, brings them together.

of friendship with Hoffmann, St. Bartholomew’s Night, of Diana Vernon, of playing the hero at the taking of Kazan by Ivan Vassilyevitch, of Clara Mowbray, of Effie Deans

The dreamer, Second Night

Dostoevsky added this list when he revised the story for his collected works of 1860. It is a young man’s reading of the 1830s and 1840s: the German fantasist Hoffmann, three heroines of Walter Scott, a massacre from French history and a Russian victory of 1552. The jumble is revealing. Nothing connects these dreams except that he is the hero of every one.

Anniversaries

Someone looking back on nights possible only when we are young tells this story, and the narrator is already a man who lives in remembrance. He keeps anniversaries of his own sensations, visits places where he was once happy in his own way, and expects to remember these four nights all his life. Nastenka promises the same in her letter. The ending makes memory a blessing: he will not reproach her, and one moment of happiness is what he keeps.

Through the book Such a night as is only possible when we are young; the anniversary of that which never existed; where have you buried your best days?; the memory of you in Nastenka’s letter; a whole moment of happiness.

In the text

It was a wonderful night, such a night as is only possible when we are young, dear reader.

First Night

The opening words settle two things: the teller is looking back, and he is talking to us. When we are young places the four nights in a lost past, so a reader knows before anything happens that this is a memory, perhaps a happy one and certainly an ended one. The tone is warm, confiding and a little self-mocking, like the rest of the story.

Do you know that I am forced now to celebrate the anniversary of my own sensations, the anniversary of that which was once so sweet, which never existed in reality

The dreamer, Second Night

He keeps anniversaries of feelings, and some of them are feelings for things that never happened. It is the saddest idea in the story, and the most exact description of what a life of daydreams leaves behind. The speech has turned from mockery to confession, and Nastenka, who was laughing at the beginning, is now wiping away a tear.
