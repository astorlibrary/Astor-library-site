Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland · Lewis Carroll

What happens

Chapters I–II Down the rabbit-hole, and the pool of tears

Boredom starts the book: Alice follows the Rabbit because an afternoon with nothing in it has become unbearable. Underground she finds a door she cannot fit, a bottle and a cake that change her size, and a pool made of her own crying. The problem is never the garden; it is that she cannot keep one body long enough to reach it.

Chapter I
The Rabbit’s waistcoat-pocket watch is the one detail Alice cannot explain away, and she is down the hole before she has thought about getting out.
Chapter II
Nine feet high and then two feet high, Alice tries lessons and recitation as proof of identity and finds both have gone wrong.

Chapters III–IV The Caucus-race, and Alice stuck in the Rabbit’s house

The soaked company invents a procedure for drying off in which everyone runs, everyone stops when told, and everyone wins. Then the Rabbit mistakes Alice for a servant, sends her for gloves, and she grows until she fills his house and has to be attacked from outside. Authority in Wonderland begins as a clerical error.

Chapter III
The Mouse’s dry history fails to dry anybody; the Dodo’s race distributes prizes without a result.
Chapter IV
Alice is taken for Mary Ann, grows to fill a house, and Bill the Lizard is sent down the chimney.

Chapters V–VI The Caterpillar and the Duchess’s kitchen

The Caterpillar asks the question the first four chapters have made unanswerable, and gives Alice a mushroom that puts size under her own control at last. In the Duchess’s kitchen violence is ordinary household business, and the baby Alice rescues turns into a pig. The Cheshire Cat then explains, reasonably, that everybody present is mad.

Chapter V
Alice recites ‘You are old, Father William’ wrongly and is told so; the mushroom becomes a tool she can use.
Chapter VI
Pepper, a flying frying-pan and a baby that becomes a pig; the Cat grins itself away and gives directions that depend on Alice not minding where she goes.

Chapter VII The Mad Tea-Party

Time has stopped for the Hatter, so the party moves round the table instead of moving on. A riddle is asked and never answered, Alice is told she means the opposite of what she says, and the Dormouse tells a story about three sisters living in a treacle-well. It is the chapter in which conversation itself stops working.

Chapter VII
No room at a table with room to spare; the raven and the writing-desk; the Hatter’s quarrel with Time; Alice walks out.

Chapters VIII–X The croquet ground, the Gryphon and the Mock Turtle

The garden is finally reached and turns out to be a croquet ground where the equipment is alive and the head gardeners are painting roses to cover a mistake. The Queen orders executions at the rate of about one a minute and nobody is ever executed. The Gryphon and the Mock Turtle then supply the book’s one sustained parody of school and sentiment.

Chapter VIII
Flamingo mallets and hedgehog balls; the Cat’s disembodied head raises the question of whether a head with no body can be beheaded.
Chapter IX
The Duchess finds a moral in everything; the Mock Turtle grieves for a schooling built entirely of puns.
Chapter X
A dance, a recitation that goes wrong again, and Alice’s admission that she cannot go back to yesterday.

Chapters XI–XII The trial of the Knave, and waking up

Everything a court needs is present, from judge and jury to witnesses, evidence and verdict, and every one of them is used in the wrong order. Alice grows through the proceedings as she loses patience with them, until she can call the court what it is made of. The waking on the bank does not cancel the dream; it returns the reader to a child who has learned to hear adult language differently.

Chapter XI
The Hatter gives evidence in terror, the jury writes down its own names in case it forgets them, and Alice begins to grow.
Chapter XII
A nonsense poem is read as proof, sentence is demanded before verdict, and Alice will have none of it.
