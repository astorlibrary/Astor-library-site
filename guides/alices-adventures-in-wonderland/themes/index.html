Identity and size

Alice’s height changes eleven times, and each change raises the question of who she is, as well as how tall. Carroll keeps the enquiry in a child’s terms (other girls she knows, lessons she can or cannot recite), so that the philosophical problem arrives without any philosophical vocabulary.

Through the book Panic in Chapter II, cross-examination in Chapter V, deliberate use of the mushroom afterwards, and finally a growth in Chapter XII that is an act of judgement.

In the text

“Curiouser and curiouser!” cried Alice (she was so much surprised, that for the moment she quite forgot how to speak good English)

Chapter II

Alice knows the comparative is wrong and says so in the same breath, so the nonsense sits one step past the usual limit of language, still within reach of it. The doubling also gives the sentence an upward motion that matches what her body is doing, which is why no other word would serve.

“Who are you?” said the Caterpillar.

Chapter V

Four words, and the book has spent four chapters making them unanswerable. Coming from a creature about to dissolve into a chrysalis and re-form as something else, the question is also a professional one, which is why Alice’s complaint about changing size gets so little sympathy.

Rules and authority

Every creature Alice meets treats a private rule as universal and expects her to know it already. The book is unusually precise about how authority sounds: the Dodo’s procedure, the Queen’s sentences, the Hatter’s corrections and the King’s courtroom all use the grammar of settled law over nothing at all.

Through the book A race with no contest, then a game with living equipment, then a trial that puts the sentence before the verdict: the forms grow more official as they grow emptier.

In the text

At last the Dodo said, “everybody has won, and all must have prizes.”

Chapter III

A result is announced for a contest that had no start, no course and no finish. The sentence is perfectly formed and entirely empty, and it is the first of several occasions on which Wonderland uses correct official phrasing to settle something that was never in dispute.

“Oh, you can’t help that,” said the Cat: “we’re all mad here. I’m mad. You’re mad.”

Chapter VI

The Cat includes itself and Alice in the same sentence, which converts an insult into a description of the premises. It then offers a proof by comparison with a dog, and the proof is formally respectable: the only thing wrong with it is that the premises about dogs are invented.

Language taken literally

Wonderland’s creatures are excellent close readers and terrible listeners. They hear the word and not the sentence, so ‘I mean what I say’ and ‘I say what I mean’ become different propositions, a tale becomes a tail, and a lesson is what lessens from day to day.

Through the book The Mouse’s tale sets the pattern; the tea-party makes it a method; at the trial it becomes a way of convicting somebody.

In the text

“Do cats eat bats? Do cats eat bats?” and sometimes, “Do bats eat cats?”

Chapter I

The question reverses because Alice is falling asleep, and the reversal is the first sign that Wonderland runs on grammar. She herself supplies the reason it does not matter which way round it is put, since she cannot answer either, and the Hatter will later use exactly that logic against her.

“Speak English!” said the Eaglet. “I don’t know the meaning of half those long words, and, what’s more, I don’t believe you do either!”

Chapter III

The Eaglet does what nobody else in the book will do: it demands that an official voice be translated. Carroll gives the demand to the smallest bird present and then has it bend its head to hide a smile, so the challenge to pompous language is itself a piece of social manoeuvring.

Manners and violence

Politeness in Wonderland is the wrapping cruelty comes in. A baby is shaken in time to a lullaby, a cook throws fire-irons during a conversation, and executions are ordered between croquet strokes, always in the correct register.

Through the book Domestic in the Duchess’s kitchen, ceremonial on the croquet ground, and judicial by the trial, with the violence growing more formal as it grows less real.

In the text

“No room! No room!” they cried out when they saw Alice coming. “There’s plenty of room!” said Alice indignantly

Chapter VII

The lie is contradicted by the furniture in the same sentence, and Alice answers it with a fact. Carroll establishes in two lines that this table runs on assertion, which is why the riddle that follows is allowed to have no answer.

the Queen was in a furious passion, and went stamping about, and shouting “Off with his head!” or “Off with her head!” about once in a minute.

Chapter VIII

Carroll gives the rate: about once a minute. Making a death sentence into a statistic drains the menace out of it, and the reader learns before Alice does that these orders are noise; the King has been pardoning everybody behind the Queen’s back.

Lessons and memory

Alice keeps reaching for the schoolroom to prove she is still herself, and the schoolroom keeps letting her down: multiplication that will not reach twenty, geography with the capitals in the wrong countries, poems that come out as other poems. Learning by heart turns out to be exactly that: learning that is not knowledge.

Through the book Recitation fails in Chapter II, fails again under the Caterpillar in Chapter V, and is mourned in the Mock Turtle’s account of his education.

In the text

But if I’m not the same, the next question is, Who in the world am I? Ah, that’s the great puzzle!

Chapter II

The reasoning is a syllogism: if I am not the same, then I am someone else, so which one? Alice tests it against the only evidence a child has (Ada’s ringlets, Mabel’s ignorance, the multiplication table), and the test fails on all three, which is why the chapter ends with her in a pool of her own tears.

“We called him Tortoise because he taught us,” said the Mock Turtle angrily: “really you are very dull!”

Chapter IX

The pun is delivered angrily, as though Alice were the slow one, and the scene depends on that: in Wonderland the person who has to have the joke explained is always at fault. The Mock Turtle’s whole education runs on the same principle, which is why he mourns it.

The dream and the waking

The frame is not a get-out. Carroll lets the dream be dangerous while it lasts and then gives it to Alice’s sister, who dreams it again knowingly, so the book ends by describing a rereading, and does not dismiss what was read.

Through the book The fall, the afternoon underground, and a waking in which the cards become leaves and the Queen’s shrieks become a shepherd boy’s voice.

In the text

“and what is the use of a book,” thought Alice “without pictures or conversations?”

Chapter I

Carroll puts a judgement about books at the front of a book, and the judgement is a child’s test of quality: pictures and conversations. What follows is made almost entirely of both, so the opening is a specification that the rest of the text meets exactly.

“I could tell you my adventures⁠—beginning from this morning,” said Alice a little timidly: “but it’s no use going back to yesterday, because I was a different person then.”

Chapter X

This is the same question the Caterpillar asked, answered at last. Alice does not work out who she is; she accepts that yesterday’s Alice is no longer available for comparison. Her timidity over saying it is the measure of how large the admission is.
