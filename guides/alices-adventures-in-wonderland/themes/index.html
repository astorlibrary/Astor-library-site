Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland · Lewis Carroll

Themes

Identity and size

Alice’s height changes eleven times, and each change raises the question of who she is, as well as how tall. Carroll keeps the enquiry in a child’s terms (other girls she knows, lessons she can or cannot recite), so that the philosophical problem arrives without any philosophical vocabulary.

Panic in Chapter II, cross-examination in Chapter V, deliberate use of the mushroom afterwards, and finally a growth in Chapter XII that is an act of judgement.

“Curiouser and curiouser!” cried Alice (she was so much surprised, that for the moment she quite forgot how to speak good English)

Rules and authority

Every creature Alice meets treats a private rule as universal and expects her to know it already. The book is unusually precise about how authority sounds: the Dodo’s procedure, the Queen’s sentences, the Hatter’s corrections and the King’s courtroom all use the grammar of settled law over nothing at all.

A race with no contest, then a game with living equipment, then a trial that puts the sentence before the verdict: the forms grow more official as they grow emptier.

“Speak English!” said the Eaglet. “I don’t know the meaning of half those long words, and, what’s more, I don’t believe you do either!”

Language taken literally

Wonderland’s creatures are excellent close readers and terrible listeners. They hear the word and not the sentence, so ‘I mean what I say’ and ‘I say what I mean’ become different propositions, a tale becomes a tail, and a lesson is what lessens from day to day.

The Mouse’s tale sets the pattern; the tea-party makes it a method; at the trial it becomes a way of convicting somebody.

“Do cats eat bats? Do cats eat bats?” and sometimes, “Do bats eat cats?”

Manners and violence

Politeness in Wonderland is the wrapping cruelty comes in. A baby is shaken in time to a lullaby, a cook throws fire-irons during a conversation, and executions are ordered between croquet strokes, always in the correct register.

Domestic in the Duchess’s kitchen, ceremonial on the croquet ground, and judicial by the trial, with the violence growing more formal as it grows less real.

“No room! No room!” they cried out when they saw Alice coming. “There’s plenty of room!” said Alice indignantly

Chapter VII

The Hatter opened his eyes very wide on hearing this; but all he said was, “Why is a raven like a writing-desk?”

Chapter VII

Lessons and memory

Alice keeps reaching for the schoolroom to prove she is still herself, and the schoolroom keeps letting her down: multiplication that will not reach twenty, geography with the capitals in the wrong countries, poems that come out as other poems. Learning by heart turns out to be exactly that: learning that is not knowledge.

Recitation fails in Chapter II, fails again under the Caterpillar in Chapter V, and is mourned in the Mock Turtle’s account of his education.

“and what is the use of a book,” thought Alice “without pictures or conversations?”

The dream and the waking

The frame is not a get-out. Carroll lets the dream be dangerous while it lasts and then gives it to Alice’s sister, who dreams it again knowingly, so the book ends by describing a rereading, and does not dismiss what was read.

The fall, the afternoon underground, and a waking in which the cards become leaves and the Queen’s shrieks become a shepherd boy’s voice.
