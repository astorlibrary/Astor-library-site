Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland · Lewis Carroll

Characters

Alice

A child of about seven, underground for an afternoon; first appears Chapter I

She is the only person in the book who revises her opinions. Alice tries school knowledge, polite introductions and recitation, notices each time that the method has failed, and keeps a running commentary on her own conduct that is often sharper than anything the creatures say to her.

The White Rabbit

A courtier in a hurry; first appears Chapter I

He is the only creature in Wonderland who is frightened of being late, and the fear turns out to be well founded once the Queen appears. Anxious upwards and imperious downwards, he sets the plot going and then spends it running errands for people more dangerous than himself.

The Mouse

The first creature Alice tries to talk to; first appears Chapter II

He is offended twice over by Alice’s cat and her neighbour’s terrier before he can begin his history, which is then delivered as the driest paragraph of Norman constitutional record Carroll could find. His long tale is the book’s first joke about a word doing two jobs.

The Caterpillar

Three inches high, on a mushroom, with a hookah; first appears Chapter V

He conducts the only real interrogation in the book, and he is entitled to it: a creature that will shortly liquefy into a butterfly has a professional interest in whether anybody stays themselves. His contempt is useful to Alice, because it forces her to say out loud that she cannot explain herself.

The Cheshire Cat

A cat that can arrive and leave in pieces; first appears Chapter VI

The only creature who is candid with Alice, and the only one who can leave a conversation gradually. His directions are logically impeccable and practically useless, which makes him the book’s clearest demonstration that valid reasoning and helpful reasoning are different things.

The Duchess

A peeress with a kitchen full of pepper; first appears Chapter VI

In her own house she shakes a baby, sings about beating it, and is under sentence of execution; released, she becomes affectionate and finds a moral in everything Alice says. The two performances belong to the same person, and her manners have no connection to her conduct.

The Hatter

Host of a tea-party that never moves on; first appears Chapter VII

He quarrelled with Time while singing at a concert and has been stuck at six o’clock ever since, which gives his rudeness a grievance behind it. He asks a riddle he cannot answer, corrects Alice’s grammar while ignoring her meaning, and later gives evidence in court shaking too hard to stand.

The Queen of Hearts

Sovereign of the croquet ground and the court; first appears Chapter VIII

She has one procedure for every difficulty, and it is beheading. Nothing she orders is ever carried out, because the King secretly pardons everybody, so her power is pure noise, frightening until somebody notices.

The Mock Turtle

A grieving former pupil of the sea; first appears Chapter IX

He exists because mock turtle soup was made of veal, so Carroll gives him a calf’s head and a permanent sob. His account of an education in Reeling, Writhing and the different branches of Arithmetic is the book’s most sustained joke about school, and it is delivered as real grief.
