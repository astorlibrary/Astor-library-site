The language really used by men

The Advertisement puts the whole book forward as an experiment in whether ordinary conversation can carry poetry. In practice that means short words, flat statements, a good deal of repetition, and reviewers who complained that they had been sent prose.

Through the book The 1798 poems try it on tramps, farmers and children; by 1800 Wordsworth had written a preface to defend what he had done, and by 1817 Coleridge had written chapters of Biographia Literaria disputing the theory.

In the text

The majority of the following poems are to be considered as experiments.

Advertisement

A word borrowed from the laboratory, at a moment when poetry was still expected to be an art of finish. It asks the reader to judge by results more than by decorum, and it is the first sign that the book means to make an argument as well as a collection.

I’ve measured it from side to side: ’Tis three feet long, and two feet wide.

The Thorn

The most mocked couplet of the age, and entirely deliberate: a man who cannot tell a story reaches for the only certainty he has, a tape measure. Wordsworth cut the lines in 1820 under pressure, which is the clearest evidence of how far the experiment had gone.

Nature as teacher

Nature in this volume teaches. The poems set it directly against books, sermons and analysis, and credit it with the moral education the schoolroom claims.

Through the book Stated as a joke against study in The Tables Turned, complicated in Lines written in early spring, and turned into something far stranger in Tintern Abbey, where the teacher is a presence the poet cannot name.

In the text

One impulse from a vernal wood May teach you more of man, Of moral evil and of good, Than all the sages can.

The Tables Turned, 21–24

The claim is deliberately excessive (more than all the sages), and that excess has been quoted against Wordsworth ever since. Read in the argument it belongs to, it is one move in a friendly debate.

a sense sublime Of something far more deeply interfused

Tintern Abbey, 98–99

Everything here is held at arm’s length (‘a sense’, ‘something’, ‘far more deeply’), because naming it would commit him to a doctrine. The vagueness is exact: this is a man describing an experience he trusts and cannot define.

Memory

The book’s last poem makes remembering an activity: a man measures his present feeling against one five years old and finds the loss and the compensation in the same landscape.

Through the book Memory is a burden in the Marinere, a comfort in Tintern Abbey, and in both cases something that has to be told to another person before it can be borne.

In the text

Five years have passed; five summers, with the length Of five long winters!

Tintern Abbey, 1–2

The same span counted three ways, each slower than the last, so that the line performs the passage it reports. After twenty-two ballads the ear notices immediately that the rhyme has gone and the sentences no longer stop every fourth line.

sensations sweet, Felt in the blood, and felt along the heart

Tintern Abbey, 29–30

The claim is physiological more than sentimental: a memory of a place is described as something circulating in the body. It is the volume’s clearest statement that feeling is a kind of evidence.

Vagrants and the poor

A woman turned off her father’s land and widowed by war, a shepherd selling his last sheep, an old huntsman too weak to cut a root, an old woman gathering sticks from a hedge. Poetry had used such figures for pathos or comedy; this book gives them the grammar of the poem and lets them speak first.

Through the book The 1798 poems mostly report the poor without moralising. War, the loss of land and the parish system stand behind many of them and are named only in passing, as when the shepherd asks the parish for relief and is told to sell his sheep.

In the text

Oh! what’s the matter? what’s the matter?

Goody Blake and Harry Gill

A poem that begins with a question a neighbour would ask over a wall. Wordsworth took the incident from a medical book and put it in ballad metre, and the effect is a case study told in the voice of village gossip.

The still, sad music of humanity

Tintern Abbey, 94

The compensation is other people’s suffering, and it is heard as music. The phrase justifies the rest of the book without fuss: the vagrants, huntsmen and mothers of the earlier poems are the music he means.

Books and nature

Two poems stage a straight argument about how a person learns, and the book’s most quoted line accuses science of killing what it examines. The position is more careful than the slogan: the poems attack dissection and praise attention.

Through the book Expostulation and Reply raises it, The Tables Turned pushes it to an extreme, and Tintern Abbey then describes a kind of seeing that is neither idleness nor analysis.

In the text

Nay, we are seven!

We are seven, 69

The poem ends with the child unmoved and the adult out of arguments, and Wordsworth gives her the last word; there is no moral. What looks like a poem about a child’s confusion is a poem about an adult’s certainty failing in the open.

Why William, on that old grey stone

Expostulation and Reply, 1

The objection is given first and given a name. By letting the sensible view speak in its own voice, the poem earns the reply it is about to make, and the pair becomes a debate.

Isolation

A child who will not revise her count, a woman who sits on a mountain saying the same two words, a sailor nobody can help, a man cursed with a cold that no fire touches. The volume keeps arriving at someone who cannot be argued out of their condition.

Through the book Early poems treat the isolation as a puzzle for the observer; by Tintern Abbey the poet has accepted that the only remedy on offer is another person’s presence.

In the text

It is an ancyent Marinere, And he stoppeth one of three.

The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere, 1–2

Archaic spelling, ballad metre and a man seizing a stranger, all in eleven words. Coleridge is announcing an old form on purpose; the volume that claims to use ordinary speech opens in a language nobody had spoken for two hundred years.

With my cross bow I shot the Albatross.

The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere

The crime is reported in a single line with no motive attached, and the poem never supplies one. Everything that follows is out of proportion to it: the Marinere’s world runs on a scale of consequence that no explanation will fit.
