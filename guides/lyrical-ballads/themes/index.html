Lyrical Ballads · William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge

Themes

The language really used by men

The Advertisement puts the whole book forward as an experiment in whether ordinary conversation can carry poetry. In practice that means short words, flat statements, a good deal of repetition, and reviewers who complained that they had been sent prose.

The 1798 poems try it on tramps, farmers and children; by 1800 Wordsworth had written a preface to defend what he had done, and by 1817 Coleridge had written chapters of Biographia Literaria disputing the theory.

The majority of the following poems are to be considered as experiments.

Advertisement

Oh! what’s the matter? what’s the matter?

Goody Blake and Harry Gill

Nature as teacher

Nature in this volume teaches. The poems set it directly against books, sermons and analysis, and credit it with the moral education the schoolroom claims.

Stated as a joke against study in The Tables Turned, complicated in Lines written in early spring, and turned into something far stranger in Tintern Abbey, where the teacher is a presence the poet cannot name.

Think you, mid all this mighty sum Of things for ever speaking, That nothing of itself will come, But we must still be seeking?

Expostulation and Reply

One impulse from a vernal wood May teach you more of man, Of moral evil and of good, Than all the sages can.

The Tables Turned, 21–24

Memory

The book’s last poem makes remembering an activity: a man measures his present feeling against one five years old and finds the loss and the compensation in the same landscape.

Memory is a burden in the Marinere, a comfort in Tintern Abbey, and in both cases something that has to be told to another person before it can be borne.

Five years have passed; five summers, with the length Of five long winters!

Tintern Abbey, 1–2

sensations sweet, Felt in the blood, and felt along the heart

Tintern Abbey, 29–30

Vagrants and the poor

A woman turned off her father’s land and widowed by war, a shepherd selling his last sheep, an old huntsman too weak to cut a root, an old woman gathering sticks from a hedge. Poetry had used such figures for pathos or comedy; this book gives them the grammar of the poem and lets them speak first.

The 1798 poems mostly report the poor without moralising. War, the loss of land and the parish system stand behind many of them and are named only in passing, as when the shepherd asks the parish for relief and is told to sell his sheep.

Oh! what’s the matter? what’s the matter?

Goody Blake and Harry Gill

The still, sad music of humanity

Tintern Abbey, 94

Books and nature

Two poems stage a straight argument about how a person learns, and the book’s most quoted line accuses science of killing what it examines. The position is more careful than the slogan: the poems attack dissection and praise attention.

Expostulation and Reply raises it, The Tables Turned pushes it to an extreme, and Tintern Abbey then describes a kind of seeing that is neither idleness nor analysis.

The cocks did crow to-whoo, to-whoo, And the sun did shine so cold.

The Idiot Boy

Nay, we are seven!

We are seven, 69

Isolation

A child who will not revise her count, a woman who sits on a mountain saying the same two words, a sailor nobody can help, a man cursed with a cold that no fire touches. The volume keeps arriving at someone who cannot be argued out of their condition.

Early poems treat the isolation as a puzzle for the observer; by Tintern Abbey the poet has accepted that the only remedy on offer is another person’s presence.

It is an ancyent Marinere, And he stoppeth one of three.

The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere, 1–2

With my cross bow I shot the Albatross.

The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere
