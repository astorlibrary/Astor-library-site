Lyrical Ballads · William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge

Characters

The Ancyent Marinere

The sailor who stops the wedding guest; first appears The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere

He shot a bird for no reason he can name and has been explaining it ever since. The poem gives him a punishment with no proportion to the crime and a moral at the end that does not cover what happened, which is why readers have argued about him for two hundred years.

The Wedding Guest

The listener, kept from the feast; first appears The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere

He is late, he is unwilling, and he is changed by the end. His resistance is the poem’s measure of the tale’s force, and his final state, “a sadder and a wiser man”, is the closest thing the volume has to a statement about what listening to other people costs.

Martha Ray

The woman by the thorn; first appears The Thorn

She sits on the mountain in a scarlet cloak crying oh misery, and the poem never establishes what happened to her child. Wordsworth gives the story to a narrator who cannot tell it, so the reader is left doing what the village does: assembling a woman’s tragedy out of gossip and a measured pond.

The narrator of The Thorn

A retired sea captain with time on his hands; first appears The Thorn

Wordsworth’s note describes him as a man of slow faculties and deep feelings who has settled in a village where he knows nobody. He measures the pond, repeats himself, and passes on what he has heard, and the poem turns out to be about his way of telling, more than the events.

The little maid

The eight-year-old of We are seven; first appears We are seven

She lives in a churchyard cottage with two of her siblings buried twelve steps from the door, and counts them in. The adult questioner repeats the arithmetic three times and gets nowhere, and the poem ends with his failure; she is never corrected.

The questioner

The adult voice in the child poems; first appears We are seven

He appears in We are seven and in Anecdote for Fathers, and in both he is the one who comes off worse. Wordsworth uses him to put the reader’s own reasonableness in the poem and let it be defeated by a child who is not arguing at all.

Betty Foy

The mother of the idiot boy; first appears The Idiot Boy

She sends her son out at night for a doctor and then spends the poem in an agony of self-reproach looking for him. Wordsworth defended the poem against charges of condescension, and the writing bears him out: her panic is given the attention a heroine’s would get.

Johnny Foy

The boy sent for the doctor; first appears The Idiot Boy

He never reaches the doctor and comes home with a report of the night that is pure sensation: cocks crowing, a cold sun, the moon. His account is the only piece of poetry produced inside the poem, which is the joke and the argument together.

The sister above the Wye

The listener of Tintern Abbey, addressed in the last fifty lines; first appears Tintern Abbey

She is silent throughout and is given the poem’s whole argument to keep. Wordsworth turns from his own loss to her present pleasure, predicts her future grief, and asks her to remember that he stood there, so memory becomes something two people hold.

The poet

The first-person voice of the Wordsworth poems; first appears Simon Lee

He walks, listens, asks the wrong questions and reports what he could not get. Across the volume he is a man being taught by the people he meets and by the places he returns to, and the last poem turns him from a collector of other people’s stories into the subject of one.
