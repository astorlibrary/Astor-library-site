Lyrical Ballads · William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge

What happens

The Advertisement and the Marinere The Advertisement, and Coleridge’s ballad

The book opens by telling its readers that they may not think these are poems at all, and then hands them the strangest thing in it. Coleridge’s Marinere is in archaic spelling and ballad stanzas, and it takes up nearly a quarter of the volume: a supernatural sea voyage placed at the front of a book that is otherwise about the rural poor.

Advertisement
The poems are offered as trials of whether the language of ordinary conversation can be used for poetry, with a warning that readers will have to set their usual expectations aside.
The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere
A wedding guest is held by an old sailor who shot a bird, watched his crew die, and cannot stop telling anyone about it.
The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere
Thirst, rotting decks and a painted ship: the stretch of the poem everybody remembers.

The talking poems Wordsworth’s narrative poems

Many of Wordsworth’s poems sound like someone telling you a story at a gate. The diction is deliberately flat, the narrators are unreliable or obtuse, and the incidents are so slight that reviewers thought the book a joke. The Thorn is narrated by a garrulous retired sea captain; The Idiot Boy spends four hundred lines on a boy who is never in any danger.

Goody Blake and Harry Gill
An old woman gathers sticks from a hedge, a young farmer catches her, and her prayer leaves him cold for the rest of his life.
Simon Lee
An old huntsman cannot cut through a root; the poet does it in one blow, and the gratitude he gets makes him more uncomfortable than ingratitude would.
The Thorn
A narrator who cannot stop measuring things tells, or half tells, the story of Martha Ray and her child.
The Idiot Boy
Betty Foy sends her son for the doctor; the comedy is real, and so is the tenderness, and Wordsworth defended the poem for the rest of his life.

The dispossessed We Are Seven, The Last of the Flock and other poems of loss

A cluster of poems about people who have lost something and will not be talked out of the loss: a girl who counts her dead siblings among the living, a shepherd down to his last sheep, a mother without a home. They are the most political poems in the book, and none of them argues.

We are seven
An adult explains twice that two of the children are dead, and the little girl declines to change her answer.
Anecdote for Fathers
A father presses his son for a reason and gets an invented one, and the poem’s subtitle calls it a lesson in teaching falsehood.
The Last of the Flock
A man weeps in a public road over the last of the flock he has sold, one by one, to feed his children.
The Female Vagrant
A woman’s account of eviction, an army camp and destitution, in Spenserian stanzas that predate the rest of the book.

The arguing poems Expostulation and Reply, and The Tables Turned

Two short poems in dialogue set out the book’s position more baldly than the Advertisement dares: reading is not the only way of learning, and dissection destroys what it examines. They are quotable to the point of being misquoted.

Expostulation and Reply
Matthew asks why the poet is sitting idle instead of reading, and gets an answer about the mind’s passive powers.
The Tables Turned
The reply, with the book closed: nature as teacher, and the famous charge that analysis murders its subject.
Lines written in early spring
Pleasure in a grove and grief at human arrangements, held in the same short poem.

Tintern Abbey Wordsworth returns to the Wye

The last poem in the volume is unlike the ballads before it: blank verse, unhurried, and built out of one long act of remembering. Wordsworth composed it in his head walking back to Bristol and wrote nothing down until he arrived. It states the whole Romantic argument about memory, landscape and consolation, and then hands it to his sister as insurance.

Tintern Abbey, lines 1–22
Five summers on, the same cliffs, the same hedge-rows, and a mind checking itself against a remembered place.
Tintern Abbey, lines 58–111
What is lost with youth is replaced by the still, sad music of humanity and a sense of something deeply interfused.
Tintern Abbey, lines 111–159
He turns to his sister and asks her to carry the memory for him, and a poem about time becomes a poem about someone else.
