Lyrical Ballads · William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge

Form and language

The ballad stanza

Four-line stanzas alternating four and three stresses, rhyming abcb: the metre of street songs and broadsides.

Using it for serious poetry was the book’s first provocation. Coleridge keeps its archaic furniture and adds to it; Wordsworth strips it down until the metre is carrying a conversation about a dead child.

It is an ancyent Marinere, And he stoppeth one of three.

The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere, 1–2

Refrain

A phrase repeated at intervals, often without change.

Martha Ray’s cry and the thorn’s measurements return again and again, so that the poem behaves like the mind of its narrator. The repetition irritated the first reviewers, which was the price of the effect.

Water, water, every where, Ne any drop to drink.

The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere

Unrhymed pentameter

Five-stress lines without rhyme, the metre of Milton and of English drama.

Blank verse runs through the volume, in The Foster-Mother’s Tale, the Yew-tree lines, The Nightingale and The Dungeon, but Tintern Abbey gives it its longest and freest run: the sentences open out over whole paragraphs, and the change of scale is the change of subject.

Five years have passed; five summers, with the length Of five long winters!

Tintern Abbey, 1–2

Dialogue poems

A poem built as an exchange between two speakers.

Expostulation and Reply and The Tables Turned are two halves of one conversation; We are seven and Anecdote for Fathers are arguments the adult loses. Putting the opposition inside the poem stops the doctrine becoming a sermon.

The majority of the following poems are to be considered as experiments.

Address

Turning from the reader to a named listener inside the text.

The Marinere fixes the wedding guest; Tintern Abbey turns to a sister who never speaks. Both poems need a listener present, so that the reader is watching someone else being told.

It is an ancyent Marinere, And he stoppeth one of three.

The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere, 1–2

Precise settings

Naming and describing real ground.

Hedge-rows run wild, the smoke rises from among the trees, the thorn stands a certain number of paces from a pond. Because of that precision, the argument about nature is more than a preference.

Retrospective narration

A narrator recounting events that are already over, with the ending known.

Almost every poem here is a report: the Marinere’s voyage, the vagrant’s ruin, the huntsman’s better days. The distance between the event and the telling is where the feeling collects.
