Mrs Dalloway · Virginia Woolf

Form and language

Free indirect style

Third-person narration that takes on a character’s vocabulary and judgements without quotation marks or attribution.

The exclamations on the first page (“What a lark! What a plunge!”) belong to Clarissa but are printed as narration, so the reader is inside her before being told whose morning this is.

Mrs. Dalloway said she would buy the flowers herself.

The morning walk

Tunnelling

Reaching a character’s history by digging back from a present moment, instead of narrating it in order.

Woolf’s own name for the method she worked out while writing this book. Bourton is never given a chapter: it arrives in fragments through whichever mind is currently remembering it, and the fragments do not agree.

What a lark! What a plunge!

The morning walk

Shared events

A single public incident observed at the same moment by strangers, used to move the narration between minds.

The car with the drawn blind and the aeroplane writing in smoke each pass through a dozen consciousnesses in a few pages, which lets Woolf change viewpoint without ever leaving the street.

In people’s eyes, in the swing, tramp, and trudge; in the bellow and the uproar; the carriages, motorcars, omnibuses, vans, sandwich men shuffling and swinging; brass bands; barrel organs; in the triumph and the jingle and the strange high singing of some aeroplane overhead was what she loved; life; London; this moment of June.

The morning walk

Refrain

A phrase repeated at intervals, gathering meaning from each new position.

Two lines of Shakespeare’s dirge from Cymbeline surface in Clarissa’s mind at a shop window, in Septimus’s before the end, and again at the party: the one piece of language the two of them share.

The leaden circles dissolved in the air.

The morning walk

The double plot

Two parallel stories staged so that each comments on the other.

Clarissa and Septimus never appear in the same room. Woolf builds the entire novel on the correspondence between them and then delivers it at the party in a few murmured sentences from the wife of the doctor who is responsible.

Death was defiance. Death was an attempt to communicate; people feeling the impossibility of reaching the centre which, mystically, evaded them; closeness drew apart

The party

Objects and omens

Things in the narrative that carry a meaning the characters half-register.

Peter’s pocket-knife, opened and shut whenever he is exposed; Richard’s roses, carried across London instead of a sentence; the green dress mended before the party; and the leaden circles the clock leaves in the air.

The leaden circles dissolved in the air.
