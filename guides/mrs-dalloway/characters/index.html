Mrs Dalloway · Virginia Woolf

Characters

Clarissa Dalloway

A politician’s wife, fifty-two, giving a party; first appears The morning walk

She is treated by everyone around her, including herself, as a woman who has chosen the shallower life, and the novel spends its day proving that the inner one is not shallow at all. Her defence of parties (they are an offering, an attempt to bring separate people into relation) is the closest the book comes to a statement of its own purpose.

Septimus Warren Smith

A clerk, decorated in France, thirty years old; first appears The motor car and the aeroplane

He went to war partly to defend a Shakespeare he had read in a Waterloo Road evening class, came back unable to feel anything, and is being treated for that inability by men who regard it as a lack of proportion. What the novel gives him is perception: in Regent’s Park the trees and the sparrows come to him charged with meaning.

Lucrezia Warren Smith

Septimus’s wife, a hat-maker from Milan; first appears Regent’s Park

She is twenty-four, in a country where she knows nobody, keeping up appearances for a husband whose illness she is repeatedly told does not exist. Woolf gives her the flattest and most unanswerable sentences in the book, and the one shared half-hour of happiness (trimming a hat together) immediately before the end.

Peter Walsh

Back from India, fifty-three, in love with somebody else’s wife; first appears Clarissa at home

He arrives with a pocket-knife he opens and shuts whenever he is nervous, criticises Clarissa’s life at length, and then spends the day thinking about nobody else. He is the book’s evidence that the road not taken is not obviously the better one.

Richard Dalloway

Member of Parliament, Clarissa’s husband; first appears Lady Bruton’s luncheon

He carries roses across London meaning to say that he loves his wife, and hands them over without saying it, and the novel treats this as a real event. His decency answers Peter’s charge that Clarissa married a nonentity.

Sally Seton, Lady Rosseter

The friend of Clarissa’s youth, now married to a mill-owner; first appears The party

At Bourton she smoked cigars, ran naked down a corridor and kissed Clarissa on the terrace, “the most exquisite moment of her whole life”. She arrives at the party uninvited, with five sons and a large house near Manchester, and the gap between the two versions is one of the things the party is for.

Elizabeth Dalloway

The Dalloways’ daughter, seventeen; first appears Clarissa at home

Dark, quiet and compared by everyone to a hyacinth or a poplar, she is the one character looking forwards. On a bus up the Strand she considers becoming a doctor or a farmer, which in 1923 is the novel’s most concrete piece of hope.

Miss Doris Kilman

Elizabeth’s history tutor; first appears Clarissa at home

She lost her teaching post in the war for refusing to say all Germans were villains, has a degree and no money, and has taken refuge in a religion that has not made her kinder. Woolf makes her grasping, self-pitying and entirely explicable, which is harder to read than a straightforward antagonist.

Sir William Bradshaw

Harley Street nerve specialist; first appears Septimus, Holmes and Bradshaw

The son of a shopkeeper who has made himself indispensable, he sees his patients for three-quarters of an hour and prescribes solitude. The novel abandons its usual sympathy to describe the goddesses he serves (Proportion and her sister Conversion), and this is the nearest Woolf comes to naming a villain.

Lady Bruton

A general’s daughter with a project about Canada; first appears Lady Bruton’s luncheon

She invites men to lunch because she needs their prose more than their company, and falls asleep afterwards satisfied that the business has been done. Through her, Woolf shows political influence working through private hospitality: the letter to the Times is composed between the chicken and the coffee.
