Memory and time

The day is short and the past inside it is enormous: a morning errand to buy flowers brings back a summer at Bourton and a refused proposal, and the memory of a kiss on a terrace follows before lunch. Woolf’s method sets public time, struck aloud by Big Ben, against private time, which expands and contracts according to what is being remembered.

Through the book Clocks begin as ordinary background, become oppressive around Septimus’s appointments, and by the evening the striking hour is the thing that tells Clarissa she has been away from her own party too long.

In the text

What a lark! What a plunge!

The morning walk

Two exclamations with no attribution and no verb: the prose has slipped into Clarissa’s own idiom within three sentences of the start. ‘Plunge’ is the word that matters, since it carries both diving into the morning and going under, and the novel will use water for both all day.

He woke with extreme suddenness, saying to himself, “The death of the soul.”

Peter walking

The phrase arrives before its object does; only afterwards does he attach it to a scene at Bourton where Clarissa was rude about a neighbour’s affair. Woolf shows a judgement forming as a sensation first and an argument second, which is how most of the novel’s opinions about people are made.

The war

It is nearly five years since the Armistice and everybody has agreed to have recovered. Septimus has not, and his symptom, an inability to feel, becomes an accusation against a society that congratulates itself on the same thing. The dead are mentioned constantly and in passing: Mrs Foxcroft’s nice boy, Lady Bexborough’s John, an officer called Evans.

Through the book Introduced as background to a backfiring car, made explicit in Septimus’s history, and finally brought into a Westminster drawing-room as a piece of dinner-party news.

In the text

The world has raised its whip; where will it descend?

The motor car and the aeroplane

It is printed as a separate paragraph, unattributed and in the present tense, so it reads as a general statement before the reader realises it is a symptom. The crowd around him is speculating about royalty; he is the only person there who has been where the whip already fell.

He had gone through the whole show, friendship, European War, death, had won promotion, was still under thirty and was bound to survive.

Septimus, Holmes and Bradshaw

Four years are itemised as a list with commas, in which a friendship, a continental war and a death carry equal weight and none carries any. ‘The whole show’ is the language soldiers used, doing here exactly the work it was invented for: making an experience small enough to carry.

Confinement and the asylum

Bradshaw’s treatment is rest: no friends, no books, no messages, six months in a home in the country. The novel presents this as force wearing professional clothes, and gives the power a name and a genealogy, so that what looks like medicine is shown to be the enforcement of a norm.

Through the book Holmes recommends porridge and the music hall; Bradshaw recommends separation; by six o’clock Septimus has decided that the only thing left to him is the window.

In the text

Once you fall, Septimus repeated to himself, human nature is on you. Holmes and Bradshaw are on you.

Septimus, Holmes and Bradshaw

The two doctors are named in the same breath as human nature and given the same grammar, something that is ‘on you’, like weather or a pack. Woolf has made the medical profession into a hunting metaphor, and the sentence explains why, an hour later, a man will prefer a window to a waiting room.

Holmes was coming upstairs. Holmes would burst open the door.

The window and the ambulance

Two short sentences that begin with the same name: Holmes is on the stairs, and by the next sentence he is at the door. Septimus then runs through the bread knife, the gas fire and his razors before he comes to the window, which is all that is left.

Isolation

Everybody in the book is alone in a crowd: a wife on a park bench, a returned lover in a hotel room, a poor tutor praying in a cathedral, a hostess in a room full of her guests. The party exists because somebody thinks the condition can be temporarily interrupted.

Through the book Rezia says it out loud in Regent’s Park; Clarissa thinks it at her window; and the last movement of the book brings the most isolated figure of all into the middle of the most crowded room, as a story.

In the text

She had a perpetual sense, as she watched the taxi cabs, of being out, out, far out to sea and alone; she always had the feeling that it was very, very dangerous to live even one day.

The morning walk

The image arrives while she is looking at traffic, when nothing has happened. Both halves double a word (‘out, out’, ‘very, very’), so the sentence sounds like someone insisting on something they cannot get anyone to take seriously, and the novel will spend the day proving that one day can indeed be fatal.

I am alone; I am alone!

Lucrezia Warren Smith, Regent’s Park

The repetition is the sound of somebody with nobody to say it to, and the sentence has no listener inside the novel at all. Woolf places it in the middle of the most public space in the book, a park full of people, and the paragraph around it widens to Roman roads and ancient marshland, giving loneliness a geological scale.

Performance and sincerity

Clarissa knows exactly how to be a hostess and suspects the skill of being worthless. Richard cannot say what he means; Bradshaw’s manner is his method; Kilman’s piety is partly a costume for rage. The novel wonders whether a practised surface is a lie, or simply the only way some people have of offering something.

Through the book The question is posed at the mended dress, sharpened by Peter’s contempt for parties, and answered ambiguously in the last pages, where Clarissa’s return to the room is presented as a retreat and also an achievement.

In the text

Mrs. Dalloway said she would buy the flowers herself.

The morning walk

It is reported speech with no speech marks, so the reader arrives in the middle of a household conversation already under way. The name is the formal one, husband’s surname and all, and the last word is the only assertion of a self in the sentence: a woman defined by a title chooses to do one small thing alone.

(But he could not bring himself to say he loved her; not in so many words.)

Lady Bruton’s luncheon

Woolf puts the most important fact of the marriage inside brackets, which is the status it has in the marriage. The reader knows what the flowers were rehearsed to say and Clarissa does not, and the novel treats that ordinary failure with the same seriousness it gives to a death.

Class and money

The distance between Clarissa’s staircase and Miss Kilman’s mackintosh is the book’s most reliable measure of feeling. Woolf notices incomes exactly (a tutor’s meagre income, a doctor’s fees, Sally’s five sons and a house near Manchester) and lets them govern how much of a person the others are willing to see.

Through the book Comic at Lady Bruton’s table, painful in the Army and Navy Stores, and structural at the party, where the Prime Minister’s arrival is registered mostly as a change in the noise.

In the text

Proportion, divine proportion, Sir William’s goddess

Septimus, Holmes and Bradshaw

Here Woolf leaves every single consciousness and speaks in the narrator’s own voice, capitalising an abstraction into a deity with a biography: hospitals walked, salmon caught, a son begotten in Harley Street. Making a medical principle into a goddess lets Woolf treat a professional opinion as a religion with converts and casualties.

Conversion is her name and she feasts on the wills of the weakly, loving to impress, to impose, adoring her own features stamped on the face of the populace.

Septimus, Holmes and Bradshaw

The verbs escalate from ‘impress’ to ‘impose’, and the last image, a face stamped on a population like a head on a coin, puts missionary work, empire and psychiatric care in a single frame. This is the most openly political sentence in the novel, and it is aimed at a doctor with excellent manners.
