Mrs Dalloway · Virginia Woolf

Themes

Memory and time

The day is short and the past inside it is enormous: a morning errand to buy flowers brings back a summer at Bourton and a refused proposal, and the memory of a kiss on a terrace follows before lunch. Woolf’s method sets public time, struck aloud by Big Ben, against private time, which expands and contracts according to what is being remembered.

Clocks begin as ordinary background, become oppressive around Septimus’s appointments, and by the evening the striking hour is the thing that tells Clarissa she has been away from her own party too long.

What a lark! What a plunge!

The war

It is nearly five years since the Armistice and everybody has agreed to have recovered. Septimus has not, and his symptom, an inability to feel, becomes an accusation against a society that congratulates itself on the same thing. The dead are mentioned constantly and in passing: Mrs Foxcroft’s nice boy, Lady Bexborough’s John, an officer called Evans.

Introduced as background to a backfiring car, made explicit in Septimus’s history, and finally brought into a Westminster drawing-room as a piece of dinner-party news.

Fear no more the heat o’ the sun Nor the furious winter’s rages.

Confinement and the asylum

Bradshaw’s treatment is rest: no friends, no books, no messages, six months in a home in the country. The novel presents this as force wearing professional clothes, and gives the power a name and a genealogy, so that what looks like medicine is shown to be the enforcement of a norm.

Holmes recommends porridge and the music hall; Bradshaw recommends separation; by six o’clock Septimus has decided that the only thing left to him is the window.

He had not cared when Evans was killed; that was worst

Once you fall, Septimus repeated to himself, human nature is on you. Holmes and Bradshaw are on you.

Isolation

Everybody in the book is alone in a crowd: a wife on a park bench, a returned lover in a hotel room, a poor tutor praying in a cathedral, a hostess in a room full of her guests. The party exists because somebody thinks the condition can be temporarily interrupted.

Rezia says it out loud in Regent’s Park; Clarissa thinks it at her window; and the last movement of the book brings the most isolated figure of all into the middle of the most crowded room, as a story.

Performance and sincerity

Clarissa knows exactly how to be a hostess and suspects the skill of being worthless. Richard cannot say what he means; Bradshaw’s manner is his method; Kilman’s piety is partly a costume for rage. The novel wonders whether a practised surface is a lie, or simply the only way some people have of offering something.

The question is posed at the mended dress, sharpened by Peter’s contempt for parties, and answered ambiguously in the last pages, where Clarissa’s return to the room is presented as a retreat and also an achievement.

Class and money

The distance between Clarissa’s staircase and Miss Kilman’s mackintosh is the book’s most reliable measure of feeling. Woolf notices incomes exactly (a tutor’s meagre income, a doctor’s fees, Sally’s five sons and a house near Manchester) and lets them govern how much of a person the others are willing to see.

Comic at Lady Bruton’s table, painful in the Army and Navy Stores, and structural at the party, where the Prime Minister’s arrival is registered mostly as a change in the noise.
