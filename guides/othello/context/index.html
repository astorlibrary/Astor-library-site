Othello · William Shakespeare

Context

Dates

1565
Cinthio’s Hecatommithi published The Italian story collection containing the tale of the Moor, the ensign and the handkerchief.
1571
Venice loses Cyprus to the Ottomans The island Shakespeare’s garrison defends had been Ottoman for a generation by the time the play was written.
1603
Knolles’s General History of the Turks Evidence of how current Ottoman affairs were in London when the play was taking shape.
1604
First recorded performance at Whitehall The Revels accounts place the Moor of Venice at court on 1 November.
1606
Parliament restrains profanity on the stage Often connected with the Folio text’s removal of oaths that the quarto prints.
1622
The First Quarto printed Printed by Nicholas Okes for Thomas Walkley; its title page records performance at the Globe and Blackfriars.
1623
The First Folio text Around 160 lines longer than the quarto, including the Willow Song and the Pontic sea speech.
1660
Desdemona and the first professional actress Restoration records associate the part with the first appearance of a woman on the English public stage.
1881
Henry Irving plays Iago to Edwin Booth’s Othello A London season that fixed the two parts as a pair of star roles for a generation.
1952
Orson Welles’s film Shot over several years and edited into a fragmented, architectural version of the tragedy.
1995
Oliver Parker’s film Laurence Fishburne as Othello and Kenneth Branagh as Iago, with Iago speaking directly to camera.
2015
Iqbal Khan’s RSC production Hugh Quarshie as Othello and Lucian Msamati as the first Black actor to play Iago at the RSC.

Places

Venice
The republic that needs Othello’s generalship and cannot quite accept his marriage.
Famagusta, Cyprus
The Venetian stronghold on the island; the walled port a garrison would have held against the Ottoman fleet.
Rhodes
The feint in the Senate scene: the Turkish fleet steers for it in order to be somewhere else.
Aleppo
The city Othello names in his last speech, in the story about the turbaned Turk.
Whitehall Palace, London
Where the earliest recorded performance took place, on 1 November 1604.

Critics

Thomas Rymer

A Short View of Tragedy (1693)

Rymer judged the play by neoclassical rules and found it absurd. He suggested that its moral was a warning to wives to “look well to their Linnen”, and called it “a Bloody Farce”. His attack is remembered mainly because later critics answered it.

Samuel Taylor Coleridge

Notes on Othello (1836)

Coleridge described Iago’s soliloquy at the end of the first act, in which he tries out reasons for hating Othello, as “the motive-hunting of a motiveless malignity”. He also argued that Othello is not jealous by nature: his trust is destroyed by what he is made to believe. The notes were published in his Literary Remains.

A. C. Bradley

Shakespearean Tragedy (1904)

Bradley saw Othello as open, trusting and not easily jealous, and Iago as driven less by the motives he names than by a craving to feel his own power over others. In this account the tragedy comes from a noble nature caught by an expert liar.

F. R. Leavis

“Diabolic Intellect and the Noble Hero”, in Scrutiny (1937)

Leavis answered Bradley. He argued that Iago succeeds so quickly because Othello is already predisposed: his self-regard and his idealised view of himself and Desdemona give way at once. The essay was reprinted in The Common Pursuit (1952).

Michael Neill

“Unproper Beds: Race, Adultery, and the Hideous in Othello”, in Shakespeare Quarterly (1989)

Neill argues that the play draws on its first audience’s fears about adultery and about marriage across race, and that it makes the audience share them. He centres his reading on the bed in the last scene, which Lodovico orders to be hidden: “The object poisons sight; / Let it be hid.”
