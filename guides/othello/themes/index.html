Othello · William Shakespeare

Themes

Jealousy

The play distinguishes between suspicion and jealousy with some care. Iago names jealousy before Othello feels it, describes it as a self-feeding animal, and thereby installs the idea in advance. What follows is the working of a machine that supplies its own fuel and cannot be shown counter-evidence.

Named by Iago in 3.3 before it exists, mimicked in 3.4, staged as farce with Bianca in 4.1, and finally spoken of by Othello in 5.2 as though it were a judicial cause.

Excellent wretch! Perdition catch my soul, But I do love thee! and when I love thee not, Chaos is come again.

Othello, 3.3.90

O, beware, my lord, of jealousy; It is the green-eyed monster which doth mock The meat it feeds on;

Iago, 3.3.165

Proof and evidence

Othello asks for ocular proof and is given a scene to watch with the sound arranged. The tragedy turns on a category error that anyone might make: he treats interpretation as sight. Nothing Iago produces would survive a question, and the play makes sure the audience notices that nobody asks it.

Demanded in 3.3, fabricated with the handkerchief in 3.4, performed in 4.1, and dismantled by Emilia in 5.2 with a single fact about who found the cloth.

Look to her, Moor, if thou hast eyes to see: She has deceived her father, and may thee.

Brabantio, 1.3.294

The Moor is of a free and open nature, That thinks men honest that but seem to be so, And will as tenderly be led by the nose As asses are.

Iago, 1.3.386

Reputation and name

Cassio calls reputation the immortal part of himself; Iago tells him it is an idle imposition, and an act later tells Othello it is the immediate jewel of the soul. He says whatever the listener needs to hear. In a garrison, where office depends on public trust, what people say about you is your standing.

Lost by Cassio in 2.3, weaponised by Iago in 3.3, and destroyed for Desdemona in 4.2, where her husband uses the language of the brothel about his own house.

I follow him to serve my turn upon him:

Iago, 1.1.42

Rude am I in my speech, And little bless'd with the soft phrase of peace:

Othello, 1.3.84

Race and belonging

Venice needs Othello and does not consider him one of its own, and the play stages that contradiction without resolving it. Iago’s most violent racial language comes before Othello has spoken a line, so the audience meets the prejudice first. Iago does not invent it; he uses material the society has already prepared.

Shouted under a window in 1.1, argued in the Senate in 1.3, internalised by Othello in 3.3, and returned to by Emilia in 5.2 with the word devil.

Even now, now, very now, an old black ram Is tupping your white ewe.

Iago, 1.1.88

Rude am I in my speech, And little bless'd with the soft phrase of peace:

Othello, 1.3.84

Marriage, duty and women

Three marriages and an attachment run in parallel: Othello and Desdemona, Iago and Emilia, Cassio and Bianca. Each is a different arrangement of trust and property, and all three women are described by men as something other than what the audience has seen them be.

Publicly defended in 1.3, privately corroded through Act III, and analysed by Emilia in 4.3 in the only scene where two women talk to each other at length.

Even now, now, very now, an old black ram Is tupping your white ewe.

Iago, 1.1.88

She loved me for the dangers I had pass'd, And I loved her that she did pity them.

Othello, 1.3.170

Honesty

The word appears more often in this play than in any other by Shakespeare, and almost always attached to Iago. It means truthful, it means chaste when applied to women, and it means socially reliable when applied to a subordinate. The tragedy uses all three meanings together and never lets any character notice the slippage.

A compliment in Acts I and II, a lever in 3.3, an accusation about Desdemona in Act IV, and in 5.2 a word Othello uses about Iago shortly before the truth arrives.

I follow him to serve my turn upon him:

Iago, 1.1.42

But I will wear my heart upon my sleeve For daws to peck at: I am not what I am.

Iago, 1.1.64
