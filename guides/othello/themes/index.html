Jealousy

The play distinguishes between suspicion and jealousy with some care. Iago names jealousy before Othello feels it, describes it as a self-feeding animal, and thereby installs the idea in advance. What follows is the working of a machine that supplies its own fuel and cannot be shown counter-evidence.

Through the play Named by Iago in 3.3 before it exists, mimicked in 3.4, staged as farce with Bianca in 4.1, and finally spoken of by Othello in 5.2 as though it were a judicial cause.

In the text

O, beware, my lord, of jealousy; It is the green-eyed monster which doth mock The meat it feeds on;

Iago, 3.3.165

A warning that functions as an instruction: the word is placed in Othello’s mind under cover of protecting him from it. The image is of something that plays with its food before eating it, a fair description of what Iago does for the rest of the scene.

It is the cause, it is the cause, my soul,— Let me not name it to you, you chaste stars!— It is the cause.

Othello, 5.2.1

He says ‘It is the cause’ three times and will not say what the cause is; he will not even name it to the stars. ‘Cause’ is a legal word, a case to be tried, and with it he talks about the murder as a sentence he must carry out as judge.

Proof and evidence

Othello asks for ocular proof and is given a scene to watch with the sound arranged. The tragedy turns on a category error that anyone might make: he treats interpretation as sight. Nothing Iago produces would survive a question, and the play makes sure the audience notices that nobody asks it.

Through the play Demanded in 3.3, fabricated with the handkerchief in 3.4, performed in 4.1, and dismantled by Emilia in 5.2 with a single fact about who found the cloth.

In the text

Trifles light as air Are to the jealous confirmations strong As proofs of holy writ:

Iago, 3.3.323

He states his epistemology out loud and it is correct: once a man is jealous, weight of evidence stops mattering and any object will do. The comparison with scripture is exact: proof that cannot be questioned, because questioning it is already the offence.

Put out the light, and then put out the light:

Othello, 5.2.7

The same six words twice, meaning a candle and then a life, and the difference between them is the whole of the following seven lines: the lamp can be relit, she cannot. He is working out the metaphor while holding the object, which is why the line can only be understood in performance.

Reputation and name

Cassio calls reputation the immortal part of himself; Iago tells him it is an idle imposition, and an act later tells Othello it is the immediate jewel of the soul. He says whatever the listener needs to hear. In a garrison, where office depends on public trust, what people say about you is your standing.

Through the play Lost by Cassio in 2.3, weaponised by Iago in 3.3, and destroyed for Desdemona in 4.2, where her husband uses the language of the brothel about his own house.

In the text

Reputation, reputation, reputation! O, I have lost my reputation! I have lost the immortal part of myself, and what remains is bestial.

Cassio, 2.3.243

Cassio uses the word five times in three lines, in prose, too drunk to vary his vocabulary, and the content is theologically serious. Iago answers by calling reputation an idle imposition, then tells Othello the opposite in 3.3, which is the clearest evidence that he believes neither.

Good name in man and woman, dear my lord, Is the immediate jewel of their souls: Who steals my purse steals trash;

Iago, 3.3.155

He argues for the sanctity of reputation as a reason to keep quiet, which makes the withholding look like scruple instead of technique. The same man told Cassio in 2.3 that reputation was an idle imposition; the contradiction is never noticed on stage because each speech is exactly what its hearer needs.

Race and belonging

Venice needs Othello and does not consider him one of its own, and the play stages that contradiction without resolving it. Iago’s most violent racial language comes before Othello has spoken a line, so the audience meets the prejudice first. Iago does not invent it; he uses material the society has already prepared.

Through the play Shouted under a window in 1.1, argued in the Senate in 1.3, internalised by Othello in 3.3, and returned to by Emilia in 5.2 with the word devil.

In the text

Even now, now, very now, an old black ram Is tupping your white ewe.

Iago, 1.1.88

The audience hears the marriage described as two animals mating before it meets either partner, and the colours carry the insult. Iago shouts from the dark and never gives Brabantio his name, so the prejudice arrives as a voice from the street.

O, the more angel she, And you the blacker devil!

Emilia, 5.2.127

She reaches for the colour vocabulary the play has been circling since the first scene, and turns it on Othello at the moment he is least able to answer. It is also the first time anyone in Cyprus contradicts him to his face, and the whole exposure of Iago follows from a servant refusing to be quiet.

Marriage, duty and women

Three marriages and an attachment run in parallel: Othello and Desdemona, Iago and Emilia, Cassio and Bianca. Each is a different arrangement of trust and property, and all three women are described by men as something other than what the audience has seen them be.

Through the play Publicly defended in 1.3, privately corroded through Act III, and analysed by Emilia in 4.3 in the only scene where two women talk to each other at length.

In the text

She loved me for the dangers I had pass’d, And I loved her that she did pity them.

Othello, 1.3.170

The couplet balances two lines and two verbs, and shows an imbalance: she loves a story, he loves being listened to. That is a real marriage and a fragile one, and Iago’s whole campaign consists of offering Othello a different story about himself.

But I do think it is their husbands’ faults If wives do fall: say that they slack their duties, And pour our treasures into foreign laps,

Emilia, 4.3.85

The argument is about causes: she explains adultery as a response to husbands’ behaviour without defending it. Coming at the end of an act in which Desdemona has been struck and called a whore, it is the play’s only sustained account of marriage from inside one, and it is spoken by the woman who found the handkerchief and gave it to Iago.

Honesty

The word appears more often in this play than in any other by Shakespeare, and almost always attached to Iago. It means truthful, it means chaste when applied to women, and it means socially reliable when applied to a subordinate. The tragedy uses all three meanings together and never lets any character notice the slippage.

Through the play A compliment in Acts I and II, a lever in 3.3, an accusation about Desdemona in Act IV, and in 5.2 a word Othello uses about Iago shortly before the truth arrives.

In the text

I follow him to serve my turn upon him:

Iago, 1.1.42

‘Follow’ and ‘serve’ are the vocabulary of loyal service, and he uses both against their meaning in a single line. The plan is announced in the play’s first fifty lines, so nothing Iago does later is a surprise to anyone except the people on stage.

Demand me nothing: what you know, you know: From this time forth I never will speak word.

Iago, 5.2.300

The one thing an audience wants at the end of this play is an explanation, and he withholds it deliberately, in a couplet, having talked more than anyone else for five acts. The silence is his last piece of control: no motive can now be checked against the deeds.
