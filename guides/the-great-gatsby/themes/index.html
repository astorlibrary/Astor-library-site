The past

Gatsby wants more than Daisy’s love: he wants her to withdraw four years of her life from the record. The novel treats time the way it treats everything else in Gatsby’s hands, as something he assumes money and effort can acquire, and the last page enlarges the same error into a national habit.

Through the book Stated as a plan in Chapter 6, put to Daisy in the Plaza suite in Chapter 7, and turned into a history of the continent in the final paragraphs.

In the text

“Can’t repeat the past?” he cried incredulously. “Why of course you can!”

Jay Gatsby, Chapter 6

Gatsby is not arguing; he is astonished that anyone would doubt it. The past is treated as something to perform again.

He wanted nothing less of Daisy than that she should go to Tom and say: “I never loved you.”

Chapter 6

What Gatsby demands is a sentence, so his project is to amend the record. Daisy cannot say it, because she loved Tom too.

So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past.

Chapter 9

“Beat on” and “borne back” pull against each other and leave no motion at all, the shape of Gatsby’s five years.

Money

Fitzgerald presents class through sound and space more than numbers: a voice full of money, two eggs identical in shape and unlike in everything else, a valley between the houses and the city. Gatsby can buy the mansion, the shirts and the car, and the one thing he cannot buy is the sound of not having had to.

Through the book Set up by the geography of Chapter 1, made explicit in Gatsby’s remark about Daisy’s voice in Chapter 7, and enforced by Tom in the same chapter.

In the text

“Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone,” he told me, “just remember that all the people in this world haven’t had the advantages that you’ve had.”

Nick’s father, Chapter 1

His father’s advice assumes that both of them are on the advantaged side, and says nothing about what to do with that.

I believe that on the first night I went to Gatsby’s house I was one of the few guests who had actually been invited. People were not invited⁠—they went there.

Chapter 3

Invitation, the basic act of hospitality, has collapsed at Gatsby’s parties; Nick’s one distinction is that he was asked.

“Her voice is full of money,” he said suddenly.

Jay Gatsby, Chapter 7

Gatsby settles in five words what Nick has failed to describe, and names the one thing he cannot buy.

Self-invention

James Gatz becomes Jay Gatsby at seventeen on the shore of Lake Superior, and the novel calls it a Platonic conception: an idea of himself that he then has to furnish. Everything from the Oxford photograph to the phrase “old sport” is evidence being assembled for a case nobody has formally brought.

Through the book Withheld through four chapters of rumour, delivered in Chapter 6, and completed in Chapter 9 by a father with a boy’s timetable written in the back of a book.

In the text

The truth was that Jay Gatsby of West Egg, Long Island, sprang from his Platonic conception of himself. He was a son of God⁠—a phrase which, if it means anything, means just that⁠—and he must be about His Father’s business, the service of a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty.

Chapter 6

Platonic philosophy and the Gospel welded together to explain a boy changing his name, and Nick declines to guarantee the sentence.

Now it was again a green light on a dock. His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one.

Chapter 5

“Count” is an accountant’s word. Getting what he wanted cost Gatsby an enchantment, because the light only worked at a distance.

There must have been moments even that afternoon when Daisy tumbled short of his dreams⁠—not through her own fault, but because of the colossal vitality of his illusion.

Chapter 5

Nick clears Daisy outright: the illusion outgrew her, “not through her own fault”.

Carelessness

The word runs from Jordan’s driving in Chapter 3 to Nick’s summary of the Buchanans in Chapter 9, and it is deliberately not the same as cruelty. Carelessness is what money makes possible: the ability to damage something and then to be somewhere else, with the address unavailable.

Through the book Introduced as a joke about a badly parked car, and paid out when a woman is killed by one and nobody who was in it is inconvenienced.

In the text

“I hope I never will,” she answered. “I hate careless people. That’s why I like you.”

Jordan Baker, Chapter 3

Jordan says she hates careless people, having just explained that her own carelessness is safe until she meets another.

“You’re a rotten driver,” I protested. “Either you ought to be more careful, or you oughtn’t to drive at all.”

Nick Carraway, Chapter 3

Driving is the novel’s practical test of character: every collision involves someone who expected the other party to take care.

They were careless people, Tom and Daisy⁠—they smashed up things and creatures and then retreated back into their money or their vast carelessness, or whatever it was that kept them together, and let other people clean up the mess they had made

Chapter 9

“Things and creatures” declines to say people. The judgement Nick promised to reserve is delivered here, in the past tense.

East Egg, West Egg and the valley of ashes

The novel’s map does the sociology. East Egg holds inherited money, West Egg new money, Manhattan the places where the rules are suspended, and between them a valley where the city’s ash is dumped and the people who shovel it live. The book’s fatal accident happens on the road through that valley.

Through the book Laid out in Chapter 2, revisited on the way to the Plaza in Chapter 7, and the site of the accident on the way back.

In the text

This is a valley of ashes⁠—a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens; where ashes take the forms of houses and chimneys and rising smoke and, finally, with a transcendent effort, of ash-grey men, who move dimly and already crumbling through the powdery air.

Chapter 2

An industrial dump described as a farm, growing what the city throws away. The men who work it arrive last in the sentence.

The eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg are blue and gigantic⁠—their retinas are one yard high. They look out of no face, but, instead, from a pair of enormous yellow spectacles which pass over a nonexistent nose.

Chapter 2

Eyes attached to nothing, measured in yards. George Wilson will later decide they are God’s.

gradually I became aware of the old island here that flowered once for Dutch sailors’ eyes⁠—a fresh, green breast of the new world

Chapter 9

The houses melt away and the island the Dutch sailors saw is laid over Long Island, enlarging one man’s obsession into a nation’s.

Nick’s judgement

Nick’s opening claim to reserve judgement is worth testing sentence by sentence against what he actually does, which is to assign moral, social and aesthetic value continually. The novel is his account, written after he has left, and the reader’s task is to notice whose interior life he grants and whose he does not.

Through the book Claimed in Chapter 1, complicated by his silence about the accident in Chapter 7, and declared outright in Chapter 9 when he tells Tom what he thinks of him.

In the text

In consequence, I’m inclined to reserve all judgements, a habit that has opened up many curious natures to me and also made me the victim of not a few veteran bores.

Chapter 1

Suspending judgement, Nick explains, is a way of being told things, and he judges on nearly every page.

it was an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person and which it is not likely I shall ever find again

Chapter 1

The verdict on Gatsby comes in the opening pages, so the novel is an account of how Nick reached it.

“They’re a rotten crowd,” I shouted across the lawn. “You’re worth the whole damn bunch put together.”

Nick Carraway, Chapter 8

The only compliment Nick ever paid Gatsby, and he adds that he disapproved of him from beginning to end.
