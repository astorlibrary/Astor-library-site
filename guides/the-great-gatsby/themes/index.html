The past

Gatsby wants more than Daisy’s love: he wants her to withdraw four years of her life from the record. The novel treats time the way it treats everything else in Gatsby’s hands, as something he assumes money and effort can acquire, and the last page enlarges the same error into a national habit.

Through the book Stated as a plan in Chapter 6, put to Daisy in the Plaza suite in Chapter 7, and turned into a history of the continent in the final paragraphs.

In the text

“Can’t repeat the past?” he cried incredulously. “Why of course you can!”

Jay Gatsby, Chapter 6

The adverb is the whole characterisation: he is not arguing, he is astonished that anyone would doubt it. Grammatically he treats the past as a direct object, something one performs again, and the rest of the novel shows that it is the one commodity his money cannot reach.

“Her voice is full of money,” he said suddenly.

Jay Gatsby, Chapter 7

Nick has spent two chapters failing to describe Daisy’s voice and Gatsby settles it in five words, which is the one moment in the book where he sees more clearly than the narrator. It is also the diagnosis of his own defeat: what he loves in her is a sound produced by a security he was not born into and cannot purchase.

Money

Fitzgerald presents class through sound and space more than numbers: a voice full of money, two eggs identical in shape and unlike in everything else, a valley between the houses and the city. Gatsby can buy the mansion, the shirts and the car, and the one thing he cannot buy is the sound of not having had to.

Through the book Set up by the geography of Chapter 1, made explicit in Gatsby’s remark about Daisy’s voice in Chapter 7, and enforced by Tom in the same chapter.

In the text

‘All right,’ I said, ‘I’m glad it’s a girl. And I hope she’ll be a fool⁠—that’s the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool.’

Daisy Buchanan, Chapter 1

It is a quotation inside a quotation, which lets her disown it while saying it, and the setting matters: she is describing the hour in which her husband was elsewhere. The line is often read as cynicism about women, but it is a piece of career advice from someone who has calculated exactly what her own intelligence has been worth to her.

I believe that on the first night I went to Gatsby’s house I was one of the few guests who had actually been invited. People were not invited⁠—they went there.

Chapter 3

The verb is repeated three times in two sentences so that invitation, the basic transaction of hospitality, can be shown to have collapsed. Nick’s one small distinction, that he was asked, qualifies him to narrate, and it also tells the reader that the parties are not for the people at them.

Self-invention

James Gatz becomes Jay Gatsby at seventeen on the shore of Lake Superior, and the novel calls it a Platonic conception: an idea of himself that he then has to furnish. Everything from the Oxford photograph to the phrase “old sport” is evidence being assembled for a case nobody has formally brought.

Through the book Withheld through four chapters of rumour, delivered in Chapter 6, and completed in Chapter 9 by a father with a boy’s timetable written in the back of a book.

In the text

There must have been moments even that afternoon when Daisy tumbled short of his dreams⁠—not through her own fault, but because of the colossal vitality of his illusion.

Chapter 5

The exoneration is explicit, ‘not through her own fault’, which makes it much harder to read the novel as a case against Daisy. ‘Vitality’ gives the illusion a life of its own, as though it had grown by itself, and by this measure Gatsby’s tragedy lies less in losing her than in the fact that no actual person could have been what he had spent five years building.

The truth was that Jay Gatsby of West Egg, Long Island, sprang from his Platonic conception of himself. He was a son of God⁠—a phrase which, if it means anything, means just that⁠—and he must be about His Father’s business, the service of a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty.

Chapter 6

Two incompatible vocabularies are welded together: Platonic philosophy and the Gospel of Luke, both used to explain a boy changing his name. The aside (‘if it means anything’) is Nick declining to guarantee his own sentence, and the last three adjectives make the object of worship squarely commercial without withdrawing the religious language.

Carelessness

The word runs from Jordan’s driving in Chapter 3 to Nick’s summary of the Buchanans in Chapter 9, and it is deliberately not the same as cruelty. Carelessness is what money makes possible: the ability to damage something and then to be somewhere else, with the address unavailable.

Through the book Introduced as a joke about a badly parked car, and paid out when a woman is killed by one and nobody who was in it is inconvenienced.

In the text

“I hope I never will,” she answered. “I hate careless people. That’s why I like you.”

Jordan Baker, Chapter 3

She has explained that it takes two to make an accident, so her own carelessness is safe until she meets another one, and then says she hates careless people. The word she introduces here is the one Nick will use, six chapters later, to sum up Tom and Daisy; she hands him the term for the novel’s central charge while demonstrating the offence.

“The poor son-of-a-bitch,” he said.

Owl Eyes, Chapter 9

The only epitaph Gatsby gets comes from a man who went to one party and never met him, and it is four words of profanity. Set it against the two pages of lyric prose that close the book: the novel gives the plain human verdict first, and the grand one afterwards, and does not say which is truer.

East Egg, West Egg and the valley of ashes

The novel’s map does the sociology. East Egg holds inherited money, West Egg new money, Manhattan the places where the rules are suspended, and between them a valley where the city’s ash is dumped and the people who shovel it live. The book’s fatal accident happens on the road through that valley.

Through the book Laid out in Chapter 2, revisited on the way to the Plaza in Chapter 7, and the site of the accident on the way back.

In the text

This is a valley of ashes⁠—a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens; where ashes take the forms of houses and chimneys and rising smoke and, finally, with a transcendent effort, of ash-grey men, who move dimly and already crumbling through the powdery air.

Chapter 2

The metaphor is agricultural (a farm, wheat, gardens), applied to industrial waste, so the valley is described as growing what the city throws away. The sentence takes four clauses to arrive at the men, and by the time it does they are made of the same material as the landscape, which is the book’s flattest statement about who is disposable.

gradually I became aware of the old island here that flowered once for Dutch sailors’ eyes⁠—a fresh, green breast of the new world

Chapter 9

The houses are made to melt away so that a seventeenth-century landscape can be laid over a twentieth-century one, and the colour green arrives from the dock light three hundred pages earlier. The move enlarges one man’s obsession into a continental habit without ever quite saying that Gatsby stands for America.

Nick’s judgement

Nick’s opening claim to reserve judgement is worth testing sentence by sentence against what he actually does, which is to assign moral, social and aesthetic value continually. The novel is his account, written after he has left, and the reader’s task is to notice whose interior life he grants and whose he does not.

Through the book Claimed in Chapter 1, complicated by his silence about the accident in Chapter 7, and declared outright in Chapter 9 when he tells Tom what he thinks of him.

In the text

“Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone,” he told me, “just remember that all the people in this world haven’t had the advantages that you’ve had.”

Nick’s father, Chapter 1

The sentence sounds like generosity and is actually an instruction in class: it assumes both speaker and listener are on the advantaged side, and says nothing about what to do with that. Nick spends the novel among people who have had more advantages than he has, and never once applies the rule to them.

“I’m thirty,” I said. “I’m five years too old to lie to myself and call it honour.”

Nick Carraway, Chapter 9

He uses his birthday as a moral unit of measurement, as though self-deception had an age limit, and by his arithmetic the age for honourable self-deception ended at twenty-five. Jordan’s reply, that she thought he was an honest, straightforward person, is the novel’s last word on his opening claim.
