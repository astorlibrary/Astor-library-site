The Great Gatsby · F. Scott Fitzgerald

Themes

The past

Gatsby wants more than Daisy’s love: he wants her to withdraw four years of her life from the record. The novel treats time the way it treats everything else in Gatsby’s hands, as something he assumes money and effort can acquire, and the last page enlarges the same error into a national habit.

Stated as a plan in Chapter 6, put to Daisy in the Plaza suite in Chapter 7, and turned into a history of the continent in the final paragraphs.

it was an extraordinary gift for hope, a romantic readiness such as I have never found in any other person and which it is not likely I shall ever find again

Chapter 1

Involuntarily I glanced seaward⁠—and distinguished nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock.

Money

Fitzgerald presents class through sound and space more than numbers: a voice full of money, two eggs identical in shape and unlike in everything else, a valley between the houses and the city. Gatsby can buy the mansion, the shirts and the car, and the one thing he cannot buy is the sound of not having had to.

Set up by the geography of Chapter 1, made explicit in Gatsby’s remark about Daisy’s voice in Chapter 7, and enforced by Tom in the same chapter.

“Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone,” he told me, “just remember that all the people in this world haven’t had the advantages that you’ve had.”

Nick's father, Chapter 1

‘All right,’ I said, ‘I’m glad it’s a girl. And I hope she’ll be a fool⁠—that’s the best thing a girl can be in this world, a beautiful little fool.’

Daisy Buchanan, Chapter 1

Self-invention

James Gatz becomes Jay Gatsby at seventeen on the shore of Lake Superior, and the novel calls it a Platonic conception: an idea of himself that he then has to furnish. Everything from the Oxford photograph to the phrase “old sport” is evidence being assembled for a case nobody has formally brought.

Withheld through four chapters of rumour, delivered in Chapter 6, and completed in Chapter 9 by a father with a boy’s timetable written in the back of a book.

I believe that on the first night I went to Gatsby’s house I was one of the few guests who had actually been invited. People were not invited⁠—they went there.

Chapter 3

Now it was again a green light on a dock. His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one.

Chapter 5

Carelessness

The word runs from Jordan’s driving in Chapter 3 to Nick’s summary of the Buchanans in Chapter 9, and it is deliberately not the same as cruelty. Carelessness is what money makes possible: the ability to damage something and then to be somewhere else, with the address unavailable.

Introduced as a joke about a badly parked car, and paid out when a woman is killed by one and nobody who was in it is inconvenienced.

“I hope I never will,” she answered. “I hate careless people. That’s why I like you.”

Jordan Baker, Chapter 3

“You’re a rotten driver,” I protested. “Either you ought to be more careful, or you oughtn’t to drive at all.”

Nick Carraway, Chapter 3

East Egg, West Egg and the valley of ashes

The novel’s map does the sociology. East Egg holds inherited money, West Egg new money, Manhattan the places where the rules are suspended, and between them a valley where the city’s ash is dumped and the people who shovel it live. The book’s fatal accident happens on the road through that valley.

Laid out in Chapter 2, revisited on the way to the Plaza in Chapter 7, and the site of the accident on the way back.

This is a valley of ashes⁠—a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens; where ashes take the forms of houses and chimneys and rising smoke and, finally, with a transcendent effort, of ash-grey men, who move dimly and already crumbling through the powdery air.

Chapter 2

The eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg are blue and gigantic⁠—their retinas are one yard high. They look out of no face, but, instead, from a pair of enormous yellow spectacles which pass over a nonexistent nose.

Chapter 2

Nick’s judgement

Nick’s opening claim to reserve judgement is worth testing sentence by sentence against what he actually does, which is to assign moral, social and aesthetic value continually. The novel is his account, written after he has left, and the reader’s task is to notice whose interior life he grants and whose he does not.

Claimed in Chapter 1, complicated by his silence about the accident in Chapter 7, and declared outright in Chapter 9 when he tells Tom what he thinks of him.

“Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone,” he told me, “just remember that all the people in this world haven’t had the advantages that you’ve had.”

Nick's father, Chapter 1

In consequence, I’m inclined to reserve all judgements, a habit that has opened up many curious natures to me and also made me the victim of not a few veteran bores.
