The Great Gatsby · F. Scott Fitzgerald

Characters

Nick Carraway

Narrator; a bond salesman from the Midwest; first appears Chapter 1

He opens by claiming a habit of suspended judgement and then spends nine chapters judging everyone he meets, often by their voices and their hands. The contradiction is the book’s engine: reserving judgement is what gets him invited everywhere, and writing the account afterwards is where the verdict is finally delivered.

Jay Gatsby

Owner of the house next door; formerly James Gatz; first appears Chapter 1

A farm boy who invented a wealthy self at seventeen and then spent fifteen years assembling the evidence for him: a name, an accent, a war record, a mansion positioned directly across the water from one particular dock. His real crime is the belief that five years can be cancelled if you buy enough; the bootlegging matters less.

Daisy Buchanan

Tom’s wife, Nick’s cousin, and the object of the whole scheme; first appears Chapter 1

She is charming in a way the novel presents as an audible property of money, and she is no fool: her remark about wanting her daughter to be “a beautiful little fool” is a report on what her intelligence has been worth to her. When the choice comes she does not make it, and she is not there when the bill arrives.

Tom Buchanan

A polo player with inherited money; first appears Chapter 1

Physically enormous, intellectually credulous, and secure in a way the novel puts down to his position more than his character. He conducts an affair openly, reads racial pseudo-science aloud at dinner, and wins the crucial argument by being the one person in the room whose past cannot be revised, without being right about anything.

Jordan Baker

A golfer; first appears Chapter 1

A professional sportswoman who once moved a ball in a semi-final and left the rumour to die on its own. She is the novel’s other unreliable narrator: it is her account in Chapter 4 that carries the history Nick cannot get from Gatsby, and she is careless in exactly the way Nick claims to despise.

Myrtle Wilson

Wife of a garage proprietor in the valley of ashes; first appears Chapter 2

She has one room in Manhattan in which she is a different person, and the novel is precise about what her ambition can actually buy: a dog collar, some magazines, a tea set. She runs out to the yellow car because she thinks Tom is driving it, and it kills her.

George Wilson

Garage proprietor under the eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg; first appears Chapter 2

The only poor man in the novel, and the only one who acts on a moral conviction. He locks his wife in a room, mistakes an advertising hoarding for God, is given a name and an address by the man who has been sleeping with her, and does what he is pointed at.

Meyer Wolfshiem

Gatsby’s business connection; first appears Chapter 4

Introduced as the man who fixed a World Series, and written with a stereotyping that a modern reader has to see and name. He is also the novel’s clearest statement of how Gatsby’s money was made, and the one associate who explains, in a letter, that he cannot afford to attend the funeral.
