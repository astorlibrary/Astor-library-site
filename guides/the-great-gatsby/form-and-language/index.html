The Great Gatsby · F. Scott Fitzgerald

Form and language

Retrospective first person

A narrator telling, after the event, a story in which he took part.

Nick is writing in the Midwest, some time after he came home ‘last autumn’, which is why the judgements he claims to withhold are already in the prose: the verdict was reached before the first sentence was written.

“Whenever you feel like criticizing anyone,” he told me, “just remember that all the people in this world haven’t had the advantages that you’ve had.”

Nick's father, Chapter 1

Delayed disclosure

Withholding the facts of a character’s life until the reader has formed an impression without them.

Gatsby is a rumour and then a host in Chapter 3, the teller of his own legend in Chapter 4, a lover in Chapter 5, and only in Chapter 6 a poor boy from North Dakota, so the reader meets the invention before the man.

Involuntarily I glanced seaward⁠—and distinguished nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock.

Chapter 1

Symbolism

An object whose meaning changes as a character’s knowledge of it changes.

The green light is an unexplained gesture in Chapter 1, a dock light in Chapter 5 once Daisy is in the room, and a figure for the whole continent’s appetite in Chapter 9.

Involuntarily I glanced seaward⁠—and distinguished nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock.

Chapter 1

Catalogue

A list used for social observation.

The guest list written on a railway timetable gives two pages of names and small disasters, and demonstrates without comment that nobody at the parties knows the host.

I believe that on the first night I went to Gatsby’s house I was one of the few guests who had actually been invited. People were not invited⁠—they went there.

Chapter 3

Weather

Using heat, rain and light to time the emotional register of a scene.

Gatsby’s reunion with Daisy is scheduled in rain that stops at the moment it should, and the Plaza confrontation is staged on the hottest day of the summer with a wedding band playing downstairs.

The ending

Shifting at the end of a passage from reported speech and event into sustained rhythmic prose.

The last two pages leave the plot entirely for a meditation on Dutch sailors and the current, and the final sentence moves from a verb of effort, beat on, to a participle, borne back, that reverses the direction of travel.

A new world, material without being real, where poor ghosts, breathing dreams like air, drifted fortuitously about
