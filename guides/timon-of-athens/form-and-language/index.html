Timon of Athens · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Catalogue

A list allowed to run on past the point of usefulness, so that the accumulation itself becomes the effect.

The curse at the wall in 4.1 works through Athenian society in order (matrons, children, slaves, bankrupts, bound servants, sons of sixteen), and the instructions to Alcibiades go through honoured age, matron, virgin and babe. The lists get longer as the argument gets weaker.

Knock me down with 'em! Cleave me to the girdle. … Cut my heart in sums! … Tell out my blood.

Timon, 3.4.89

Antithesis

Two opposites set against each other inside a single line, so that the sentence turns on the join.

The gold speech is built of pairs: black white, foul fair, wrong right, base noble, old young, coward valiant. Six reversals in two lines with no verb between them, which is the whole claim about what money does.

Ah, when the means are gone that buy this praise, The breath is gone whereof this praise is made. Feast-won, fast-lost

Flavius, 2.2.165

Epigram

A short balanced sentence that sounds like settled wisdom and usually turns a received idea inside out.

Scene after scene closes on a rhyming couplet that settles the argument: ‘Who bates mine honour shall not know my coin’, ‘Feast-won, fast-lost’, ‘Men shut their doors against a setting sun’. Self-interest keeps arriving in the shape of a proverb.

I gave it freely ever, and there's none Can truly say he gives if he receives.

Timon, 1.2.11

The set piece

A self-contained episode built to a single effect and complete in itself, whether comic or ceremonial.

Both banquets are complete performances, and the second is an exact copy of the first with the food taken out. The masque of Cupid and Amazons in 1.2 is a court entertainment dropped whole into the middle of a dinner and applauded by the man paying for it.

O you gods, what a number of men eat Timon, and he sees ’em not! It grieves me to see so many dip their meat in one man’s blood;

Apemantus, 1.2.40

Stage property

An object handled on stage that carries far more weight than its use in the scene explains.

A casket of jewels, an empty box under Flaminius’s cloak, three solidares thrown on the floor, a sheaf of bills, covered dishes, a spade, a root, a stone, a glove and a wax impression of an epitaph. The argument is carried by things passing from hand to hand.

May you a better feast never behold, You knot of mouth-friends! Smoke and lukewarm water Is your perfection.

Timon, 3.6.71

Double plot

A second story running beside the first, repeating its problem in another key and commenting on it.

Alcibiades is wronged by Athens in the same act as Timon, exiled in the same act, and given gold in the same scene in the wood. He is the control in the experiment: the same injury, and a man who can still tell one Athenian from another.

Banish me? Banish your dotage, banish usury, That makes the Senate ugly.

Alcibiades, 3.5.101

Foils

A character constructed to throw another’s choices into relief by making different ones.

Apemantus has hated mankind from the start and it has never cost him anything; Flavius has every reason to hate and does not. Timon stands between them, and the long quarrel of 4.3 is an argument about which of the two he has turned into.

The middle of humanity thou never knewest, but the extremity of both ends.

Apemantus, 4.3.311
