Timon of Athens · William Shakespeare

What happens

Act I The open house

Athens comes to Timon’s door with things to sell and favours to ask. He pays five talents to free Ventidius, matches an old man’s dowry so that a servant may marry his daughter, and takes a jewel and a painting for which he gives back more than either is worth. At the banquet Apemantus eats a root at his own table and tells the room they are eating their host. Flavius, holding the bills, asks twice for a word and is twice put off.

1.1.1–101
The Poet, Painter, Jeweller and Merchant wait in the hall and price their wares. The Poet describes his new piece: Fortune throned on a hill, one man beckoned up past his fellows, and every follower letting him slip when she spurns him down.
1.1.102–287
Timon frees Ventidius from a debtors’ prison, counterpoises a dowry so that Lucilius may marry an old man’s daughter, and buys both the painting and the jewel. Apemantus arrives and calls the whole room knaves on the grounds that they are Athenians.
1.2.1–105
Ventidius, newly rich, offers to repay the five talents and Timon refuses them. Apemantus takes a table by himself, says a grace that asks the gods for nothing, and watches the guests dip their meat in one man’s blood.
1.2.106–248
Cupid brings in a masque of Amazons with lutes; Timon sends Flavius for the jewels and gives them away with horses and a courser a guest happened to praise. Flavius, aside, works out that his master now owes for every word he speaks.

Act II The bills come in

A senator does the arithmetic Timon has never done and sends his man out with the bonds. Creditors’ servants collect at the gate while dinner goes on inside. Flavius gets his hour at last and lays the books open: the land is sold, the coffers are empty, and the greatest of Timon’s having lacks a half of what he owes. Timon’s answer is that his wants are a blessing, because now he can find out who his friends are.

2.1
Five-and-twenty thousand owing, and a creditor who says that a beggar’s dog given to Timon would coin gold. The senator sends Caphis to Timon’s house with the bonds and the dates filled in, and ‘a visage of demand’.
2.2.1–50
Caphis and the servants of Varro and Isidore press their bills on Timon as he comes in from hunting. He tells them to go to his steward, asks for breath, and walks in to dinner.
2.2.51–113
While the creditors wait, Apemantus and a brothel-keeper’s fool call them usurers’ men, asses and bawds between gold and want, and the play stops for a bout of wordplay about fools, knaves and whoremasters.
2.2.114–234
Flavius shows that he brought the books many times and was waved away. Timon sends servants to Lucius, Lucullus and Sempronius for fifty talents each, and to the senate for a thousand, and is told the senate has already shaken its head.

Act III Timon’s friends refuse him

Lucullus comes downstairs expecting a present, hears the request and tries to buy the messenger’s silence with three coins. Lucius performs astonishment at Lucullus’s meanness and then refuses in the same breath. Sempronius, asked fourth, is insulted that he was not asked first, and makes the insult his excuse. In the same act the senate condemns a soldier and banishes Alcibiades for pleading mercy, and Timon invites the whole circle back for a second dinner.

3.1
Flaminius is welcomed as the bearer of a gift. When the box under his cloak turns out to be empty, Lucullus explains that friendship is no security for a loan and offers him coins to say he saw nobody. Flaminius throws the money back.
3.2–3.3
Lucius tells three strangers how little honour Lucullus showed, and then discovers that he unluckily spent everything the day before. Sempronius makes a grievance of the order of asking and sends Timon’s servant away with a couplet about honour and coin.
3.4
Titus, Hortensius, Philotus and the servants of Varro compare sums in Timon’s hall, several of them collecting for lords who are still wearing his jewels. Timon breaks out among them and tells them to cut his heart into sums.
3.5
Alcibiades asks the senate to spare a soldier who killed a man in hot blood. The senators answer that to bear is valour and to revenge is none, sentence the man to die, and banish Alcibiades for pressing them twice.
3.6
The guests come back, telling one another that the first request must have been a trial of their friendship. Timon says a grace asking the gods to bless nobody, uncovers the dishes, throws the water in their faces and beats them out of the room.

Act IV Out of Athens

Timon turns at the wall and curses the city by rank and by age (matrons, children, bound servants, bankrupts, sons of sixteen), then goes to the woods to live on roots. The household breaks up behind him and Flavius divides the last of his own money among the dismissed servants. Digging for food, Timon turns up gold, and everyone who hears of it comes out to the wood: a general, two women, a philosopher, three thieves, and the one man who followed him for nothing.

4.1
Alone outside Athens, Timon asks that obedience fail in children, that bound servants steal and that the wrinkled senate be pulled from the bench, and prays that his own hatred may grow as he grows.
4.2
The servants ask where their master is. Flavius calls them broken implements of a ruined house, shares out what money he has left among them, and sets off to find Timon and serve him still.
4.3.1–48
Timon digs for a root and turns up gold, and names straight away what it will do: make black white, foul fair and the coward valiant. A drum sounds in the distance and he buries most of it, keeping some out for earnest.
4.3.49–205
Alcibiades arrives with drum and fife on his way to Athens. Timon gives him gold for the war and tells him to spare neither age nor virgin nor babe, then pays Phrynia and Timandra to carry disease back into the city.
4.3.207–545
Apemantus accuses Timon of taking up misanthropy only when the money ran out, and they trade insults until a stone is thrown. Three bandits get gold and a lecture proving the sun and moon are thieves. Flavius arrives, weeps, and is named the one honest man alive.

Act V The tomb and the gate

The Poet and the Painter come out to the cave with promises instead of work, having heard about the gold, and are driven off with what they came for. Two senators offer Timon absolute power in Athens if he will hold Alcibiades off; he offers them his tree to hang themselves on. He dies offstage by the sea, leaving an epitaph that curses whoever reads it, and Alcibiades takes the city on terms.

5.1.1–109
The Poet and the Painter agree to offer Timon a piece each will only promise. He overhears the plan, calls them honest men until the word is worn through, and gives them gold on condition that each drives the other’s villain out of his company.
5.1.110–229
Athens offers Timon the captainship, special dignities and heaps of love and wealth. He says he is writing his epitaph, invites the whole city in order of degree to come and hang on his tree before he fells it, and goes back inside.
5.2
Two more senators hear from a messenger that Alcibiades’ files are as full as the report said, and that letters of entreaty have already gone from him to Timon’s cave.
5.3
A soldier finds a grave on the sea’s edge and an inscription he cannot read, takes the characters away in wax, and carries them to a captain who has skill in every figure.
5.4
The senators argue that crimes are not inherited and that the walls were not built by the men who wronged him. Alcibiades throws down his glove, names only his enemies and Timon’s for punishment, hears the epitaph read aloud, and marches in.
