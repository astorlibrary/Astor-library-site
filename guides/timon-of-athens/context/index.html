Timon of Athens · William Shakespeare

Context

Dates

1579
North’s Plutarch published in English The Lives of the Noble Grecians and Romanes, where the lives of Antony and Alcibiades carry the few paragraphs about Timon and both of the epitaphs.
1602
An academic Timon comedy circulates in manuscript An anonymous English play on the same story, with a mock banquet of painted stones, survives from about this date. Whether Shakespeare knew it is disputed.
1606
The play written, probably with Thomas Middleton No payment, performance or Stationers’ Register entry fixes the date. The verse and the vocabulary sit close to King Lear.
1623
Printed in the First Folio The only early text, set between Romeo and Juliet and Julius Caesar in a gap that opened when the printers ran into difficulty over Troilus and Cressida.
1678
Shadwell’s The History of Timon of Athens, the Man-Hater An adaptation that gave Timon two women, one faithful and one false, and kept the story on the London stage for most of a century.
1771
Richard Cumberland’s version at Drury Lane Another rewriting, this time providing Timon with a daughter to be married to Alcibiades.
1851
Samuel Phelps stages it at Sadler’s Wells Close to Shakespeare’s text, and the first London production in generations that was not an adaptation.
1965
Paul Scofield plays Timon for the Royal Shakespeare Company Directed by John Schlesinger at Stratford, and the production that brought the play back into the regular repertory.
1980
Richard Pasco plays Timon for the RSC One of a run of postwar revivals that treated the play as a drama of patronage and money instead of an unfinished curiosity.
1999
Gregory Doran directs Michael Pennington at the RSC Staged in a world of corporate hospitality, with the first banquet played as a company party.
2016
The New Oxford Shakespeare prints it as a collaboration Timon of Athens appears under two names, Shakespeare’s and Middleton’s, after decades of stylistic and textual work.
2018
Simon Godwin directs Kathryn Hunter as Timon At the RSC, with gold as the visible substance of the first half and a set that decayed as the money went.

Places

Athens
Timon’s house, the senate house, and the wall he turns back to curse in 4.1.
Sparta
Lacedaemon, where Timon says his land once reached, and where Alcibiades’ condemned soldier served.
Istanbul
Byzantium, the other campaign Alcibiades names when he asks the senate to weigh service against one killing.
Chaeronea
Plutarch’s town. His lives of Antony and Alcibiades hold the few paragraphs the play grew from, and both epitaphs.
Sadler's Wells, London
Where Samuel Phelps put Shakespeare’s text back on a London stage in 1851, after nearly two centuries of adaptations.

Critics

Karl Marx

Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts (1844)

Marx quoted Timon’s speech on gold, “Thus much of this will make black white, foul fair”, as a description of what money does: it turns every quality into its opposite and makes anything exchangeable for anything else.
