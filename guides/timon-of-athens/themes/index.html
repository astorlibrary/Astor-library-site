Money

Talents, solidares, crowns, bonds, interest, dowries, jewels and horses are counted out all through the play, and every relationship in it has a figure attached. The gold Timon finds in the wood does openly what his generosity did discreetly: it brings people to him.

Through the play In Act I gifts are a currency of love; Act III prices that love at fifty talents and finds it will not pay; in Act IV the metal is dug out of the ground and Timon names its powers one by one.

In the text

When Fortune in her shift and change of mood Spurns down her late beloved, all his dependants, Which laboured after him to the mountain’s top Even on their knees and hands, let him slip down, Not one accompanying his declining foot.

Poet, 1.1.91

The Poet summarises the next four acts before any of them have happened, then hands the manuscript to the man who will fall. ‘Let him slip down’ is the exact verb: nobody pushes, everyone simply stops holding on. There is no clean division here between corrupt flatterers and honest art, because the diagnosis is itself for sale.

If I want gold, steal but a beggar’s dog And give it Timon, why, the dog coins gold.

A Senator, 2.1.5

The senator has found the mechanism and states it as a recipe: worthless object in, gold out. His is the first voice to treat Timon’s bounty as a market and not a virtue, and the calculation ends with an order to fetch the bonds and fill in the dates. Athens does not set out to ruin Timon; it simply notices him.

Friendship

Timon’s word for the men at his table is brothers, and he tells them the gods provided friends so that he might have need of them. The play then makes him need them, three times in three scenes, and gives each refusal a different set of manners and the same answer.

Through the play ‘I am wealthy in my friends’ at the end of Act II is answered by Lucullus, Lucius and Sempronius in Act III, and again by the crowd who come back for a second dinner.

In the text

O you gods, what a number of men eat Timon, and he sees ’em not! It grieves me to see so many dip their meat in one man’s blood;

Apemantus, 1.2.40

In Apemantus’s line the dinner becomes cannibalism with one verb: the guests do not eat with Timon, they eat Timon. It is the play’s governing image of consumption and it is literally true of the estate, which the guests are working through course by course. He says it aloud, at the table, in front of everyone, and nothing whatever follows.

Ah, when the means are gone that buy this praise, The breath is gone whereof this praise is made. Feast-won, fast-lost

Flavius, 2.2.165

Praise is made of breath and paid for with dinners, so when the dinners stop the praise simply evaporates, and the metaphor is physical. ‘Feast-won, fast-lost’ compresses the whole of Act III into four words, and the two senses of ‘fast’, quickly and going without food, sit one on top of the other.

Loyal service

The servants keep the play honest. Flaminius throws Lucullus’s coins on the floor and curses the meat his master paid for, Flavius brings the accounts and is waved off twice in one scene, and the dismissed household leaves with the steward’s own money in their hands.

Through the play Flavius is interrupted in 1.2, heard at last in 2.2, shares out his wealth in 4.2, and is the single exception Timon allows himself in the wood.

In the text

His promises fly so beyond his state That what he speaks is all in debt; he owes For every word.

Flavius, 1.2.190

Flavius makes speech itself a form of borrowing. Timon’s generosity is verbal before it is financial (he gives away the bay courser because a guest praised it in passing), so every sentence draws on an account already empty. It is the most precise thing said about him in the play, and it is said to nobody at all.

Poor honest lord, brought low by his own heart, Undone by goodness! Strange, unusual blood When man’s worst sin is he does too much good!

Flavius, 4.2.38

This is the most generous reading of Timon in the play and it comes from the one man entitled to be bitter about him. ‘Blood’ here means temperament. Flavius does not claim the giving was wise, only that the fault in it was goodness, and the exclamation marks are the sound of a bookkeeper running out of categories.

Law, mercy and the senate

Alcibiades asks the senate to weigh a soldier’s service at Lacedaemon and Byzantium against one killing in hot blood, and is told that to bear is valour and to revenge is none. The same body lends at large interest, refuses Timon a loan, and at the end pleads that crimes are not inherited.

Through the play The senators freeze Flavius into silence in 2.2, condemn the soldier and banish Alcibiades in 3.5, and in 5.4 ask for exactly the discrimination they would not give.

In the text

Banish me? Banish your dotage, banish usury, That makes the Senate ugly.

Alcibiades, 3.5.101

He takes their verb and turns it three times, and the third turn names what is actually wrong with the room: the men refusing mercy are lending at large interest. It is the same charge the Timon plot brings against Athens, said out loud in the senate house, and it gets him thrown out of the city instead of answered.

Bring me into your city, And I will use the olive with my sword, Make war breed peace, make peace stint war, make each Prescribe to other, as each other’s leech.

Alcibiades, 5.4.83

The play ends with the one man who can tell one Athenian from another. ‘Leech’ means physician, so war and peace are set to treat each other, and the balanced clauses do on the page what the terms do in the city. It is a settlement and not a reconciliation: nobody is forgiven, and the cure is mutual restraint.

Ceremony

Timon says ceremony was devised to set a gloss on faint deeds and hollow welcomes, and then holds two banquets. The second uses the entire form of hospitality (invitation, music, grace, covered dishes, servants) to serve nothing but warm water.

Through the play The masque of Cupid and Amazons in 1.2 is an entertainment Timon pays for and then thanks his guests for; 3.6 repeats the same ceremony with warm water under the covers.

In the text

I should fear those that dance before me now Would one day stamp upon me. ’T has been done. Men shut their doors against a setting sun.

Apemantus, 1.2.133

The couplet has the shape of a proverb and the function of a prophecy: in 3.4 Timon finds his own doors shut against him from the inside. ‘Stamp’ keeps the dancing in view, so that the feet entertaining him now are the feet that will be on him, and the line is spoken over music nobody else stops hearing.

May you a better feast never behold, You knot of mouth-friends! Smoke and lukewarm water Is your perfection.

Timon, 3.6.71

‘Mouth-friends’ is the play’s best coinage: friends who exist at a table and no further than the mouth. Warm water is what a feast leaves when the food is taken out of it, the honest substance under the sauces, and ‘perfection’ means completion, the finished form of what they always were. He then throws it in their faces.

Exile

Timon leaves Athens with nothing but nakedness and asks the earth for a root, and the wood immediately fills up with the people he left behind. He has a cave, a spade, a stone to throw, and a grave he chooses himself at the edge of the sea.

Through the play The curse at the wall in 4.1 promises solitude; 4.3 brings a general, two women, a philosopher, three thieves and a steward; 5.1 brings the senators and the two artists.

In the text

Timon will to the woods, where he shall find Th’ unkindest beast more kinder than mankind.

Timon, 4.1.35

He speaks of himself in the third person, as though reading his own epitaph early. The play on ‘kind’, a species and kindness, carries the argument: the beast is kinder because it was never of his kind. Apemantus takes the comparison apart in 4.3 by showing that every beast in the list is prey to some other beast.

Why, I was writing of my epitaph; It will be seen tomorrow. My long sickness Of health and living now begins to mend And nothing brings me all things.

Timon, 5.1.184

Health and living are the disease and dying is the cure, and the inversion is held perfectly steady across three lines. ‘Nothing brings me all things’ is the last of his reversals and the calmest of them: he has stopped cursing and started arranging. It is also the only preparation the play gives for a death that happens offstage.

Warnings ignored

Everybody in the play can state its moral. The Poet has written it as an allegory, Apemantus says it at the table, Flavius works it out in the corner with the bills in his hand, and Timon hears all three and goes on giving until there is nothing left to give.

Through the play The Fortune speech of 1.1 forecasts the fall, the asides of 1.2 count what it will cost, the fall arrives in 3.4, and Timon concludes that mankind is vile, which is a larger conclusion than that he misjudged some men.

In the text

For by these Shall I try friends. You shall perceive how you Mistake my fortunes. I am wealthy in my friends.

Timon, 2.2.180

He has been told he is ruined and he hears an opportunity. ‘Try’ means test, and he is right that a test is coming; what he has wrong is which way it will go. The line is the turning point of the play, and the confidence in it is why the three refusals of Act III land as hard as they do.

The middle of humanity thou never knewest, but the extremity of both ends.

Apemantus, 4.3.311

The sharpest criticism of Timon in the play, and the reason it is hard to call him a tragic hero: there is no scene in which he learns anything, only a switch from one absolute to its opposite. Apemantus has earned the observation by occupying the same extreme for years and knowing exactly what it is worth.
