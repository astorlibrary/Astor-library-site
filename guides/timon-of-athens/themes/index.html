Timon of Athens · William Shakespeare

Themes

Money

Talents, solidares, crowns, bonds, interest, dowries, jewels and horses are counted out all through the play, and every relationship in it has a figure attached. The gold Timon finds in the wood does openly what his generosity did discreetly: it brings people to him.

In Act I gifts are a currency of love; Act III prices that love at fifty talents and finds it will not pay; in Act IV the metal is dug out of the ground and Timon names its powers one by one.

When Fortune in her shift and change of mood Spurns down her late beloved, all his dependants, Which laboured after him to the mountain's top Even on their knees and hands, let him slip down, Not one accompanying his declining foot.

Poet, 1.1.91

I gave it freely ever, and there's none Can truly say he gives if he receives.

Timon, 1.2.11

Friendship

Timon’s word for the men at his table is brothers, and he tells them the gods provided friends so that he might have need of them. The play then makes him need them, three times in three scenes, and gives each refusal a different set of manners and the same answer.

‘I am wealthy in my friends’ at the end of Act II is answered by Lucullus, Lucius and Sempronius in Act III, and again by the crowd who come back for a second dinner.

I gave it freely ever, and there's none Can truly say he gives if he receives.

Timon, 1.2.11

O you gods, what a number of men eat Timon, and he sees ’em not! It grieves me to see so many dip their meat in one man’s blood;

Apemantus, 1.2.40

Loyal service

The servants keep the play honest. Flaminius throws Lucullus’s coins on the floor and curses the meat his master paid for, Flavius brings the accounts and is waved off twice in one scene, and the dismissed household leaves with the steward’s own money in their hands.

Flavius is interrupted in 1.2, heard at last in 2.2, shares out his wealth in 4.2, and is the single exception Timon allows himself in the wood.

His promises fly so beyond his state That what he speaks is all in debt; he owes For every word.

Flavius, 1.2.190

Ah, when the means are gone that buy this praise, The breath is gone whereof this praise is made. Feast-won, fast-lost

Flavius, 2.2.165

Law, mercy and the senate

Alcibiades asks the senate to weigh a soldier’s service at Lacedaemon and Byzantium against one killing in hot blood, and is told that to bear is valour and to revenge is none. The same body lends at large interest, refuses Timon a loan, and at the end pleads that crimes are not inherited.

The senators freeze Flavius into silence in 2.2, condemn the soldier and banish Alcibiades in 3.5, and in 5.4 ask for exactly the discrimination they would not give.

But I perceive Men must learn now with pity to dispense, For policy sits above conscience.

First Stranger, 3.2.80

Banish me? Banish your dotage, banish usury, That makes the Senate ugly.

Alcibiades, 3.5.101

Ceremony

Timon says ceremony was devised to set a gloss on faint deeds and hollow welcomes, and then holds two banquets. The second uses the entire form of hospitality (invitation, music, grace, covered dishes, servants) to serve nothing but warm water.

The masque of Cupid and Amazons in 1.2 is an entertainment Timon pays for and then thanks his guests for; 3.6 repeats the same ceremony with warm water under the covers.

When Fortune in her shift and change of mood Spurns down her late beloved, all his dependants, Which laboured after him to the mountain's top Even on their knees and hands, let him slip down, Not one accompanying his declining foot.

Poet, 1.1.91

I should fear those that dance before me now Would one day stamp upon me. 'T has been done. Men shut their doors against a setting sun.

Apemantus, 1.2.133

Exile

Timon leaves Athens with nothing but nakedness and asks the earth for a root, and the wood immediately fills up with the people he left behind. He has a cave, a spade, a stone to throw, and a grave he chooses himself at the edge of the sea.

The curse at the wall in 4.1 promises solitude; 4.3 brings a general, two women, a philosopher, three thieves and a steward; 5.1 brings the senators and the two artists.

Knock me down with 'em! Cleave me to the girdle. … Cut my heart in sums! … Tell out my blood.

Timon, 3.4.89

Timon will to the woods, where he shall find Th' unkindest beast more kinder than mankind.

Timon, 4.1.35

Warnings ignored

Everybody in the play can state its moral. The Poet has written it as an allegory, Apemantus says it at the table, Flavius works it out in the corner with the bills in his hand, and Timon hears all three and goes on giving until there is nothing left to give.

The Fortune speech of 1.1 forecasts the fall, the asides of 1.2 count what it will cost, the fall arrives in 3.4, and Timon concludes that mankind is vile, which is a larger conclusion than that he misjudged some men.
