American Ghost Stories · F. Marion Crawford, Ambrose Bierce, Ellen Glasgow and others

Form and language

Journalistic style

Reporting the supernatural in the flat register of a local news item.

Bierce opens by calling the haunting well known and dismissing doubters as cranks. The mock-official tone makes the house’s reputation a fact before anything has happened in it.

Even the shock of death—for her brother Edward lay dead in the house—could not disturb her outward serenity of demeanor.

The Shadows on the Wall

Ghost narrator

Giving the story to a teller whose position no reader can check.

Dunbar’s narrator is dead, jealous and certain of her rights to the house. The story works because she gradually becomes less reliable to herself and more generous to everyone else.

It was intolerably unchanged, the dim, dark-toned room.

The Shell of Sense

Domestic detail

A household object that carries the story’s meaning.

A work-basket in disorder, gaps in a bookshelf, a shadow on a study wall, footprints in dust, a skipping-rope on the stairs. The evidence is usually something a housekeeper would notice first.

“That strange shadow on the wall,” replied Mrs. Brigham.

The Shadows on the Wall

Endings

Holding back the decisive fact until the very end.

Bierce’s final line names the dead woman by her missing toe, and everything before it rearranges. Freeman ends on a telegram and never says what Henry did.

“Henry had words with Edward in the study the night before Edward died,” said Caroline Glynn.

The Shadows on the Wall

Frame narrative

A story presented as told aloud to an audience within the fiction.

Crawford’s Upper Berth is told over cigars, after dinner, to men who have run out of conversation, which lets the teller admit how frightened he was.

American interiors

The old haunted-house form set in newer, plainer rooms.

Studies, sickrooms, farmhouse closets, a steamship state-room, a shack on the plains. The haunted house has moved into ordinary American rooms.
