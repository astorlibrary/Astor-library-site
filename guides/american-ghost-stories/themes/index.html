Proof

Bierce begins with testimony and ends with a footprint; Freeman’s Henry tests the shadow by moving every piece of furniture; Dunbar’s husband believes a message he cannot explain. Again and again the writers ask what a ghost story would need to convince anybody.

Through the book The early stories rely on witnesses and objects; by Dunbar and Wharton belief is private, and the proof is what people do after it.

In the text

“That strange shadow on the wall,” replied Mrs. Brigham.

The Shadows on the Wall

It enters the story as something to remark on, and Caroline will not turn round to look. Freeman lets the reader work out that the shadow is old news to everyone in the room but Emma.

It is well known that the old Manton house is haunted.

The Middle Toe of the Right Foot

Well known is the phrase of a man who has not checked. Bierce starts in the voice of local consensus so that the story’s end can show what the consensus missed.

Women writers

Seven of the ten writers are women, and several of their stories turn on women noticing what the men of the house deny: Glasgow’s nurse, Freeman’s sisters, Mrs Rutledge in Bewitched.

Through the book Crawford’s story is told among men after dinner; the later ones centre on a nurse, a young bride, three sisters and a dead wife.

In the text

my work-basket was in disorder; it was preposterous that so small a thing should hurt me so

The Shell of Sense

The jealousy of the whole story arrives through a sewing basket. Dunbar makes the smallest domestic change carry the largest loss, and lets the narrator be embarrassed by her own feeling.

Haunted houses

Nearly every story is about a building: a state-room, a study, a room that may be a closet, a house that is not there, houses moved through the streets. The ghosts belong to places more than to people.

Through the book The houses go from fixed and notorious in Bierce to unreliable in Wynne, Peattie and Dawson, until Dunbar’s narrator is the house’s memory of itself.

In the text

Even the shock of death—for her brother Edward lay dead in the house—could not disturb her outward serenity of demeanor.

The Shadows on the Wall

The dash drops the corpse into the middle of a sentence about composure. Freeman’s comedy of manners is the ghost story: the body in the house is a parenthesis to the sisters’ self-possession.

The evidence that the house is haunted is of two kinds; the testimony of disinterested witnesses who have had ocular proof, and that of the house itself.

The Middle Toe of the Right Foot

The house is called as a witness. The legal parody is funny, and it also tells you exactly how the story will be solved: by what the house kept, whatever anyone saw.

Guilt before a death

Several stories turn on something done shortly before a death and never admitted: a quarrel in a study, a duel arranged as a trick, a child’s death her mother blames on her husband.

Through the book No one is tried. The guilty die instead, and the stories leave open whether a ghost did it.

In the text

“Henry had words with Edward in the study the night before Edward died,” said Caroline Glynn.

The Shadows on the Wall

Freeman opens a murder story in the grammar of a family complaint. Had words is exactly what a sister would say, and it puts the crime, if there is one, inside the ordinary vocabulary of a household quarrel.

“The middle toe is missing—it was Gertrude!”

The Middle Toe of the Right Foot

The detail was planted on the hotel porch, where King admits he once threw over a girl who had lost a toe. It returns as identification. Bierce’s method in one line: the supernatural is proved by a physical oddity, and the reader supplies the rest.

What the dead leave behind

Dunbar’s narrator sees the unfilled gaps in her bookshelves and the disorder in her work-basket. The collection is unusually interested in the ordinary things a death leaves behind and who keeps them.

Through the book Grief begins as possession (this is my room) and ends, in “The Shell of Sense”, as release.

In the text

It was intolerably unchanged, the dim, dark-toned room.

The Shell of Sense

Intolerably is the word that tells you who is speaking: only someone who has left could find sameness unbearable. The ghost’s first feeling is a housekeeper’s discomfort at a room that has carried on without her.

the very gaps that I myself had left in my bookshelves still stood unfilled

The Shell of Sense

The dead notice absences. Dunbar gives her ghost an exact, bookish observation: the gaps are hers, and nobody has closed them. Grief is described from the side of the person grieved for.

Who tells the story

A clubman’s after-dinner yarn, a newspaper’s certainty, a nurse ready to swear on her deathbed, a dead woman’s first person. The form of the telling changes the ghost each time.

Through the book Dunbar’s story is told by the ghost herself, and in it the ghost does the kindest thing in the book.

In the text

I am freed.

The Shell of Sense

Three words, after pages of possessive watching. The living do not lay this ghost to rest. It gets out by letting them go, which reverses the usual direction of the form.

Theirs is the fullest joy that the dwellers in the shell of sense can know. Mine is the transcendent joy of the unseen spaces.

The Shell of Sense

The title phrase comes back. Halfway through, Frances used it in a line of verse for the living, pitying them; now it names the happiness she is leaving them to. Dunbar gives her ghost the last word and a larger joy than anyone left in the room.
