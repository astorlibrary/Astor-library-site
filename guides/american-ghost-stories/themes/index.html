American Ghost Stories · F. Marion Crawford, Ambrose Bierce, Ellen Glasgow and others

Themes

Proof

Bierce begins with testimony and ends with a footprint; Freeman’s Henry tests the shadow by moving every piece of furniture; Dunbar’s husband believes a message he cannot explain. Again and again the writers ask what a ghost story would need to convince anybody.

The early stories rely on witnesses and objects; by Dunbar and Wharton belief is private, and the proof is what people do after it.

“That strange shadow on the wall,” replied Mrs. Brigham.

The Shadows on the Wall

Not a line of its terrible outlines wavered.

The Shadows on the Wall

Women writers

Seven of the ten writers are women, and several of their stories turn on women noticing what the men of the house deny: Glasgow’s nurse, Freeman’s sisters, Mrs Rutledge in Bewitched.

Crawford’s story is told among men after dinner; the later ones centre on a nurse, a young bride, three sisters and a dead wife.

my work-basket was in disorder; it was preposterous that so small a thing should hurt me so

The Shell of Sense

Haunted houses

Nearly every story is about a building: a state-room, a study, a room that may be a closet, a house that is not there, houses moved through the streets. The ghosts belong to places more than to people.

The houses go from fixed and notorious in Bierce to unreliable in Wynne, Peattie and Dawson, until Dunbar’s narrator is the house’s memory of itself.

“Henry had words with Edward in the study the night before Edward died,” said Caroline Glynn.

The Shadows on the Wall

Even the shock of death—for her brother Edward lay dead in the house—could not disturb her outward serenity of demeanor.

The Shadows on the Wall

Guilt before a death

Several stories turn on something done shortly before a death and never admitted: a quarrel in a study, a duel arranged as a trick, a child’s death her mother blames on her husband.

No one is tried. The guilty die instead, and the stories leave open whether a ghost did it.

“Henry had words with Edward in the study the night before Edward died,” said Caroline Glynn.

The Shadows on the Wall

“The middle toe is missing—it was Gertrude!”

The Middle Toe of the Right Foot

What the dead leave behind

Dunbar’s narrator sees the unfilled gaps in her bookshelves and the disorder in her work-basket. The collection is unusually interested in the ordinary things a death leaves behind and who keeps them.

Grief begins as possession (this is my room) and ends, in “The Shell of Sense”, as release.

It was intolerably unchanged, the dim, dark-toned room.

The Shell of Sense

the very gaps that I myself had left in my bookshelves still stood unfilled

The Shell of Sense

Who tells the story

A clubman’s after-dinner yarn, a newspaper’s certainty, a nurse ready to swear on her deathbed, a dead woman’s first person. The form of the telling changes the ghost each time.

Dunbar’s story is told by the ghost herself, and in it the ghost does the kindest thing in the book.

Even the shock of death—for her brother Edward lay dead in the house—could not disturb her outward serenity of demeanor.

The Shadows on the Wall

It is well known that the old Manton house is haunted.
