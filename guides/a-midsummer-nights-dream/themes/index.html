A Midsummer Night's Dream · William Shakespeare

Themes

Love without reason

The play’s working definition of love is that it cannot be argued with and cannot be trusted: Helena says it looks with the mind, not the eyes, Bottom observes that reason and love keep little company, and a flower does the work of a lifetime’s devotion. The drug does nothing unnatural; it is only quicker.

Stated as theory in 1.1, demonstrated on the lovers in Acts II and III, and left standing at the end with Demetrius still under the influence.

Now, fair Hippolyta, our nuptial hour Draws on apace; four happy days bring in Another moon: but, O, methinks, how slow This old moon wanes!

Theseus, 1.1.1

Hippolyta, I woo'd thee with my sword, And won thy love, doing thee injuries; But I will wed thee in another key,

Theseus, 1.1.16

Law, fathers and consent

Athens opens with a father asking a duke to enforce his right to kill his daughter, and the duke agreeing that this is the law. Nothing in the play repeals it: in Act 4 Theseus simply overrules Egeus, which leaves the comedy’s happy ending resting on a ruler’s mood.

Egeus makes his demand in 1.1 and repeats it in 4.1; Theseus overrules him without argument, and Egeus is given nothing further to say.

Now, fair Hippolyta, our nuptial hour Draws on apace; four happy days bring in Another moon: but, O, methinks, how slow This old moon wanes!

Theseus, 1.1.1

I beg the ancient privilege of Athens, As she is mine, I may dispose of her: Which shall be either to this gentleman Or to her death, according to our law

Egeus, 1.1.41

Transformation

Everyone in the wood is altered and almost nobody notices at the time. The one visible change, Bottom’s head, is the least distressing of them; the play saves its fear for the invisible ones, like a man waking to loathe the woman he loved.

Titania and Lysander are drugged in 2.2, Bottom transformed in 3.1 and Demetrius drugged in 3.2; three of the four are undone by 4.1, and Demetrius is not.

And thorough this distemperature we see The seasons alter: hoary-headed frosts Fall in the fresh lap of the crimson rose;

Titania, 2.1.106

It fell upon a little western flower, Before milk-white, now purple with love's wound, And maidens call it love-in-idleness.

Oberon, 2.1.166

Night, sleep and the morning story

The play’s settlement depends on everybody agreeing to call the night a dream. Theseus produces the sceptical account, Hippolyta doubts it, Bottom cannot fit his experience into words, and Puck finally offers the whole audience the same bargain.

The lovers wake half-remembering in 4.1; Theseus explains it away in 5.1; the epilogue makes the explanation the audience’s responsibility.

And ere a man hath power to say 'Behold!' The jaws of darkness do devour it up: So quick bright things come to confusion.

Lysander, 1.1.147

I'll put a girdle round about the earth In forty minutes.

Puck, 2.1.175

Theatre about theatre

The mechanicals worry about the things real companies worry about (casting, props, whether the audience will be frightened, whether a boy can play a woman) and solve them by explaining everything aloud. Their failure demonstrates the conventions the surrounding play is relying on at that exact moment.

Rehearsal in 1.2 and 3.1, performance in 5.1, and an epilogue that begs the same indulgence the mechanicals begged for in their prologue.

Let me play the lion too: I will roar, that I will do any man's heart good to hear me;

Bottom, 1.2.58

Lord, what fools these mortals be!

Puck, 3.2.115

Who is allowed to decide

Fathers, dukes and fairy kings all get what they want, and each of them calls it correction. The play lets that pattern show by repeating it at three social levels within an hour of stage time.

Egeus invokes the law, Theseus invokes his sword and his wedding, Oberon invokes a flower; the changeling boy changes hands offstage and is never mentioned again.
