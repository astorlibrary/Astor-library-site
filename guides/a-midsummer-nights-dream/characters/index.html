A Midsummer Night's Dream · William Shakespeare

Characters

Theseus

Duke of Athens; first appears 1.1

He begins by upholding a law that would kill a girl for choosing the wrong husband and ends by overruling it without explanation, having changed nothing about his beliefs in between. His great speech dismisses lovers, lunatics and poets as the same kind of unreliable witness, which is spoken in a play that has just shown the audience fairies.

Hippolyta

Queen of the Amazons, betrothed to Theseus; first appears 1.1

She says very little and almost all of it disagrees with her future husband. When he explains away the lovers’ night as imagination, she points out that four people telling the same story is evidence of something, which is the only argument in the play for believing the audience’s own eyes.

Egeus

Hermia’s father; first appears 1.1

He appears twice, demanding the law against his daughter in the first scene and against her lover in the fourth, and is then simply overruled and forgotten. Shakespeare gives the opening crisis to a man with no inner life at all, so the law he invokes looks like machinery.

Hermia

Egeus’s daughter, in love with Lysander; first appears 1.1

Threatened with death in the first scene, she argues back in front of a duke and then leaves the city. In the wood she loses her certainty piece by piece (first her lover, then her oldest friend), and the play’s harshest comedy is about her height, which is the one thing she cannot answer.

Lysander

An Athenian in love with Hermia; first appears 1.1

He is Demetrius’s equal in everything except the father’s approval, which is the whole plot. Drugged, he turns on Hermia with a viciousness the spell does not really account for, and the play never gives him a line acknowledging what he said.

Helena

In love with Demetrius, who has abandoned her; first appears 1.1

She betrays her friend’s escape plan in order to be thanked by a man who despises her, and knows exactly how little that is worth while she does it. Her offer to be treated like a dog is the most uncomfortable speech in the comedy, and when both men suddenly want her she assumes it is a joke at her expense.

Demetrius

Egeus’s choice for Hermia; first appears 1.1

He courted Helena, dropped her, and threatens her with violence in the wood when she will not stop following him. He is the only one of the four never released from the drug: the comedy’s final pairing rests on an enchantment nobody on stage mentions again.

Oberon

King of the fairies; first appears 2.1

He wants a boy his wife is raising and drugs her into humiliation until she gives him up. The same night he also takes pity on a woman he has never met and tries to fix her love life, and the play makes no attempt to reconcile the two impulses in one character.

Titania

Queen of the fairies; first appears 2.1

Her account of the ruined seasons makes the fairy quarrel a matter of flooded fields and human disease, which is a larger claim than any other character makes for themselves. She keeps the changeling boy out of loyalty to his dead mother, and loses him while drugged into loving a weaver with an ass’s head.

Puck

Robin Goodfellow, Oberon’s servant; first appears 2.1

He comes from English village folklore, a long way from Athens, and he is an employee and a hazard at the same time: he does what he is told and enjoys what goes wrong. He is given the last word, and uses it to offer the audience a way out of everything they have seen.

Nick Bottom

A weaver, playing Pyramus; first appears 1.2

He wants to play every part in the play and is the only mortal who meets the fairies awake and is not frightened of them. He is also the only one who tries to describe the night afterwards, and the speech in which he fails (senses swapped over, words giving out) is the play’s most serious statement about what has happened to everyone.
