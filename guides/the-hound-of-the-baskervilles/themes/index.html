Superstition

A family legend is set against a detective who has hitherto confined his investigations to this world. Mortimer half believes in the hound; the peasants describe it with hell-fire; Sir Henry is shaken by its cry; Watson clings to his common sense. The legend is finally explained by a dog from a dealer in the Fulham Road and a paste of phosphorus, but the book gets much of its power from the fear it claims to dispel.

Through the book The manuscript of 1742; a realm in which detectives are helpless; the devil’s agents of flesh and blood; the cry on the moor; common sense; phosphorus; the family ghost laid.

In the text

standing over Hugo, and plucking at his throat, there stood a foul thing, a great, black beast, shaped like a hound, yet larger than any hound that ever mortal eye has rested upon.

Chapter II

In the language of an old family paper, all long piling clauses and archaic ceremony, the legend gives the book its monster. Shaped like a hound, yet larger than any hound is the phrase the whole book tests. Watson’s description of the real hound in chapter XIV echoes it.

He might have been the very spirit of that terrible place.

Chapter IX

Watson describes the mysterious watcher as a ghost, and the reader cannot tell whether he is an enemy or something supernatural. The figure is Holmes, and the moment is typical of the book: a Gothic image that turns out to have the most rational explanation of all.

Proof

Holmes knows early who the murderer is, and the problem is proving it. A death from fright leaves no mark; a hound that never bites cannot be linked to its master; no jury would believe the story. The case is built from small material facts, a boot, a cut newspaper, cigar ash, a smell of white jessamine, and finally from a trap in which Sir Henry is the bait.

Through the book The stick; the Times leader; two boots; the cigar ash; a photograph from York; it is not what we know, but what we can prove.

In the text

you are a conductor of light. Some people without possessing genius have a remarkable power of stimulating it.

Sherlock Holmes, Chapter I

Holmes’s compliment is backhanded, and it defines the partnership: Watson has no genius, but he stimulates it. His wrong guesses guide Holmes towards the truth, as Holmes explains a moment later. The phrase also describes Watson’s role in this book, carrying a light he does not generate himself.

Please, please, as you are a gentleman, burn this letter, and be at the gate by ten o’clock.

Laura Lyons, Chapter X

A charred scrap of paper is the missing link between Sir Charles and the gate. Its pleading tone, and its request to burn it, suggest scandal before crime. It was dictated by Stapleton, who used a woman’s trouble to draw his victim out at night.

Inheritance and the family curse

Everything turns on who inherits: a baronetcy, an entailed estate and a fortune of nearly a million from South Africa. The legend makes inheritance a curse passed down the generations for Hugo’s sin, and the real danger is an heir, the disgraced younger brother’s son, who wants the estate. Sir Henry means to restore the grandeur of his line; Stapleton means to take it.

Through the book The warning to his sons; seven hundred and forty thousand pounds; the entail; the Desmonds; the portrait; the three ways of claiming the property.

In the text

There is no devil in hell, Mr. Holmes, and there is no man upon earth who can prevent me from going to the home of my own people

Sir Henry Baskerville, Chapter IV

Sir Henry’s answer shows the fiery temper of the Baskervilles, which Watson notices immediately. His courage is real and so is his pride in family, the feeling the murderer counts on. The devil and the man are the two possible enemies weighed throughout the story.

The face of Stapleton had sprung out of the canvas.

Chapter XIII

With the portrait, the legend becomes evidence. By hiding the costume Holmes shows the face beneath, and the family resemblance supplies the motive the case lacked: Stapleton is a Baskerville with designs on the succession.

Heredity and the throwback

Like many books of its time, this one is interested in what the body inherits. Mortimer writes on atavism and reversion and reads skulls; Selden’s face looks like a prehistoric savage’s; Holmes sees Stapleton in Hugo’s portrait and calls him a throw-back, physical and spiritual. Doyle takes these ideas half seriously and half as comedy, as in Mortimer’s wish for a cast of Holmes’s skull.

Through the book Some Freaks of Atavism; the covetable skull; the Celt and the Ivernian; the evil yellow face; the throw-back in the portrait.

In the text

It is not my intention to be fulsome, but I confess that I covet your skull.

Dr James Mortimer, Chapter I

Mortimer’s enthusiasm for skulls is comic, and it belongs to the science of his day, which tried to read character and race in the shape of the head. The theme of inherited type will return seriously in the throw-back, but here Doyle lets Holmes answer with a cigarette.

A study of family portraits is enough to convert a man to the doctrine of reincarnation. The fellow is a Baskerville—that is evident.

Sherlock Holmes, Chapter XIII

Holmes jokes that family portraits could convert a man to reincarnation, then states the fact that matters: Stapleton is a Baskerville. A moment before, he has called the likeness a throw-back, physical and spiritual, borrowing the vocabulary of heredity, so that the curse of the Baskervilles becomes a bad strain returning in a descendant.

The moor

Dartmoor is the book’s great presence: vast, barren, granite-flecked, scattered with the huts of prehistoric men and hiding the Grimpen Mire, where a false step means death. It carries Watson out of modern England, shelters a convict and a detective, and at the end swallows the murderer. The moor is where the legend becomes believable.

Through the book The first sight of the moor; the mire and the pony; the stone huts; the man on the tor; the fog like a sea; for ever buried.

In the text

A false step yonder means death to man or beast.

Jack Stapleton, Chapter VII

Stapleton, the friendly naturalist, introduces the landscape’s great danger, and his own boast that he alone knows the paths. The irony is complete by the end: the mire hides his hound and, somewhere in the fog, swallows the man himself.

When you are once out upon its bosom you have left all traces of modern England behind you

Chapter VIII

Watson’s letters begin with atmosphere before facts, and he apologises for it. On the moor he feels its vastness and its grim charm, and he steps out of his own century among the huts and graves of forgotten people. That is the setting in which a legend can seem believable to a sensible man.

Disguise and false identity

Almost no one is who they seem. Stapleton wears a false beard in London, gives the cabman Holmes’s name, has been Vandeleur, and is really a Baskerville; his wife passes as his sister; Holmes hides as a tourist in a hut; Selden dies in Sir Henry’s clothes. Holmes says the first quality of a criminal investigator is to see through a disguise.

Through the book The black beard; You have been driving Mr. Sherlock Holmes; the sister who is a wife; the tourist in the tweed suit; the dead man in the ruddy tweed; the portrait.

In the text

As you value your life or your reason keep away from the moor.

Chapter IV

Holmes treats the warning as a puzzle and a clue about its sender: he traces the words to a Times leader and reasons that an educated person disguised the hand. Only late in the book do we learn that Beryl Stapleton sent it, the one act she dared against her husband.

My unhappy brother is starving on the moor. We cannot let him perish at our very gates.

Eliza Barrymore, Chapter IX

Barrymore’s secret, which made him the chief suspect, is an act of family loyalty. The revelation clears one mystery and adds a second fugitive to the moor. Mrs Barrymore’s love for a murderer is one of the book’s few purely human moments.

Women under a man’s control

The two women closest to the crime are both Stapleton’s instruments. His wife passes as his sister under threats and blows, and Laura Lyons writes the fatal letter because he has promised to marry her. Each knows or suspects part of the truth and is silenced by fear or hope. Mrs Barrymore, loyal to a murderous brother, completes a picture of women bound to dangerous men.

Through the book The warning on the path; the curious lisp; the letter signed L. L.; Prove it to me!; the weal across her neck; mind and soul tortured.

In the text

Go straight back to London, instantly.

Beryl Stapleton, Chapter VII

Beryl’s warning comes before any introduction and is the book’s clearest early hint that something is wrong at Merripit House. Beryl risks her husband’s anger to say it, then covers it with talk of orchids. Her mistaken identity is the first of many.

Evil indeed is the man who has not one woman to mourn him.

Chapter XIII

Watson’s moral is sentimental and generous. To the world Selden was half animal and half demon; to his sister he was a child who clung to her hand. The line softens the book’s treatment of its most brutal figure, and contrasts with the cold villain who will have no one to mourn him.
