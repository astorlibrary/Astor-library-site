The Hound of the Baskervilles · Arthur Conan Doyle

Form and language

The chain of inference

Reasoning from small observed details to confident conclusions.

Holmes opens the book by reading a walking-stick and correcting Watson’s reading of it, and continues with the Times leader, the hotel ink and the boots. Doyle then keeps Holmes off the stage for half the book, so that the reader must reason with Watson from partial evidence.

you are a conductor of light. Some people without possessing genius have a remarkable power of stimulating it.

Sherlock Holmes, Chapter I

Watson’s narration

A narrator telling their own earlier life, with the distance between the two selves doing the judging.

Watson writes after the event, and says his letters show his feelings and suspicions of the moment more accurately than his memory could. His admiration for Holmes, his sensitivity to the moor and his occasional wounded pride shape what the reader feels.

It is a lovely evening, my dear Watson ... I really think that you will be more comfortable outside than in.

Sherlock Holmes, Chapter XI

Letters

A story told partly through letters or documents written by the characters.

For three chapters the narrative becomes Watson’s reports to Holmes and extracts from his diary. The switch gives immediacy and uncertainty, since the letters were written without knowing the outcome, and it prepares the surprise that Holmes has been reading them in a hut a few miles away.

When you are once out upon its bosom you have left all traces of modern England behind you

Chapter VIII

The Gothic explained

The stock furniture of Gothic writing put to a particular use.

A cursed family, an ancestral portrait, a gloomy hall with a minstrel’s gallery, a woman sobbing in the night, candles at windows, a spectral hound: Doyle uses the whole Gothic inheritance, and then gives each item a natural cause.

standing over Hugo, and plucking at his throat, there stood a foul thing, a great, black beast, shaped like a hound, yet larger than any hound that ever mortal eye has rested upon.

Chapter II

Misdirection

Leading the reader towards a false suspect or explanation.

Suspicion falls in turn on the black-bearded Barrymore, the sobbing housekeeper, the mysterious man on the tor and Frankland’s telescope; each is explained, and the friendly naturalist who explains the moor is the murderer.

It is so vast, and so barren, and so mysterious.

Jack Stapleton, Chapter VII

The material clue

A physical object on which the solution turns.

The boots are the key clue: a new one stolen and returned, an old one stolen in its place, which tells Holmes that the enemy needs a scent for a real dog. The cut-out Times leader, the cigar ash and a cigarette stub marked Bradley do similar work.

Mr. Holmes, they were the footprints of a gigantic hound!

Dr James Mortimer, Chapter II

Landscape and weather

Place and weather made to press on the people living in them.

Doyle times the weather to the story: sunlight and red earth at the arrival, rain and fog in Watson’s diary, moonlight for the man on the tor, and a creeping white fog that nearly wrecks Holmes’s plan at the climax.

A false step yonder means death to man or beast.

Jack Stapleton, Chapter VII

The legend as prophecy

A statement about the future that shapes the action by being believed.

The manuscript warns the family to avoid the moor in the dark hours when the powers of evil are exalted, and Holmes and Sir Henry both repeat the phrase. The legend works as a prophecy because the murderer makes it come true.

standing over Hugo, and plucking at his throat, there stood a foul thing, a great, black beast, shaped like a hound, yet larger than any hound that ever mortal eye has rested upon.

Chapter II
