The Hound of the Baskervilles · Arthur Conan Doyle

Characters

Sherlock Holmes

Consulting detective, who works the case in secret from a hut on the moor; first appears Chapter I

Holmes treats the legend as a fairy tale and the footmark as material. He sends Watson to Devon, pretends to be busy with a blackmail case in London, and lives in a prehistoric hut, fed by the boy Cartwright, to watch without being watched. He is outwitted in London by a man who gives the cabman his name, and he admits that he nearly lost his client by waiting to make his case complete.

Dr John Watson

Narrator, sent to Dartmoor to guard Sir Henry and report; first appears Chapter I

Watson does most of the detective work in this book. He follows Barrymore at night, questions Laura Lyons, tracks the boy to the hut and waits there with his revolver. His letters are full of the moor’s atmosphere and of his own suspicions, some wrong, and he insists on common sense against the peasants’ fiend dog. He is hurt when he learns that Holmes has been on the moor all along, reading his reports.

Sir Henry Baskerville

The heir, a young baronet raised in Canada and the United States; first appears Chapter IV

Small, sturdy, dark-eyed and about thirty, he has the fiery temper of the Baskervilles and the manners of the American West. He refuses to be frightened away from the home of his own people, plans electric lamps for the drive, and falls quickly in love with Miss Stapleton. He does as Holmes tells him and walks home across the moor, not knowing he is the bait, and survives the hound only to suffer a breakdown; he and Mortimer leave on a voyage round the world.

Jack Stapleton

A naturalist at Merripit House; in fact Rodger Baskerville, next in line after Sir Henry; first appears Chapter VII (in person); Chapter IV (in disguise)

Small, prim, flaxen-haired, with a butterfly-net and a tin box for specimens, he is a recognised authority on moths and one of the few people who know the paths through the Grimpen Mire. He is also the son of Sir Charles’s disgraced younger brother, an embezzler and a former schoolmaster under another name, who passes his wife off as his sister, murders Sir Charles with a painted hound and plans to kill Sir Henry the same way. He disappears into the mire in the fog.

Beryl Stapleton

Passed off as Stapleton’s sister; in fact his wife, born Beryl Garcia in Costa Rica; first appears Chapter VII

Tall, dark and elegant, with a curious lisp, she is darker than any brunette Watson has seen in England. Afraid of her husband, who beats her, she warns Watson, whom she takes for Sir Henry, to go back to London, and she composes the warning letter from words cut out of The Times. When she learns of the plot against Sir Henry and of her husband’s affair, she turns against him; he ties her up, and afterwards she leads Holmes to his path through the mire.

Dr James Mortimer

Country doctor at Grimpen and executor of Sir Charles’s will; first appears Chapter I

Tall, thin and absent-minded, a former house-surgeon at Charing Cross who gave up a London career to marry, he writes on atavism and covets Holmes’s skull. He brings the case to Baker Street, half believing in the hound, and withheld the footprints from the inquest because a man of science shrinks from seeming to endorse a superstition. He loses his curly-haired spaniel to the hound.

John Barrymore

Butler at Baskerville Hall; first appears Chapter VI

Tall, handsome and pale, with a square black beard, he is the first suspect: he found Sir Charles’s body, benefits from the will, and walks the corridors at night. His secret turns out to be loyalty to his wife, who is feeding her brother, the escaped convict. In gratitude for Sir Henry’s silence he reveals the letter signed L. L., and Sir Henry’s old clothes, passed to Selden, cost the convict his life.

Eliza Barrymore

Housekeeper at the Hall, and the convict’s sister; first appears Chapter VI

A heavy, solid, intensely respectable woman whom Watson hears sobbing in the night. Her maiden name was Selden, and to her the Notting Hill murderer is still the curly-headed little brother she nursed. She takes the blame for the signals and weeps bitterly when he dies.

Selden

The Notting Hill murderer, escaped from Princetown; first appears Chapter VI (named); Chapter IX

A convict whose death sentence was commuted because of doubts about his sanity, he hides on the moor, fed by his sister and her husband. Watson sees his evil yellow face over a rock like one of the old savages of the burrows. Wearing Sir Henry’s cast-off clothes, he is hunted down by the hound and dies falling over a cliff.

Laura Lyons

Frankland’s daughter, a typist in Coombe Tracey; first appears Chapter X (named); Chapter XI

Deserted by the artist she married against her father’s wishes, and helped into a typewriting business by Sir Charles, Stapleton and Mortimer, she hopes to divorce her husband and marry Stapleton. At his dictation she writes asking Sir Charles to meet her at the gate at ten, and he then stops her from going. Shown proof that he is married, she tells Holmes everything.

Mr Frankland

A litigious old man of Lafter Hall; first appears Chapter VIII

Red-faced, white-haired and choleric, he spends his fortune on lawsuits for the pleasure of fighting and is carried in triumph or burned in effigy by the villagers of Fernworthy. He has disowned his daughter. From his roof he sweeps the moor with a telescope, and his spite against the police leads Watson to the boy and the hut.

Sir Charles Baskerville

The late baronet, found dead in the yew alley; first appears Chapter II (before the story opens)

A widower who made a fortune in South Africa and came home to restore the Hall and help the countryside. He took the family legend so seriously that he would not cross the moor at night, and his heart was weak. Lured to the moor-gate by a letter, he saw the painted hound and ran down the alley until he fell dead.

Hugo Baskerville

The wicked ancestor of the legend; first appears Chapter II (in the legend)

In the time of the Great Rebellion he abducted a yeoman’s daughter, vowed his body and soul to the Powers of Evil to catch her when she escaped, and was found on the moor with his throat torn out by a black beast shaped like a hound. His portrait in the dining-hall, dated 1647, shows a prim, hard, stern face.

Lestrade

Detective from Scotland Yard; first appears Chapter XIII

A small, wiry bulldog of a man who arrives on the London express with an unsigned warrant, and who now looks at Holmes with reverence. When the hound breaks out of the fog he yells with terror and throws himself on the ground.
