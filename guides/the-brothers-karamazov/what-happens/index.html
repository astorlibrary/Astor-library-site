The Brothers Karamazov · Fyodor Dostoevsky

What happens

Book I The History of a Family

The narrator, a townsman writing thirteen years after the events, introduces the family. Fyodor Pavlovitch Karamazov, a buffoon and a sensualist who began with next to nothing and died with a hundred thousand roubles, married twice and forgot all three of his sons. Dmitri, the son of the first marriage, was looked after by the servant Grigory and then by relations, served in the army, and has come back to settle accounts over his mother’s property. Ivan and Alexey, sons of the second wife, a meek orphan who suffered from hysterical fits, were educated at the expense of a stranger. Ivan has become a clever young writer whose article on the church courts has puzzled both sides; Alyosha, the youngest, is twenty and a novice at the monastery near the town, drawn there by the elder Zossima.

Book I, chapter 1
A landowner who hardly lived on his land, an elopement, and a first wife who ran away.
Book I, chapter 2
Three-year-old Mitya forgotten in the servants’ cottage, and taken up by his mother’s cousin Miüsov.
Book I, chapter 3
A meek orphan bride, two more sons, and a general’s widow who carries them off in a rug.
Book I, chapters 4–5
The narrator’s hero, a novice of twenty, and what an elder is.

Book II An Unfortunate Gathering

To settle the quarrel over Dmitri’s money the family meets in Father Zossima’s cell, with Miüsov and his young relation Kalganov. Fyodor Pavlovitch plays the buffoon, and the elder tells him above all not to lie to himself. Between the sessions Zossima blesses the peasant women who have come to him, among them a mother grieving for her little son Alexey, dead before he was three, and tells the rich widow Madame Hohlakov that love in action is harsh and dreadful beside love in dreams. The talk turns to Ivan’s argument that without belief in immortality everything would be lawful. When Dmitri arrives late and quarrels with his father over Grushenka, the elder bows down to the ground at his feet, and the young seminarist Rakitin tells Alyosha that the old man has sniffed a crime.

Book II, chapter 2
Fyodor Pavlovitch performs, and the elder tells him not to lie to himself.
Book II, chapters 3–4
A mother who has lost her son, and Madame Hohlakov’s dream of loving humanity.
Book II, chapter 5
Ivan’s article on the church courts, and his claim that without immortality everything is lawful.
Book II, chapters 6–8
Dmitri against his father, the elder’s bow, Rakitin’s theory, and a scandal at the Father Superior’s dinner.

Book III The Sensualists

The narrator explains how Smerdyakov, the cook, was born to Lizaveta, a homeless holy fool whom Fyodor Pavlovitch was rumoured to have seduced, and was brought up by Grigory and his wife. In a summer-house Dmitri makes Alyosha the confession of a passionate heart: how Katerina Ivanovna came to his lodging to save her father’s honour, and he handed her five thousand roubles and bowed; how they became engaged; and how he has squandered the three thousand roubles she asked him to post to Moscow on a spree with Grushenka. Over the brandy the father asks his sons whether there is a God, and that evening Dmitri bursts in looking for Grushenka, knocks the old man down and kicks him in the face. Alyosha then watches Grushenka humiliate Katerina Ivanovna.

Book III, chapters 1–2
Grigory, his six-fingered baby, and Lizaveta’s child born in the bath-house.
Book III, chapters 3–5
Schiller, beauty and Sodom, the five thousand roubles, and the three thousand spent at Mokroe.
Book III, chapters 6–8
The cook’s argument about faith, and the father’s question whether there is a God.
Book III, chapters 9–11
Dmitri’s attack on his father, and Grushenka’s refusal to kiss Katerina Ivanovna’s hand.

Book IV Lacerations

Father Ferapont, an ascetic who hates the elders, sees devils everywhere in the monastery. Out in the town Alyosha meets schoolboys throwing stones at a sickly boy of about nine, who bites his finger to the bone. At the Hohlakovs’ Katerina Ivanovna declares that she will be a god to whom Dmitri can pray, and Ivan, who loves her, says he is leaving. Alyosha takes two hundred roubles from her to Captain Snegiryov, the boy’s father, whom Dmitri had dragged out of a tavern by his beard in front of his son Ilusha. The captain, overjoyed, dreams aloud of what the money will do for his family, then crumples the notes and stamps them into the sand.

Book IV, chapter 1
A monk who eats little but bread and quarrels with the elders.
Book IV, chapter 3
Stones across a ditch, and a bitten finger.
Book IV, chapter 5
Katerina Ivanovna’s resolve, and Ivan’s departure.
Book IV, chapters 6–7
The captain’s family, the wisp of tow, and two hundred roubles trampled in the sand.

Book V Pro and Contra

Lise Hohlakov, a girl of fourteen who cannot walk, has written Alyosha a love letter, and they agree to marry one day. Alyosha overhears Smerdyakov with his guitar telling a neighbour’s daughter that he hates Russia, then finds Ivan in the Metropolis tavern, and the brothers talk properly for the first time. Ivan loves life and the sticky little leaves of spring, but cannot accept a world in which children are tortured, and gives back his ticket. He then tells the poem he has composed, The Grand Inquisitor, in which Christ returns to Seville in the time of the Inquisition and an old cardinal imprisons him and explains why the Church has corrected his work. At home Smerdyakov hints that Ivan had better be away, and as Ivan drives off next morning he says that it is always worth while speaking to a clever man.

Book V, chapter 1
Lise’s letter, and a promise to marry when she can walk.
Book V, chapters 3–4
Sticky little leaves, tortured children, and a ticket returned.
Book V, chapter 5
Christ in Seville, the three temptations, and a kiss.
Book V, chapters 6–7
Smerdyakov’s hints about a fit and the envelope, and Ivan’s journey.

Book VI The Russian Monk

On the last evening of his life Father Zossima talks to his friends, and Alyosha later wrote down what he said. The notes tell how Zossima’s elder brother Markel died at seventeen, telling their mother that everyone is responsible to all men for all; how Zossima, a young officer, struck his servant the night before a duel, then let his opponent fire, threw away his own pistol and asked forgiveness, and left the army for the monastery; and how a respected man of the town confessed to him a murder committed fourteen years before. The conversations and exhortations that follow speak of prayer, of the Russian people, of loving all God’s creation, and of hell as the suffering of being unable to love. The elder dies that night.

Book VI, chapter 1
The elder’s last evening, and why Alyosha’s face reminds him of his brother.
Book VI, chapter 2
Markel, the blows to the servant Afanasy, a pistol thrown into the wood, and the mysterious visitor.
Book VI, chapter 3
The Russian monk, masters and servants, prayer and love, and hell.

Book VII Alyosha

The monks and townspeople expect a miracle from the elder’s body; instead, before three in the afternoon, it begins to smell, and Father Ferapont and the elder’s enemies triumph. Shaken, Alyosha lets Rakitin take him to Grushenka, who has promised Rakitin twenty-five roubles for bringing him. She is waiting for word from the Polish officer who seduced her five years before, and meaning at first to seduce Alyosha she learns that Zossima has died; Alyosha calls her a true sister, and she tells the fable of the onion. Back at the coffin, while Father Païssy reads the Gospel of the marriage at Cana, Alyosha dreams that Zossima is at the feast, then goes out, falls on the earth and kisses it.

Book VII, chapter 1
A body that does not stay sweet, and Father Ferapont’s triumph.
Book VII, chapter 2
Alyosha’s rebellion against the injustice of it, and Rakitin’s sausage and vodka.
Book VII, chapter 3
Grushenka on Alyosha’s knee, the news of the elder’s death, and the fable.
Book VII, chapter 4
The first miracle, the elder at the feast, and the starry sky over the monastery.

Book VIII Mitya

Desperate to repay Katerina Ivanovna before he goes to Grushenka, Dmitri spends a day trying to raise three thousand roubles: from the merchant Samsonov, who sends him on a fool’s errand to a timber-dealer called Lyagavy, and from Madame Hohlakov, who recommends the gold-mines. Believing that Grushenka has gone to his father, he takes a brass pestle, climbs into Fyodor Pavlovitch’s garden and watches him at the window; running away, he strikes down Grigory on the fence. Learning that Grushenka has gone to Mokroe to meet her first lover, he buys wine and food with a bundle of notes and drives there, meaning to see her once more and shoot himself at dawn. The Pole turns out to be a petty cheat, Grushenka says it is Dmitri she loves, and in the early morning the police arrive.

Book VIII, chapters 1–3
A day of borrowing that raises nothing.
Book VIII, chapter 4
The garden, the lighted window, the pestle, and Grigory on the fence.
Book VIII, chapters 5–6
Pistols redeemed, champagne ordered, and a driver told that one must make way.
Book VIII, chapters 7–8
Cards with the Poles at Mokroe, a night of revelry, and the arrival of the police.

Book IX The Preliminary Investigation

Fyodor Pavlovitch has been found murdered, and the envelope that held the three thousand roubles lies torn open and empty on the floor. At Mokroe the police captain, the prosecutor Ippolit Kirillovitch and the investigating lawyer Nikolay Parfenovitch question Dmitri through the night. He admits that he hated his father and meant perhaps to kill him, but swears he did not. At last, as his great secret, he tells them that the money he spent came from a rag sewn round his neck: fifteen hundred roubles kept back from Katerina Ivanovna’s three thousand, which made him, he says, a scoundrel but not a thief. Nobody believes him. Dozing, he dreams of a starving baby in a burnt-out village, and wakes to find that someone has put a pillow under his head; then he is taken away to prison.

Book IX, chapters 1–2
Bloodstained hands, a borrowed pistol, and the news from Fyodor Pavlovitch’s house.
Book IX, chapters 3–6
Dmitri’s story told, doubted and pressed by the prosecutor.
Book IX, chapter 7
The fifteen hundred roubles in the rag, received with hisses.
Book IX, chapters 8–9
Witnesses, a dream of a burnt village, and the carts that take Mitya away.

Book X The Boys

Two months later the story turns to the schoolboys. Kolya Krassotkin, thirteen, clever and proud of calling himself a socialist, once lay between the rails while a train passed over him to win a bet. He was Ilusha’s protector until they quarrelled over the dog Zhutchka, which Ilusha, taught by Smerdyakov, had fed a piece of bread with a pin in it. Now Ilusha is dying, and Alyosha has reconciled him with the other boys. Kolya at last visits the sick boy with a dog called Perezvon, who turns out to be Zhutchka. A famous doctor from Moscow says there is no hope, and Kolya, who had meant to be superior, runs out into the passage in tears.

Book X, chapters 1–3
The train, the widow’s son, and the little children he minds.
Book X, chapter 4
The pin in the bread, and why Kolya stayed away.
Book X, chapters 5–6
Perezvon’s tricks, a cannon, and Kolya showing off to Alyosha.
Book X, chapter 7
The doctor from Moscow, and a father who cannot bear it.

Book XI Ivan

On the day before the trial Alyosha visits Grushenka, ill and jealous of Katerina Ivanovna; Lise, who has broken off their engagement, tells him her cruel fantasies and crushes her own finger in the door; and Dmitri, in prison, tells him that a new man has risen up in him and that he means to sing a hymn to God underground. That evening Alyosha tells Ivan that it was not he who killed their father. Ivan has been to Smerdyakov twice, and now goes a third time: Smerdyakov takes three thousand roubles out of his stocking, tells how he killed Fyodor Pavlovitch with an iron paper-weight, and says that Ivan, who taught him that everything is lawful, is the real murderer. At home Ivan is visited by the devil, a shabby gentleman who talks like a poor relation, until Alyosha knocks with the news that Smerdyakov has hanged himself.

Book XI, chapters 1–3
Madame Hohlakov’s news, and Lise’s pineapple compote.
Book XI, chapters 4–5
Dmitri’s new man, and Alyosha’s words to Ivan under the lamp-post.
Book XI, chapters 6–8
The fit on the cellar stairs, the apple-tree, and the money in the stocking.
Book XI, chapters 9–10
A visitor in check trousers, and a knock at the window.

Book XII A Judicial Error

The trial draws interest from all over Russia. The witnesses tell against Dmitri, the doctors disagree about his sanity, and old Dr Herzenstube remembers giving him a pound of nuts when he was a neglected child. Ivan, delirious, produces the three thousand roubles and says that Smerdyakov killed their father and he incited him; to save Ivan, Katerina Ivanovna hands the court the drunken letter in which Dmitri swore to kill his father. The prosecutor makes the speech of his life about the Karamazov family and the galloping troika of Russia, and the defending counsel from Petersburg, Fetyukovitch, argues that there was no robbery and no murder, and that such a father is no father. The jury of peasants and clerks answers yes, guilty, to every question.

Book XII, chapters 1–4
Grigory, Rakitin, the doctors, and a pound of nuts.
Book XII, chapter 5
Ivan’s evidence, and Katerina Ivanovna’s letter.
Book XII, chapters 6–9
Sketches of character, a treatise on Smerdyakov, and the galloping troika.
Book XII, chapters 10–14
No money, no robbery, no murder, a corrupter of thought, and peasants who stand firm.

Epilogue The escape plan and the stone

Five days after the trial Ivan lies in a fever at Katerina Ivanovna’s, and she and Alyosha talk over the plan he has made for Dmitri to escape at the third halting-place on the road to Siberia and go to America with Grushenka. Katerina Ivanovna visits Dmitri in the prison hospital, and for a moment, as the chapter title says, the lie becomes truth: they tell each other they will love each other all their lives. Then comes Ilusha’s funeral. By the big stone where Ilusha wanted to be buried, Alyosha asks the boys to remember this day and one another all their lives, since a good memory from childhood may save a man, and the boys cheer for Karamazov.

Epilogue, chapter 1
Ivan’s scheme, and Katerina Ivanovna’s jealousy of Grushenka.
Epilogue, chapter 2
Katya at the prison hospital.
Epilogue, chapter 3
Bread for the sparrows, the boys’ promise, and pancakes at the funeral dinner.
