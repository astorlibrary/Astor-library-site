The Brothers Karamazov · Fyodor Dostoevsky

Form and language

The town chronicler

A narrator telling past events from a later point, with the distance between then and now shaping the telling.

The story is told thirteen years later by an unnamed townsman who knew the family, heard the gossip and attended the trial. He calls Alyosha his hero, admits what he does not know, apologises for his digressions, and only in Book XI reveals the name of the town. His modest, chatty voice makes the extremes of the characters seem more real.

He was simply an early lover of humanity, and that he adopted the monastic life was simply because at that time it struck him, so to say, as the ideal escape for his soul struggling from the darkness of worldly wickedness to the light of love.

Book I, chapter 4

Argument in dialogue

Ideas worked out in conversation between characters, each speaking with a voice of their own.

Most of the great ideas arrive in talk: the elder’s cell, the brandy at dinner, the brothers in the tavern, Ivan with Smerdyakov and then with the devil. Dostoevsky lets opponents speak at full strength, so that Ivan’s case against God is as powerful as anything said against it.

love in action is a harsh and dreadful thing compared with love in dreams. Love in dreams is greedy for immediate action, rapidly performed and in the sight of all.

Father Zossima, Book II, chapter 4

Inset stories

Complete stories or texts set inside the main narrative and told by characters.

The novel contains a poem in prose, The Grand Inquisitor; a saint’s life, Alyosha’s notes of Father Zossima; the fable of the onion; Ivan’s collection of true stories about children; and Mitya’s confession in verse, anecdote and heels up. Each inset works as a parable that the main plot then tests.

Didst Thou forget that man prefers peace, and even death, to freedom of choice in the knowledge of good and evil? Nothing is more seductive for man than his freedom of conscience, but nothing is a greater cause of suffering.

The Grand Inquisitor, Book V, chapter 5

Doubles

Characters who mirror each other, so that one seems to act out what the other thinks.

Smerdyakov carries out Ivan’s idea, and the devil speaks Ivan’s worst thoughts in a shabby gentleman’s voice. Dmitri is his father’s rival in appetite; Alyosha repeats Christ’s silent kiss from the Grand Inquisitor. Ivan sees himself in each double with horror.

Alyosha got up, went to him and softly kissed him on the lips. “That’s plagiarism,” cried Ivan, highly delighted. “You stole that from my poem.

Book V, chapter 5

Symbolic objects

An object that comes back again and again until it stands for the whole story.

The plot turns on things: the envelope addressed to Grushenka, the brass pestle, the rag with the fifteen hundred roubles, the paper-weight, the notes in Smerdyakov’s stocking. Other objects carry the moral argument, like the onion in Grushenka’s fable, the bread crumbled for the sparrows and the stone where Alyosha speaks.

On the envelope is written: ‘To my angel, Grushenka, when she will come to me.’

Dmitri Karamazov, Book III, chapter 5

Irony

Saying one thing in a form that invites the opposite judgement.

Chapter titles are often ironic: The Prosecutor Catches Mitya, Fortune Smiles on Mitya, An Argument That Cuts Both Ways, The Peasants Stand Firm. The trial reaches a wrong verdict by careful argument, and the book calls itself A Judicial Error.

It’s a true saying then, that ‘it’s always worth while speaking to a clever man,’

Smerdyakov, Book V, chapter 7

Allusion

A reference to another text or story, which the reader is expected to recognise.

Scripture runs through the book, from Job, which Zossima loved as a child, to the marriage at Cana read over his coffin. Dmitri quotes Schiller, Ivan’s poem retells the temptations in the wilderness, and the prosecutor borrows Gogol’s troika to describe Russia.

There are three powers, three powers alone, able to conquer and to hold captive for ever the conscience of these impotent rebels for their happiness—those forces are miracle, mystery and authority.

The Grand Inquisitor, Book V, chapter 5

Dreams and visions

Dreams and hallucinations that show a character what he cannot yet say.

At three turning points a character dreams: Alyosha sees Zossima at the feast at Cana, Dmitri dreams of the starving babe in a burnt village, and Ivan receives the devil in a nightmare. Each vision changes the dreamer.

He had fallen on the earth a weak boy, but he rose up a resolute champion, and he knew and felt it suddenly at the very moment of his ecstasy.

Book VII, chapter 4

Courtroom rhetoric

Speeches in court designed to persuade a jury, studied for how they argue.

Book XII gives the two closing speeches almost in full. The prosecutor builds a psychological portrait and a picture of Russia; the defence answers him point by point, then turns to emotion, and finally to the claim that such a father is no father. Dostoevsky shows how both clever speeches miss the truth the reader knows.

For if the troika were drawn by his heroes, Sobakevitch, Nozdryov, Tchitchikov, it could reach no rational goal, whoever might be driving it.

Book XII, chapter 6

Foreshadowing

Hints early in a story that prepare for later events.

From its first sentence the narrator promises to describe the father’s death in its proper place. Zossima bows to Dmitri as to future suffering, Rakitin predicts a crime, Ivan says one reptile will devour the other, and Smerdyakov foresees his own fit.

An elder was one who took your soul, your will, into his soul and his will. When you choose an elder, you renounce your own will and yield it to him in complete submission, complete self-abnegation.

Book I, chapter 5

Confession

A character laying bare his own guilt or desire at length to a listener.

Almost everyone confesses: Dmitri to Alyosha in the summer-house, Zossima’s mysterious visitor to him, Smerdyakov to Ivan, Ivan to the court, Lise to Alyosha. Confession is a relief and also a performance, and the elder’s rule is the test: above all, do not lie to yourself.

Above all, don’t lie to yourself. The man who lies to himself and listens to his own lie comes to such a pass that he cannot distinguish the truth within him, or around him, and so loses all respect for himself and for others.

Father Zossima, Book II, chapter 2
