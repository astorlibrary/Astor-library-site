The Brothers Karamazov · Fyodor Dostoevsky

Themes

Faith and doubt

Whether there is a God, and what follows if there is not, is the question at the centre of the book. Fyodor Pavlovitch asks his sons over the brandy; Ivan accepts God but returns his ticket to God’s world; Smerdyakov and the devil draw the conclusion that everything is lawful. Against them stand Zossima’s teaching, Alyosha’s shaken and restored faith, and Dmitri’s hymn from underground. Dostoevsky meant Book VI, The Russian Monk, as the answer to Ivan, given, he said, as an artistic picture and not point by point.

Is there a God, or not?; I most respectfully return Him the ticket; What is hell?; a glorious hymn to God; I am x in an indeterminate equation.

He was simply an early lover of humanity, and that he adopted the monastic life was simply because at that time it struck him, so to say, as the ideal escape for his soul struggling from the darkness of worldly wickedness to the light of love.

Book I, chapter 4

Above all, don’t lie to yourself. The man who lies to himself and listens to his own lie comes to such a pass that he cannot distinguish the truth within him, or around him, and so loses all respect for himself and for others.

Father Zossima, Book II, chapter 2

Freedom

Ivan’s Grand Inquisitor argues that men cannot bear the freedom Christ gave them and will gladly hand it over for bread, miracle and authority. Dostoevsky tests the claim everywhere: in the elder to whom a monk surrenders his will in order to find freedom, in Dmitri’s longing to be free of Katerina Ivanovna’s money, and in Ivan’s freedom from all law, which ends in delirium.

An elder was one who took your soul, your will; freedom of choice in the knowledge of good and evil; miracle, mystery and authority; everything is lawful.

An elder was one who took your soul, your will, into his soul and his will. When you choose an elder, you renounce your own will and yield it to him in complete submission, complete self-abnegation.

Book I, chapter 5

Moreover, nothing then would be immoral, everything would be lawful, even cannibalism.

Book II, chapter 6

Guilt and responsibility

One man strikes the blow, but the novel spreads the guilt. Smerdyakov kills; Ivan taught him that everything is lawful and left the house; Dmitri wished his father dead and nearly killed him. Zossima’s teaching, that each of us is responsible to all for all, answers the question of who is guilty with a claim on everyone. Dmitri accepts his punishment for the thought he did not act on.

Each is responsible to all for all; I only know one thing, it wasn’t you killed father; you are the real murderer; I accept my punishment, not because I killed him.

The elder distinctly and deliberately bowed down at Dmitri’s feet till his forehead touched the floor.

Book II, chapter 6

One reptile will devour the other. And serve them both right, too.

Ivan Karamazov, Book III, chapter 9

Fathers and sons

A father who forgot his children is killed, and every son is implicated. Beside the Karamazovs Dostoevsky sets other fathers and sons: Captain Snegiryov and Ilusha, whose love survives humiliation; Grigory, who raised other men’s sons; the elder as a spiritual father to Alyosha. At the trial the defence argues that a father who does not love is no father, and Ivan asks the court who does not desire his father’s death.

He gets rid of his eldest son; One reptile will devour the other; What should I say to my boy?; Who doesn’t desire his father’s death?; Fathers, provoke not your children.

Speak, all the same, is there a God, or not? Only, be serious. I want you to be serious now.” “No, there is no God.” “Alyosha, is there a God?” “There is.”

Book III, chapter 8

One reptile will devour the other. And serve them both right, too.

Ivan Karamazov, Book III, chapter 9

Children and their suffering

Suffering children stand at the centre of the argument. Ivan collects true stories of tortured children and says that no future harmony is worth a child’s tears; Dmitri dreams of a starving baby and decides to go to Siberia for it; Ilusha dies after defending his father. The schoolboys of Book X, clever, cruel and loyal, are the audience for Alyosha’s last speech.

The truth is not worth such a price; Why is the babe poor?; a pin in the bread; crumble a piece of bread on it; a good memory, especially a memory of childhood.

And if the sufferings of children go to swell the sum of sufferings which was necessary to pay for truth, then I protest that the truth is not worth such a price.

Ivan Karamazov, Book V, chapter 4

Besides, too high a price is asked for harmony; it’s beyond our means to pay so much to enter on it. And so I hasten to give back my entrance ticket, and if I am an honest man I am bound to give it back as soon as possible. And that I am doing. It’s not God that I don’t accept, Alyosha, only I most respectfully return Him the ticket.

Ivan Karamazov, Book V, chapter 4

Active love

Zossima distinguishes love in dreams, eager for applause, from active love, which is labour and patience. Madame Hohlakov loves humanity in general; Katerina Ivanovna loves out of pride and gratitude; Grushenka gives an onion; Alyosha’s love is practical and constant. The novel also asks whether one can love one’s neighbour at all, as Ivan doubts.

Love in action is a harsh and dreadful thing; Love all God’s creation; I’ve found a true sister; the suffering of being unable to love.

He was simply an early lover of humanity, and that he adopted the monastic life was simply because at that time it struck him, so to say, as the ideal escape for his soul struggling from the darkness of worldly wickedness to the light of love.

Book I, chapter 4

Above all, don’t lie to yourself. The man who lies to himself and listens to his own lie comes to such a pass that he cannot distinguish the truth within him, or around him, and so loses all respect for himself and for others.

Father Zossima, Book II, chapter 2

Justice and the law

The novel is set just after Russia’s court reforms of the 1860s brought in public trials, professional advocates and juries, and Books IX and XII follow an investigation and a trial in detail. The court convicts the wrong man by honest reasoning from false inference. Behind it Dostoevsky raises other justice: Ivan’s demand for retribution for children, the elder’s forgiveness, and Dmitri’s acceptance of a punishment he has not earned in law.

The prosecutor catches Mitya; The galloping troika; an argument that cuts both ways; the peasants have stood firm; A Judicial Error.

I accept the torture of accusation, and my public shame, I want to suffer and by suffering I shall be purified.

Dmitri Karamazov, Book IX, chapter 9

For if the troika were drawn by his heroes, Sobakevitch, Nozdryov, Tchitchikov, it could reach no rational goal, whoever might be driving it.

Book XII, chapter 6

The Karamazov nature

The family’s inheritance is appetite: for women, money, drink, argument and life itself. Fyodor Pavlovitch is a sensualist to the end; Dmitri knows that God and the devil fight in his heart over beauty; Ivan confesses a thirst for life that he calls Karamazov; even Alyosha, Rakitin taunts him, has it in him. The novel treats this vitality as dangerous and precious.

The battlefield is the heart of man; the sticky little leaves; the sensualists; Hurrah for Karamazov!

The awful thing is that beauty is mysterious as well as terrible. God and the devil are fighting there and the battlefield is the heart of man.

Dmitri Karamazov, Book III, chapter 3

On the envelope is written: ‘To my angel, Grushenka, when she will come to me.’

Dmitri Karamazov, Book III, chapter 5

Memory

Alyosha remembers his mother in a slanting ray of the setting sun; Zossima remembers his brother Markel; Dmitri remembers Dr Herzenstube’s pound of nuts. The novel ends with Alyosha’s speech to the boys at the stone: a good memory from childhood may be the best education, and may save a man from evil.

A good memory, especially a memory of childhood; let us never forget one another; the pound of nuts.

You must know that there is nothing higher and stronger and more wholesome and good for life in the future than some good memory, especially a memory of childhood, of home.

Alyosha Karamazov, Epilogue, chapter 3

The monastery and the elders

At the monastery near the town, the elders, spiritual guides whom some monks distrust as an innovation, are opposed by ascetics like Father Ferapont. Pilgrims come for Zossima’s blessing, the monks expect miracles from his body, and his quick decay causes a scandal. Dostoevsky visited the monastery of Optina Pustyn in 1878, and drew on it for the setting.

What was such an elder?; Peasant women who have faith; the breath of corruption; Cana of Galilee.

An elder was one who took your soul, your will, into his soul and his will. When you choose an elder, you renounce your own will and yield it to him in complete submission, complete self-abnegation.

Book I, chapter 5

The elder distinctly and deliberately bowed down at Dmitri’s feet till his forehead touched the floor.

Book II, chapter 6

Money and inheritance

Money drives the plot: the inheritance Dmitri believes his father owes him, the three thousand roubles in the envelope, the three thousand Katerina Ivanovna gave Dmitri to post, the fifteen hundred sewn in a rag, and the two hundred Snegiryov stamps into the sand. Almost every character has a price, and the few who refuse to be bought stand out.

To my angel, Grushenka; a scoundrel but not a thief; for our shame; the money in the stocking.

On the envelope is written: ‘To my angel, Grushenka, when she will come to me.’

Dmitri Karamazov, Book III, chapter 5

What should I say to my boy if I took money from you for our shame?

Captain Snegiryov, Book IV, chapter 7
