Faith and doubt

Whether there is a God, and what follows if there is not, is the question at the centre of the book. Fyodor Pavlovitch asks his sons over the brandy; Ivan accepts God but returns his ticket to God’s world; Smerdyakov and the devil draw the conclusion that everything is lawful. Against them stand Zossima’s teaching, Alyosha’s shaken and restored faith, and Dmitri’s hymn from underground. Dostoevsky meant Book VI, The Russian Monk, as the answer to Ivan, given, he said, as an artistic picture and not point by point.

Through the book Is there a God, or not?; I most respectfully return Him the ticket; What is hell?; a glorious hymn to God; I am x in an indeterminate equation.

In the text

He was simply an early lover of humanity, and that he adopted the monastic life was simply because at that time it struck him, so to say, as the ideal escape for his soul struggling from the darkness of worldly wickedness to the light of love.

Book I, chapter 4

Before the novel shows Alyosha doing anything, the narrator defends him against being thought odd. The word simply appears twice, as if love of humanity needed no explanation. The phrase darkness of worldly wickedness looks ahead to the family we are about to meet.

Besides, too high a price is asked for harmony; it’s beyond our means to pay so much to enter on it. And so I hasten to give back my entrance ticket, and if I am an honest man I am bound to give it back as soon as possible. And that I am doing. It’s not God that I don’t accept, Alyosha, only I most respectfully return Him the ticket.

Ivan Karamazov, Book V, chapter 4

Ivan’s image of the ticket makes salvation a purchase he will not make: he does not deny God, he declines to enter. The irony of most respectfully keeps his anger polite. Alyosha calls it rebellion, and Ivan answers that one cannot live in rebellion.

Freedom

Ivan’s Grand Inquisitor argues that men cannot bear the freedom Christ gave them and will gladly hand it over for bread, miracle and authority. Dostoevsky tests the claim everywhere: in the elder to whom a monk surrenders his will in order to find freedom, in Dmitri’s longing to be free of Katerina Ivanovna’s money, and in Ivan’s freedom from all law, which ends in delirium.

Through the book An elder was one who took your soul, your will; freedom of choice in the knowledge of good and evil; miracle, mystery and authority; everything is lawful.

In the text

An elder was one who took your soul, your will, into his soul and his will. When you choose an elder, you renounce your own will and yield it to him in complete submission, complete self-abnegation.

Book I, chapter 5

Obedience is chosen here in order to reach freedom, which the narrator says is the aim of this terrible school. The same trade will be made on a vast scale in the Grand Inquisitor, where men give up their freedom for bread. Dostoevsky sets the two surrenders side by side, one chosen and one sold.

Didst Thou forget that man prefers peace, and even death, to freedom of choice in the knowledge of good and evil? Nothing is more seductive for man than his freedom of conscience, but nothing is a greater cause of suffering.

The Grand Inquisitor, Book V, chapter 5

The Inquisitor’s charge is that Christ asked too much of human beings. He speaks as one who loves weak men and will relieve them of a burden they cannot carry. The argument has troubled readers ever since, and Ivan offers it without any answer except a kiss.

Guilt and responsibility

One man strikes the blow, but the novel spreads the guilt. Smerdyakov kills; Ivan taught him that everything is lawful and left the house; Dmitri wished his father dead and nearly killed him. Zossima’s teaching, that each of us is responsible to all for all, answers the question of who is guilty with a claim on everyone. Dmitri accepts his punishment for the thought he did not act on.

Through the book Each is responsible to all for all; I only know one thing, it wasn’t you killed father; you are the real murderer; I accept my punishment, not because I killed him.

In the text

It’s a true saying then, that ‘it’s always worth while speaking to a clever man,’

Smerdyakov, Book V, chapter 7

Smerdyakov’s proverb seals an agreement that Ivan never consciously made. By going away he has left the house open to whatever happens. Ivan remembers the words for the rest of the novel, and they return in each of his interviews with Smerdyakov.

little heart of mine, my joy, believe me, every one is really responsible to all men for all men and for everything. I don’t know how to explain it to you, but I feel it is so, painfully even.

Book VI, chapter 2

Zossima’s central idea comes first from a dying boy who cannot explain it. Responsibility here is a way of seeing that makes each person answerable for all; it has nothing to do with sharing out blame. Zossima says his brother’s face was a prophecy for him, and sees it again in Alyosha.

Fathers and sons

A father who forgot his children is killed, and every son is implicated. Beside the Karamazovs Dostoevsky sets other fathers and sons: Captain Snegiryov and Ilusha, whose love survives humiliation; Grigory, who raised other men’s sons; the elder as a spiritual father to Alyosha. At the trial the defence argues that a father who does not love is no father, and Ivan asks the court who does not desire his father’s death.

Through the book He gets rid of his eldest son; One reptile will devour the other; What should I say to my boy?; Who doesn’t desire his father’s death?; Fathers, provoke not your children.

In the text

One reptile will devour the other. And serve them both right, too.

Ivan Karamazov, Book III, chapter 9

Ivan speaks of his father and brother as animals, and seems almost to wish the outcome. Alyosha shudders. The phrase returns to haunt Ivan, since his indifference to what happens between the reptiles is the moral ground of his guilt.

It was he, not my brother, killed our father. He murdered him and I incited him to do it … Who doesn’t desire his father’s death?

Ivan Karamazov, Book XII, chapter 5

Ivan tells the truth, and it sounds like madness. His question to the court turns the accusation on everyone watching, and they take it as proof of delirium. The court’s reaction is the novel’s judgement on how people hear an unwelcome truth.

Children and their suffering

Suffering children stand at the centre of the argument. Ivan collects true stories of tortured children and says that no future harmony is worth a child’s tears; Dmitri dreams of a starving baby and decides to go to Siberia for it; Ilusha dies after defending his father. The schoolboys of Book X, clever, cruel and loyal, are the audience for Alyosha’s last speech.

Through the book The truth is not worth such a price; Why is the babe poor?; a pin in the bread; crumble a piece of bread on it; a good memory, especially a memory of childhood.

In the text

And if the sufferings of children go to swell the sum of sufferings which was necessary to pay for truth, then I protest that the truth is not worth such a price.

Ivan Karamazov, Book V, chapter 4

Ivan’s rebellion is moral: he will not buy harmony with a child’s tears. The argument is made from love, which is why it is so hard to answer. Dostoevsky drew several of the cases from real trials he had written about in his journal.

I am a Socialist, Karamazov, I am an incurable Socialist,

Kolya Krassotkin, Book X, chapter 6

Kolya’s announcement comes apropos of nothing, and Alyosha laughs, reminding him he is only thirteen. The comedy is affectionate. His borrowed opinions echo Ivan and Rakitin on a schoolboy’s scale, and Alyosha treats him as a person worth arguing with.

Active love

Zossima distinguishes love in dreams, eager for applause, from active love, which is labour and patience. Madame Hohlakov loves humanity in general; Katerina Ivanovna loves out of pride and gratitude; Grushenka gives an onion; Alyosha’s love is practical and constant. The novel also asks whether one can love one’s neighbour at all, as Ivan doubts.

Through the book Love in action is a harsh and dreadful thing; Love all God’s creation; I’ve found a true sister; the suffering of being unable to love.

In the text

love in action is a harsh and dreadful thing compared with love in dreams. Love in dreams is greedy for immediate action, rapidly performed and in the sight of all.

Father Zossima, Book II, chapter 4

Zossima does not flatter her; he tells her that real love is labour and fortitude. Love in dreams wants an audience, and the novel is full of it, in Katerina Ivanovna’s grand sacrifices and Madame Hohlakov’s enthusiasms. Alyosha’s quiet visits to the Snegiryovs show the other kind.

Alyosha got up, went to him and softly kissed him on the lips. “That’s plagiarism,” cried Ivan, highly delighted. “You stole that from my poem.

Book V, chapter 5

Alyosha answers his brother the way Christ answered the Inquisitor, with love where argument fails. Ivan’s joke shows he understands, and that he is moved. The repetition makes the poem’s ending into the brothers’ real parting.

Justice and the law

The novel is set just after Russia’s court reforms of the 1860s brought in public trials, professional advocates and juries, and Books IX and XII follow an investigation and a trial in detail. The court convicts the wrong man by honest reasoning from false inference. Behind it Dostoevsky raises other justice: Ivan’s demand for retribution for children, the elder’s forgiveness, and Dmitri’s acceptance of a punishment he has not earned in law.

Through the book The prosecutor catches Mitya; The galloping troika; an argument that cuts both ways; the peasants have stood firm; A Judicial Error.

In the text

I accept the torture of accusation, and my public shame, I want to suffer and by suffering I shall be purified.

Dmitri Karamazov, Book IX, chapter 9

Dmitri takes on his punishment as a moral cleansing while insisting that he did not kill. He is ready to suffer for what he wished, and in this Zossima’s teaching reaches him through the babe dream.

For if the troika were drawn by his heroes, Sobakevitch, Nozdryov, Tchitchikov, it could reach no rational goal, whoever might be driving it.

Book XII, chapter 6

The prosecutor borrows Gogol’s famous image and puts Gogol’s rogues in the harness. It wins him applause, the first of his career, and sets the Karamazovs up as a picture of Russia. The trial becomes a national allegory, and Dmitri disappears behind it.

The Karamazov nature

The family’s inheritance is appetite: for women, money, drink, argument and life itself. Fyodor Pavlovitch is a sensualist to the end; Dmitri knows that God and the devil fight in his heart over beauty; Ivan confesses a thirst for life that he calls Karamazov; even Alyosha, Rakitin taunts him, has it in him. The novel treats this vitality as dangerous and precious.

Through the book The battlefield is the heart of man; the sticky little leaves; the sensualists; Hurrah for Karamazov!

In the text

The awful thing is that beauty is mysterious as well as terrible. God and the devil are fighting there and the battlefield is the heart of man.

Dmitri Karamazov, Book III, chapter 3

Dmitri makes beauty the ground of a war inside himself. His images are sensual, and the struggle is moral and religious. The confession that follows, with its five thousand roubles and its bow, shows both sides of the fight in one man.

Though I may not believe in the order of the universe, yet I love the sticky little leaves as they open in spring.

Ivan Karamazov, Book V, chapter 3

Before arguing against God’s world, Ivan confesses that he loves it. The image is small and physical, young leaves still sticky from the bud. Alyosha says that he must love life before its meaning, and in parting Ivan promises to love the leaves by remembering him.

Memory

Alyosha remembers his mother in a slanting ray of the setting sun; Zossima remembers his brother Markel; Dmitri remembers Dr Herzenstube’s pound of nuts. The novel ends with Alyosha’s speech to the boys at the stone: a good memory from childhood may be the best education, and may save a man from evil.

Through the book A good memory, especially a memory of childhood; let us never forget one another; the pound of nuts.

In the text

You must know that there is nothing higher and stronger and more wholesome and good for life in the future than some good memory, especially a memory of childhood, of home.

Alyosha Karamazov, Epilogue, chapter 3

Alyosha ends the book with an exhortation to remember. His claim is modest and practical: one good memory may keep a man from evil. His audience is children, the people for whom Ivan returned his ticket.

The monastery and the elders

At the monastery near the town, the elders, spiritual guides whom some monks distrust as an innovation, are opposed by ascetics like Father Ferapont. Pilgrims come for Zossima’s blessing, the monks expect miracles from his body, and his quick decay causes a scandal. Dostoevsky visited the monastery of Optina Pustyn in 1878, and drew on it for the setting.

Through the book What was such an elder?; Peasant women who have faith; the breath of corruption; Cana of Galilee.

In the text

The elder distinctly and deliberately bowed down at Dmitri’s feet till his forehead touched the floor.

Book II, chapter 6

Nobody understands the bow, and the narrator reports it without explanation. Rakitin says the elder sniffed a crime; Zossima later tells Alyosha he bowed to the great suffering in store for Dmitri. The gesture stands over the whole plot.

from their humble coffins, too, the breath of corruption had come, naturally, as from all dead bodies, but that had caused no scandal nor even the slightest excitement.

Book VII, chapter 1

The narrator’s calm reasonableness exposes the crowd’s hunger for a miracle. Nature takes its course with the saint as with anyone, and the scandal lies in the watchers. Alyosha’s grief at it becomes a crisis of faith.

Money and inheritance

Money drives the plot: the inheritance Dmitri believes his father owes him, the three thousand roubles in the envelope, the three thousand Katerina Ivanovna gave Dmitri to post, the fifteen hundred sewn in a rag, and the two hundred Snegiryov stamps into the sand. Almost every character has a price, and the few who refuse to be bought stand out.

Through the book To my angel, Grushenka; a scoundrel but not a thief; for our shame; the money in the stocking.

In the text

On the envelope is written: ‘To my angel, Grushenka, when she will come to me.’

Dmitri Karamazov, Book III, chapter 5

No object matters more to the plot: the envelope motivates Dmitri, tempts Smerdyakov, and lies torn and empty beside the murdered man. The endearment on it shows the father buying love as he buys everything.

What should I say to my boy if I took money from you for our shame?

Captain Snegiryov, Book IV, chapter 7

Snegiryov saves his dignity and loses his family’s bread, and he knows it. The money would have been payment for the beating in front of his son, and Ilusha’s love for him will not allow it. Alyosha sees that the man did not know until the last moment that he would throw it away.
