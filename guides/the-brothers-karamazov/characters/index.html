The Brothers Karamazov · Fyodor Dostoevsky

Characters

Fyodor Pavlovitch Karamazov

The father, murdered in his own house; first appears Book I, chapter 1

Fifty-five, rich and greedy for life, he began as a sponger at other men’s tables and ended with a hundred thousand roubles. He married twice, abandoned all three sons as children, and plays the buffoon on purpose, because, as he says, he always feels that people take him for one. He keeps three thousand roubles in an envelope for Grushenka, and is killed on the night he expects her.

Dmitri Karamazov

The eldest son, called Mitya, accused of the murder; first appears Book I, chapter 2

A retired army officer of twenty-seven, violent, generous and ashamed of himself, he calls himself an insect and quotes Schiller. He is engaged to Katerina Ivanovna but mad for Grushenka, and believes his father has cheated him of his mother’s money. He threatened to kill his father, was in the garden that night and struck down Grigory, but swears he is not a murderer. Arrested, he decides to accept his suffering for the babe, and for having wished the crime.

Ivan Karamazov

The second son, a writer and unbeliever; first appears Book I, chapter 3

Twenty-three, proud and brilliant, he has made his name with articles and has come home for reasons nobody can explain. He loves life and cannot accept God’s world because of the suffering of children, and he tells Alyosha his poem of the Grand Inquisitor. His saying that without immortality everything is lawful reaches Smerdyakov, who acts on it. Ivan’s discovery that he is morally the murderer brings on a breakdown, a visit from the devil and a collapse in court.

Alyosha Karamazov

The youngest son, a novice, and the narrator’s hero; first appears Book I, chapter 1

Twenty, healthy, cheerful and loved by everyone, he joined the monastery because Father Zossima seemed to him the way out of darkness into the light of love. The elder sends him out into the world, and he spends the novel going between his brothers, the women who love them, the Snegiryovs and the schoolboys. His faith is shaken when the elder’s body decays, and restored by Grushenka’s pity and a dream of Cana.

Smerdyakov

Fyodor Pavlovitch’s cook and valet; first appears Book III, chapter 6

Born in the bath-house to Lizaveta, a homeless holy fool, and widely thought to be Fyodor Pavlovitch’s son, he was brought up by Grigory and his wife. As a child he hanged cats; now he is an epileptic, a fastidious cook trained in Moscow, contemptuous of Russia and fond of arguing about faith. He admires Ivan and takes his ideas literally, and on the night of the murder he fakes a fit on the cellar stairs.

Grushenka

The woman Dmitri and his father both want; first appears Book III, chapter 10

Twenty-two, beautiful and sharp-tongued, she was seduced at seventeen by a Polish officer and then kept by an old merchant, Samsonov, and has made money of her own. She plays father and son against each other while she waits for her first lover to send for her. When the Pole turns out to be a petty cheat, she chooses Dmitri and follows him into his punishment. Her pity for Alyosha on the night of the elder’s death shows the good in her.

Katerina Ivanovna

Dmitri’s betrothed; first appears Book III, chapter 10

The proud daughter of a colonel, she once went to Dmitri’s lodging for money to save her father, and was given it without conditions; later a rich relation in Moscow gave her a fortune. She insists on being engaged to Dmitri out of gratitude and wounded pride, while loving Ivan and tormenting him. At the trial she first testifies to Dmitri’s generosity, then, to save Ivan, produces the letter that convicts him.

Father Zossima

The elder of the monastery; first appears Book I, chapter 5

Sixty-five and near death, he is revered by pilgrims who come for his blessing and resented by monks who distrust elders. As a young officer he was cruel and quarrelsome, until he struck his servant on the eve of a duel and was converted. He teaches that each of us is responsible to all for all and that hell is the inability to love. His body decays quickly after his death, to the scandal of the monastery.

Grigory

Fyodor Pavlovitch’s old servant; first appears Book I, chapter 2

Gloomy, stubborn, honest and devoted, he looked after all three boys when their father forgot them, and took in Lizaveta’s baby after his own six-fingered child died. He sees the garden door open on the night of the murder, and Dmitri strikes him down on the fence with the brass pestle. His evidence about the open door weighs heavily against Dmitri at the trial.

Rakitin

A seminarist on the make; first appears Book II, chapter 2

An ambitious young divinity student with liberal opinions and an eye for money, he explains everyone’s motives cynically, and predicts a crime in the Karamazov family on the first day. He takes Alyosha to Grushenka for twenty-five roubles on the night of the elder’s death, writes about the case, and gives evidence at the trial, where the defence exposes him.

Lise Hohlakov

Madame Hohlakov’s daughter; first appears Book II, chapter 4

Fourteen, clever and unable to walk, she writes Alyosha a love letter and they agree to marry one day. By Book XI she has broken it off, and tells him about her wish to be destroyed and to torment, her dreams of devils, and a story of a crucified child, before she crushes her finger in the door.

Madame Hohlakov

A rich widow of the town; first appears Book II, chapter 3

Thirty-three and fond of new ideas, she tells Father Zossima that she dreams of becoming a sister of mercy but could not bear ingratitude. She receives everyone, gossips about everything, and when Dmitri comes to her for three thousand roubles advises him to go to the gold-mines.

Captain Snegiryov

A poor retired captain, Ilusha’s father; first appears Book IV, chapter 6

A clownish, desperate man of forty-five with a beard like a wisp of tow, he lives in a hut with a mad wife, a hunchbacked daughter and a sick son. Dmitri dragged him out of a tavern by the beard in front of Ilusha. He refuses Katerina Ivanovna’s money for the boy’s sake, and at Ilusha’s funeral crumbles bread on the grave for the sparrows.

Ilusha

Captain Snegiryov’s son; first appears Book IV, chapter 3

About nine, fierce and proud, he saw his father humiliated and decided to punish the Karamazovs, fighting the schoolboys and biting Alyosha’s finger. He is tormented by guilt over the dog he hurt, falls ill, and dies. His funeral and the speech at the stone end the novel.

Kolya Krassotkin

A schoolboy of thirteen; first appears Book X, chapter 1

Thirteen and a widow’s son, clever, conceited and brave, he argues about Voltaire and calls himself a socialist, and once lay between the rails under a train for a bet. He trains a dog called Perezvon and brings it to Ilusha’s bedside. Won over by Alyosha, he leads the boys in the cheer that ends the book.

Father Ferapont

An old ascetic monk; first appears Book IV, chapter 1

Seventy-five or more, a great faster and a great enemy of the elders, he lives alone in a cell beyond the monastery apiary, sees devils in every corner, and claims to talk with the Holy Spirit in the form of a bird. When Zossima’s body begins to smell he bursts into the cell crying, Casting out I cast out!

Pan Mussyalovitch

Grushenka’s first lover; first appears Book VIII, chapter 7

The Polish officer who seduced Grushenka five years before, now a retired official who served in Siberia as a veterinary surgeon. He summons her to Mokroe, speaks of her proudly and grandly, cheats at cards with his companion, and will take Dmitri’s money to leave.

The Grand Inquisitor

The cardinal of Ivan’s poem; first appears Book V, chapter 5

An old man of almost ninety, tall and erect, he has burned heretics in Seville and has Christ arrested when he returns. In a long speech in the prison he argues that men cannot bear freedom and will give it up for bread, and that the Church has corrected Christ’s work with miracle, mystery and authority. Christ answers him with a kiss, and he lets him go.

The devil

Ivan’s visitor in his nightmare; first appears Book XI, chapter 9

He appears as a shabby Russian gentleman of about fifty, in check trousers and a threadbare scarf, a poor relation who has sunk to living off old friends. He repeats Ivan’s own ideas back to him with mockery, talks of rheumatism and the public baths, while Ivan insists that he is only the embodiment of his own worst thoughts.

Fetyukovitch

The defending counsel from Petersburg; first appears Book XII, chapter 1

A celebrated lawyer brought from Petersburg partly at Katerina Ivanovna’s instance, he undermines the witnesses one by one, argues that there was no robbery and no murder, and ends by saying that a father like Fyodor Pavlovitch is no father at all.

Ippolit Kirillovitch

The public prosecutor; first appears Book IX, chapter 2

A clever, sensitive man who has never been properly heard, he takes part in the investigation at Mokroe and at the trial makes the speech of his life, an account of the Karamazov family as a picture of Russia. He is applauded for the first time, and dies of consumption nine months later.
