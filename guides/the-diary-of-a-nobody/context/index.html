The Diary of a Nobody · George and Weedon Grossmith

Context

Dates

1841
Punch begins publication The weekly that would print the diary in twenty-six instalments, read largely by the professional and clerical households it made its subject.
1847
George Grossmith is born in London On 9 December, son of a police-court reporter. He worked at Bow Street himself before making a living on the platform as a comic singer at the piano.
1854
Weedon Grossmith is born in London On 9 June. He trained as a painter and exhibited before turning to the stage, and is credited as co-author of the book as well as its illustrator.
1877
George Grossmith joins the Gilbert and Sullivan company He created John Wellington Wells in The Sorcerer and went on to the run of leading comic parts, among them Sir Joseph Porter, Bunthorne, the Lord Chancellor and Ko-Ko.
1883
The Cheap Trains Act Railway companies are obliged to run workmen’s trains at low fares, one of the pressures that filled districts such as Holloway with streets of small rented villas, often, as at The Laurels, with the line at the end of the garden and two pounds off the rent.
1888
The ‘Is Marriage a Failure?’ press correspondence Set off by Mona Caird’s article on marriage in the Westminster Review that August and carried on at length in the daily press. Pooter and Carrie talk it over on 2 November and agree that it has been no failure in their case.
1888
The serial begins in Punch Twenty-six instalments between May 1888 and May 1889, written while George Grossmith was still playing nightly for D’Oyly Carte.
1889
George Grossmith leaves the D’Oyly Carte company After twelve years of comic leads he returned to touring as a solo entertainer, which had been his living before Gilbert and Sullivan and remained more profitable.
1889
The Eiffel Tower opens in Paris Named by Hardfur Huttle at Franching’s dinner, beside the Brooklyn Bridge, as the kind of thing a man of the happy medium will never build.
1892
J. W. Arrowsmith publishes the book in Bristol Expanded by roughly four months of entries and illustrated with twenty-six drawings by Weedon Grossmith. The same firm had published Three Men in a Boat three years earlier.
1910
A new issue carries prefatory tributes The early book editions attracted little notice; the 1910 issue, with appreciations by Lord Rosebery and Augustine Birrell, marks the point at which admirers, more than sales, made the book’s reputation.
1912
George Grossmith dies at Folkestone On 1 March. He had published two volumes of reminiscence of his own, which is one of the jokes behind Pooter’s preface.
1919
Weedon Grossmith dies On 14 June, after a second career as actor, playwright and theatre manager, and a memoir of his own about the move from painting to the stage.
1964
Ken Russell films the diary for the BBC Made for the arts programme Monitor and played as a silent comedy with captions, which suits a book whose narrator cannot hear how he sounds.
2007
A four-part adaptation on BBC Four With Hugh Bonneville as Pooter speaking the entries straight to camera, the nearest television has come to reproducing the effect of the diary form.

Places

Holloway, north London
The Laurels, Brickfield Terrace, is invented, but the district is not: streets of small rented villas built for clerks, with the Great Northern line close enough to crack the garden wall.
The Mansion House, City of London
The Lord Mayor’s reception for the representatives of trades and commerce, where Pooter meets his ironmonger, drinks champagne with a sheriff and falls over in the middle of a waltz.
Broadstairs, Kent
The family’s usual holiday. They take apartments near the station, because rooms on the cliffs cost twice as much; Lupin objects to the fly in the butter.
Margate, Kent
Where the Cummings are staying, and where Gowing’s game of Cutlets ends with the whole party on the floor and Carrie’s head against the fender.
Muswell Hill
Watney Lodge, the house of Mr. Edgar Paul Finsworth, a few minutes from the station: a collie, a spaniel called Bibbs, water-colours of India and two portraits of the dead.
Sutton, Surrey
Home of Mrs. James, whose visits bring smocking, manicure, toy beetles on the mantelpiece and finally table-turning into The Laurels.
Peckham
Mr. Franching’s house, a long way from Holloway by two omnibuses, and the setting for the dinner at which Hardfur Huttle defines respectability.
Bayswater
Lupin’s furnished apartments at two guineas a week, taken to be near the Murray Poshes and because, in his phrase, Brickfield Terrace is a bit off.
