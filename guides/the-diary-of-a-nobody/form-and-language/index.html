The Diary of a Nobody · George and Weedon Grossmith

Form and language

The diary form

A narrative built from dated entries written close to the events, so that the teller has no hindsight, no chance to shape the story and no way of leaving anything out that he has not yet learnt to be ashamed of.

Everything depends on it. Pooter writes up the evening’s humiliation the same night, while it still feels like a triumph, and the entry is what convicts him. The form also supplies the running gags free of charge, since a scraper or a blanc-mange can simply reappear on the next date, and it produces one perfect accident: he notices the following morning that he wrote retired tired, thinks it rather funny, and says that if he were not so worried he might have made a joke of it.

Why should I not publish my diary? I have often seen reminiscences of people I have never even heard of, and I fail to see—because I do not happen to be a ‘Somebody’—why my diary should not be interesting.

Charles Pooter, Introduction by Mr. Pooter

Dramatic irony

A gap between what the speaker understands and what the audience can see, held open long enough for the audience to do the judging.

The gap is never closed by a second narrator, because there is not one. The reader assembles the other view from the evidence Pooter files: the silence after his best joke, the guests’ laughter at something they will not explain, Perkupp’s remark that the foolery seems amusing. The Grossmiths take the risk that the reader will see nothing, and rely entirely on our knowing what a room looks like when it has stopped finding you funny.

It’s concerning you both; for doesn’t it seem odd that Gowing’s always coming and Cummings’ always going? … I think this was one of the best jokes I have ever made.

Charles Pooter, Chapter III, April 27

Bathos

A drop from the elevated to the trivial, usually at the end of a sentence or a passage.

The diarist builds the flight himself and then falls out of it. He is bleeding to death like Marat in the waxworks until he remembers the enamel paint. He is a proud man leading his pretty wife to the Lord Mayor until his boots go from under him. His sixteen closely written pages are answered in less than sixteen lines. The pattern is so regular that by the middle of the book the reader starts bracing for the last clause of every entry.

I restrained my feelings, and quietly remarked that I thought it was possible for a city clerk to be a gentleman. He replied he was very glad to hear it, and wanted to know whether I had ever come across one, for he hadn’t.

Chapter I, April 6

Wordplay

Puns, near-homophones and turns on a word, made for their own sake and judged by the response they get.

Pooter’s puns are the book’s cruellest device, because they are funny twice over: once as puns and once as evidence. He gets into a scrape about a scraper, tells Gowing he is talking dry rot, finds that Gowing is always coming and Cummings always going, and is amazed when the room goes quiet. He records the laughter carefully, and the record shows that the only reliable laugher is his wife.

I don’t often make jokes, but I replied: “You’re talking a lot of dry rot yourself.” I could not help roaring at this, and Carrie said her sides quite ached with laughter. I never was so immensely tickled by anything I have ever said before. I actually woke up twice during the night, and laughed till the bed shook.

Chapter II, April 12

Comic repetition

A detail brought back at intervals until the return itself raises the laugh.

The scraper claims Cummings, Gowing, the butterman and the curate in the first chapter. The blanc-mange comes back to the table for five days, disguised at last in a glass dish with jam round it. Padge says that it is right, and nothing else, for two whole evenings. Cummings produces the same cycling paper twice, years apart in feeling if not in time. The diary form makes all of this effortless: a date is enough to bring the thing back.

My first thought was that I had ruptured an artery, and was bleeding to death, and should be discovered, later on, looking like a second Marat, as I remember seeing him in Madame Tussaud’s. My second thought was to ring the bell, but remembered there was no bell to ring. My third was, that there was nothing but the enamel paint, which had dissolved with boiling water.

Chapter III, April 29

The unreliable narrator

A narrator whose account the reader learns to read against, because he misjudges himself or the meaning of what he reports.

Pooter is accurate about facts and wrong about their weight. He quotes insults verbatim and files them under friendship, calls a snub a compliment, and reports his own jokes alongside the exact length of the silence that followed. Nothing is hidden and nothing is admitted, so the reader is left holding two versions of every evening, which is also what it is like to read anybody’s diary.

When I returned to the parlour, Gowing was retailing his idiotic joke about the odd sock, and Carrie was roaring with laughter. I suppose I am losing my sense of humour.

Chapter XI, November 25

Style

A style so particular to one speaker that a sentence can be identified without the name attached.

The voice is made of a few habits: the apologetic by-the-by, the offered reward (ten shillings, half-a-sovereign) for information nobody will give, the phrase I am a poor man used before spending, the solemn adverbs (I simply said, I replied coolly, I treated the observation with silence), and italics laid on so thickly that the emphasis stops meaning anything. It is a clerk’s prose (careful, hedged, slightly legal) applied to a boot-scraper.

Why should I not publish my diary? I have often seen reminiscences of people I have never even heard of, and I fail to see—because I do not happen to be a ‘Somebody’—why my diary should not be interesting.

Charles Pooter, Introduction by Mr. Pooter
