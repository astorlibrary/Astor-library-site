The Diary of a Nobody · George and Weedon Grossmith

Characters

Charles Pooter

The diarist; a senior clerk in the City and the tenant of The Laurels; first appears Introduction

He writes the book and is its target, without ever suspecting that the two facts are connected. He is punctual, honest, thrifty, fond of his wife, proud of his beard, and convinced that a joke is a thing you announce before you make. Every humiliation in the diary is one he wrote down himself, in his own hand, usually the same evening, because he thought it would interest posterity. That is the whole machine of the comedy: he is the prosecution’s only witness and does not know he is testifying.

Caroline Pooter

His wife; called Carrie throughout, and Caroline when he is being solemn; first appears Chapter I

She laughs at his jokes more often than anyone else does, which is why he keeps making them. She is also the only person in the book who can beat him in an argument, and she does it once, at length, after the Mansion House ball, listing his evening’s conduct item by item while he writes it all down. Her weakness is Mrs. James of Sutton, whose advice reaches the house as smocking, manicure, toy beetles on the mantelpiece and finally table-turning. Pooter’s love for her is the one feeling in the diary that survives being written down.

Willie Lupin Pooter

Their son, twenty at the start of the diary, who has dropped his first name; first appears Chapter I

He arrives home dismissed from a bank, calls his father Guv., despises Perkupp, makes two hundred pounds in a day and loses his father eighteen, gets engaged to a woman eight years older and is thrown over for a hat manufacturer, hands his father’s firm’s best client to a rival and is paid a commission for it. He is quick, rude, generous and entirely modern. The diary treats him as a worry; the reader can see that he is also the only person in the book who is going anywhere.

Mr. Gowing

An old friend who calls most evenings and never knocks; first appears Chapter I

He complains about the smell of the paint, borrows the umbrella, walks into the room wearing two hats and Carrie’s fur tippet, invites the Pooters to a party and then leaves town, and punches his host in the dark without recognising the head. He is the reason the book is not a cosy one: he is truly unkind, and Pooter cannot do without him. His friend Padge, who takes the best armchair and says nothing all evening except that it is right, is his contribution to the household.

Mr. Cummings

An old friend who lives opposite, and an enthusiastic tricyclist; first appears Chapter I

He is the gentler of the two friends and the one more easily wounded. He is ill twice without anybody calling, and both times produces the copy of the Bicycle News in which the accident was reported, as though the paper were proof that he had been slighted. He takes Gowing’s fifteen pounds of Parachikka Chlorates off his hands and loses the lot. His running complaint, that illness shows you who your true friends are, is the only sustained reproach anybody in the book makes to the Pooters.

Mr. Perkupp

The principal of Pooter’s firm in the City; first appears Chapter II

He is grave, courteous and slightly absurd, and Pooter’s feeling for him is the nearest thing in the book to worship. He gives in Pooter’s name for the Mansion House without saying so, arrives at the party at the exact moment of the Blondin Donkey, takes Lupin on out of respect for the father and dismisses him for the same reason, and at the end buys the freehold of the house. Whether this makes the ending a reward or a tip is a question the diary does not think to ask.

Sarah

The Pooters’ only servant; first appears Chapter I

She answers the side door, fetches the whisky, is suspected of burning the diary and accuses the charwoman of it, and tells her master to his face that he is always complaining. She has no separate story in the book, and that is worth noticing: the household whose respectability the diary defends rests on one young woman who is paid to run up and down ten steps, and whose opinion of the red enamel paint is the only honest review it gets.

Mr. Farmerson

The Holloway ironmonger, who moves the scraper and repairs the bells; first appears Chapter II

He is hired in chapter I because the landlord seems not worth troubling, bores through the gas-pipe, and is still owed a visit to repaint the bath when Pooter meets him at the Lord Mayor’s reception with a sheriff’s arm round him. His reply to being snubbed (“if you, why not me?”) knocks out the whole basis of Pooter’s evening. He then takes a lift home in the cab and does not offer a farthing towards it.

Daisy Mutlar

Lupin’s fiancée, and afterwards Mrs. Murray Posh; first appears Chapter VIII

She is introduced as the nicest, prettiest and most accomplished girl Lupin ever met, and Pooter’s heart sinks the moment he sees her: a big young woman, he thinks at least eight years older than his son, and not even good-looking. She sings out of tune, rolls bread into pellets at supper and throws them, and within four months marries a hat manufacturer with ten thousand pounds settled on her. The book never shows her thinking; she is the shape of Lupin’s ambition more than a person he loves.

Murray Posh

Of Posh’s three-shilling hats; Lupin’s rival, and later his patron; first appears Chapter XV

A tall, fat, nervous young man who will not ride in a hansom, he takes no active part in the business that has made him rich and gives a long and interesting account of the difficulties of manufacturing cheap hats. He marries Daisy, loses six hundred pounds on Lupin’s tip, settles ten thousand each on his wife and his sister, and is finally engaged to be Lupin’s brother-in-law. He is the book’s picture of the money that was actually being made while Pooter was earning his hundred-pound rise.

Mr. Burwin-Fosselton

Of the Holloway Comedians; an amateur actor who resembles Henry Irving; first appears Chapter XI

He cracks the Pooters’ round table by leaning on it, insists on the double name every time he is addressed, kicks Carrie’s shins under it, digs a piece out of Gowing’s neck in the middle of an imitation, and writes a two-page letter telling his host to acknowledge defeat and take his whipping gracefully. Pooter hands the letter to Lupin as proof of the man’s character, and Lupin replies that he read it before it was sent and thinks an apology is owed.

Mr. Hardfur Huttle

An American writer, the guest of honour at Franching’s dinner; first appears Chapter XX

He talks for an entire chapter, keeps the ladies at the table, and states the case against the Pooters of the world so plainly that the room has no answer: the middle way is a vulgar half-measure, and the man who takes it will never build anything. He then describes a soft man with a soft beard and a made-up tie, and Pooter, who is wearing one, twice checks the glass. Carrie’s verdict on the way home is that he is exactly like Lupin, which is the nearest the book comes to naming its own subject.
