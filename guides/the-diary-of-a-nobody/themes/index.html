Pooter’s self-portrait

Pooter never lies. He reports what was said and done, including the things said to him, and then adds his own view of it, and the two do not fit. He writes out an insult word for word and calls it a compliment; he copies a sentence into the diary because he thinks it perfect, and admits three lines later that it was an apology for having been insulted. The comedy needs him to be scrupulous. A dishonest diarist would simply leave the defeats out.

Through the book The curate’s request to take round the plate, recorded as a great compliment, in chapter I; the forgiveness letter in II; the joke about Gowing and Cummings in III; the Mansion House in IV; the odd sock in XI; the bill for civility in XVIII; and a chapter of Hardfur Huttle describing him to his face in XX.

In the text

Why should I not publish my diary? I have often seen reminiscences of people I have never even heard of, and I fail to see—because I do not happen to be a ‘Somebody’—why my diary should not be interesting.

Charles Pooter, Introduction by Mr. Pooter

The book’s title is answered before the book starts, and the answer is a question nobody asked. Pooter argues from a real grievance (celebrated nobodies publish their memories, so why not an uncelebrated one?), and the argument is perfectly sound; it is the tone that gives him away, the defensive dash, the sniff at ‘Somebody’ in inverted commas. He is the first of the two brothers’ jokes and the last: the reader is about to agree with him, for two hundred pages, for reasons he would not accept.

I was a little vexed at everybody subsequently laughing at some joke which they did not explain, and it was only on going to bed I discovered I must have been walking about all the evening with an antimacassar on one button of my coat-tails.

Chapter VII, August 27

The perfect emblem of the book, and Pooter files it without comment. The sentence is built to delay: a little vexed, a joke not explained, and then the discovery at bedtime, hours too late to do anything but write it down. An antimacassar is the most domestic object in the house, the cloth that protects a chair-back from hair-oil, and he has been dragging respectability’s own furnishing round the room behind him. He does work it out in the end. He simply works it out too late, every time, and keeps the record anyway.

Respectability

Everything Pooter does is aimed at a standard he cannot quite reach and cannot afford: a front door kept locked and used only on special occasions, lavender gloves, two white ties in case one is spoiled, visiting cards ordered twenty-five at a time. The joke is less about snobbery than about the standard itself, which is a trap: every attempt to meet it produces the accident that ruins it. Huttle names the cost in one sentence, and Pooter cannot argue.

Through the book The front door that will not open in chapter I; the dress-coat the tailor takes back for want of a shilling in IV; the paragraph about Charles Pewter in V; the party in IX; the three-pound supper bill in XVIII; and the definition of the happy medium in XX.

In the text

“We have received two letters from Mr. and Mrs. Charles Pewter, requesting us to announce the important fact that they were at the Mansion House Ball.” I tore up the paper and threw it in the waste-paper basket. My time is far too valuable to bother about such trifles.

Chapter V, May 16

The paper has worked out what he is doing and printed it as news, with the phrase ‘the important fact’ carrying the whole of the editor’s opinion. The comedy then doubles back on him twice. He has written twice and ordered a dozen copies to send to friends, so the sneer is exactly earned; and his closing line about his time being too valuable arrives after three entries spent on nothing else. The misspellings (Porter, Pewter) are the crudest of jokes and the best, because a man whose name cannot survive a typesetter has no business worrying about the Lord Mayor.

I apologised for the foolery, but Mr. Perkupp said: “Oh, it seems amusing.” I could see he was not a bit amused.

Chapter IX, November 15

In three sentences there are three levels of knowledge, and for once Pooter is on the reader’s level. He sees the politeness for what it is. The entry is the turning point of the chapter: the hour he has planned for since the invitations went out arrives while there is a pantomime donkey in the drawing-room. The joke needs nothing but the flat repetition of ‘amusing’ and ‘amused’, with a ‘not’ dropped into it. Downstairs the table is a wreck, and the great man asks for soda water and is offered port.

Class

The diary is exact about money (two pounds off the rent, seven-and-six for a stick, thirty-eight shillings for a dozen of whisky, a hundred a year) and exact about rank, which is why the two keep colliding. A drunk butterman doubts that a City clerk can be a gentleman. The ironmonger who botched the scraper turns out to be on back-slapping terms with a sheriff. Pooter looks down on tradesmen and errand-boys and is looked down on by his son, his son’s friends and the stockbroking world that is making the real money.

Through the book Borset in chapter I; Merton on Society in III; Farmerson at the Mansion House in IV; Posh’s three-shilling hats in XV; the six-hundred-pound loss in XVI; Lupin’s two hundred a year in XXI; and one radical thought in the carriage in XXIII.

In the text

I restrained my feelings, and quietly remarked that I thought it was possible for a city clerk to be a gentleman. He replied he was very glad to hear it, and wanted to know whether I had ever come across one, for he hadn’t.

Chapter I, April 6

This is the book’s subject stated in the first chapter by a man delivering butter. Pooter’s sentence is a small feat of self-defence, with four hedges in one line (‘restrained’, ‘quietly’, ‘remarked’, ‘thought it was possible’), so that the claim is barely made before it is knocked down. The riposte works because it accepts his terms and simply denies the evidence. Two sentences later he hears Borset fall over the scraper and is glad he never had it moved, and the entry ends with him thinking of a splendid answer he ought to have given, which he keeps for another occasion and never uses.

On the way home in the carriage, for the first time in my life, I was inclined to indulge in the radical thought that money was not properly divided.

Chapter XXIII, July 4

One sentence of political doubt in a book with no politics, and it is placed with great care: in a rich man’s carriage, going back to a rented house in Holloway, at the end of an evening in which a necklace costing three hundred pounds has been mentioned. The hedging is the whole character: ‘inclined to indulge in’, and the word ‘radical’ held at arm’s length, as if the thought belonged to somebody disreputable. He does not develop it. The next paragraph is about a cab waiting outside his house, and the idea never appears again. Six days later he is given a house and the question is closed.

The Laurels

The house is the book’s real subject: six rooms and a basement, rented, with the railway at the bottom of the garden and a wall already cracking. Pooter hammers, paints, plants mustard-and-cress, repairs the cornice and alters the stair-carpets, and half the comedy comes from the house resisting. He is happiest doing this and says so plainly. The ending hands him the freehold, which is either the reward the diary has earned or the proof that his life was always in another man’s gift.

Through the book The tin-tack and the Venetian blind in chapter I; the enamel paint in III; the chimney-glass bought with the rise in XIV; the cistern and the cornice in XVIII and XXII; Lupin’s opinion that Brickfield Terrace is a bit off in XXIII; and Perkupp’s gift in Chapter the Last.

In the text

After professing to snub Mr. Farmerson, you permit him to snub you, in my presence, and then accept his invitation to take a glass of champagne with you, and you don’t limit yourself to one glass. You then offer this vulgar man, who made a bungle of repairing our scraper, a seat in our cab on the way home.

Caroline Pooter, Chapter V, May 9

This is the only sustained attack on Pooter delivered by somebody who loves him, and it is the only one he transcribes in full. Carrie’s method is a list in chronological order with no adjectives except vulgar, which is the word he would have used himself, and the sting is that every item is true and the reader has already seen it happen. The entry then does the thing that makes the book more than a set of jokes: he writes down the whole speech, calls himself humiliated, and does not answer it. Gowing comes in wearing two hats and calls it a lovers’ quarrel.

I am getting quite accustomed to being snubbed by Lupin, and I do not mind being sat upon by Carrie, because I think she has a certain amount of right to do so; but I do think it hard to be at once snubbed by wife, son, and both my guests.

Chapter XIX, April 29

The sentence is a man drawing up the terms of his own defeat and finding them, on the whole, acceptable. The concession to Carrie is the heart of it (‘she has a certain amount of right to do so’), and it is a joke about a henpecked husband and also an honest statement of how he sees his marriage. The snubbing he can take; what he objects to is the arithmetic, since four ‘at once’ is too many. The dream itself, blocks of ice on fire, is the single imaginative thing he produces in the book, and it is exactly what nobody will let him have.

Work at Perkupp’s

Pooter’s standing at the office is his whole claim on the world, and he states its terms twice: twenty-one years of industry and strict attention to the interests of his superiors. The book sets that against Lupin, who arrives late, takes no interest and is paid better for it, and against a market in which a tip on chlorates can make two hundred pounds in a day. Nothing in the diary argues that Pooter is wrong to have worked as he has. It simply keeps the two accounts side by side.

Through the book Pitt’s insolence in chapter II; the hundred-pound rise and Lupin’s parody of the announcement in XIV; the collapse of the chlorates in XVI; the sixteen-page letter to Crowbillon in XXI; and Gylterson’s offer of two hundred a year to a young man who has been at it a few weeks.

In the text

Imagine my dismay when he replied with a loud guffaw: “It’s no use. If you want the good old truth, I’ve got the chuck!”

Lupin Pooter, Chapter VI, August 6

Two Englishes collide in one exchange. The father’s is formal and futile, the language of a man who believes an employer can be written to. The son’s is four syllables of slang that cancel the whole speech. The guffaw matters: Lupin is not ashamed, and the reader can see what Pooter cannot, that the resignation was a face-saving lie which his father’s pomposity has just forced him to drop. Five days later Pooter records that being sacked for idleness is, on the whole, satisfactory.

My boy, as a result of twenty-one years’ industry and strict attention to the interests of my superiors in office, I have been rewarded with promotion and a rise in salary of £100. … “Having been in the firm of Job Cleanands, stock and share-brokers, a few weeks, and not having paid particular attention to the interests of my superiors in office, my Guv’nor, as a reward to me, allotted me £5 worth of shares in a really good thing. The result is, to-day I have made £200.”

Chapter XIV, January 5

The best-built joke in the book, and it belongs to Lupin. He stands up, takes his father’s exact syntax and swaps in his own facts: twenty-one years becomes a few weeks, strict attention becomes not having paid particular attention, a hundred pounds a year becomes two hundred in a day. Nothing is said about what the parody means. The two sentences are left side by side for the reader to compare.

Household objects

Objects in Holloway are hostile and very nearly animate. A boot-scraper trips four separate callers and is finally moved by a man who bores through the gas-pipe. A bell rings in the wrong room, then has no handle, then does not exist when it is needed. Enamel paint dissolves in a hot bath. A stylographic pen oozes ink and gets somebody else blamed. The pattern lets Pooter keep his dignity by blaming the equipment, and it is also an exact account of a household run on a small budget.

Through the book The scraper and the bells through chapters I and II; the red enamel through III; Gowing’s stick in V; the antimacassar in VII; the blanc-mange in X; the stylographic pen and the cistern in XVIII.

In the text

My first thought was that I had ruptured an artery, and was bleeding to death, and should be discovered, later on, looking like a second Marat, as I remember seeing him in Madame Tussaud’s. My second thought was to ring the bell, but remembered there was no bell to ring. My third was, that there was nothing but the enamel paint, which had dissolved with boiling water.

Chapter III, April 29

The paragraph counts down from tragedy to household paint: first, second, third, each thought smaller than the last. The middle step is the one to watch: the bell that has been broken since the first page, so that even the melodrama cannot be rung for. Marat is exactly the right reference for him: he knows the murder from a waxwork, which is how the whole of Pooter’s culture reaches him. Then the paragraph ends with the paint, and the business of the morning: he will tell Farmerson to come and paint the bath white.

Friendship

Gowing and Cummings come in without knocking, eat the supper, mock the host, and are the fixed points of his week. Nobody in the book says a word about affection. What holds the three together is habit and the side entrance, and the diary keeps score: who called, who did not, who was ill and not visited, whose stick was ruined. It is a truthful picture of male friendship kept up for twenty years on very little, and the book is generous enough to make it look worth having.

Through the book The walk to the Cow and Hedge in chapter II; the letters of forgiveness in II; the blackened stick in V; the party at which they eat as though starved in IX; Cummings’s reproaches in XIV and XVIII; and the telegrams sent on the last day.

In the text

I thought I was the aggrieved party; but as I freely forgive you, you—feeling yourself aggrieved—should bestow forgiveness on me. … I have copied this verbatim in the diary, because I think it is one of the most perfect and thoughtful sentences I have ever written. I posted the letter, but in my own heart I felt I was actually apologising for having been insulted.

Chapter II, April 17

The entry makes three movements, and each cancels the one before. The sentence itself is a knot: a forgiveness that requires forgiving back, so that the injured party ends by asking pardon. Then comes the vanity, and the reason the reader gets to see the knot at all: he has copied it out because he is proud of it. Then the last line, which is the sharpest thing in the first half of the book, because it shows he knows. The diary form does all the work here. No other shape would let a man record his own cleverness and his own defeat in the same paragraph, in that order.

When I returned to the parlour, Gowing was retailing his idiotic joke about the odd sock, and Carrie was roaring with laughter. I suppose I am losing my sense of humour.

Chapter XI, November 25

This is the saddest line in the book, and it is a joke. Gowing’s remark is no better than anything Pooter has produced, arguably worse, and the difference is entirely in the delivery and in who is making it. Pooter’s conclusion is exactly the wrong one, since his sense of humour is unimpaired; what has failed is his standing. The verb ‘retailing’ carries the grievance: Gowing is selling the joke round the house, and Carrie, who has laughed at dry rot until her sides ached, is buying. Then Pooter goes back to complaining about Padge, which is what a man does when he cannot say the true thing.
