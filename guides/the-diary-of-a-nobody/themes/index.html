The Diary of a Nobody · George and Weedon Grossmith

Themes

Pooter’s self-portrait

Pooter never lies. He reports what was said and done, including the things said to him, and then adds his own view of it, and the two do not fit. He writes out an insult word for word and calls it a compliment; he copies a sentence into the diary because he thinks it perfect, and admits three lines later that it was an apology for having been insulted. The comedy needs him to be scrupulous. A dishonest diarist would simply leave the defeats out.

The curate’s request to take round the plate, recorded as a great compliment, in chapter I; the forgiveness letter in II; the joke about Gowing and Cummings in III; the Mansion House in IV; the odd sock in XI; the bill for civility in XVIII; and a chapter of Hardfur Huttle describing him to his face in XX.

Why should I not publish my diary? I have often seen reminiscences of people I have never even heard of, and I fail to see—because I do not happen to be a ‘Somebody’—why my diary should not be interesting.

Charles Pooter, Introduction by Mr. Pooter

I don’t often make jokes, but I replied: “You’re talking a lot of dry rot yourself.” I could not help roaring at this, and Carrie said her sides quite ached with laughter. I never was so immensely tickled by anything I have ever said before. I actually woke up twice during the night, and laughed till the bed shook.

Chapter II, April 12

Respectability

Everything Pooter does is aimed at a standard he cannot quite reach and cannot afford: a front door kept locked and used only on special occasions, lavender gloves, two white ties in case one is spoiled, visiting cards ordered twenty-five at a time. The joke is less about snobbery than about the standard itself, which is a trap: every attempt to meet it produces the accident that ruins it. Huttle names the cost in one sentence, and Pooter cannot argue.

The front door that will not open in chapter I; the dress-coat the tailor takes back for want of a shilling in IV; the paragraph about Charles Pewter in V; the party in IX; the three-pound supper bill in XVIII; and the definition of the happy medium in XX.

Why should I not publish my diary? I have often seen reminiscences of people I have never even heard of, and I fail to see—because I do not happen to be a ‘Somebody’—why my diary should not be interesting.

Charles Pooter, Introduction by Mr. Pooter

I restrained my feelings, and quietly remarked that I thought it was possible for a city clerk to be a gentleman. He replied he was very glad to hear it, and wanted to know whether I had ever come across one, for he hadn’t.

Chapter I, April 6

Class

The diary is exact about money (two pounds off the rent, seven-and-six for a stick, thirty-eight shillings for a dozen of whisky, a hundred a year) and exact about rank, which is why the two keep colliding. A drunk butterman doubts that a City clerk can be a gentleman. The ironmonger who botched the scraper turns out to be on back-slapping terms with a sheriff. Pooter looks down on tradesmen and errand-boys and is looked down on by his son, his son’s friends and the stockbroking world that is making the real money.

Borset in chapter I; Merton on Society in III; Farmerson at the Mansion House in IV; Posh’s three-shilling hats in XV; the six-hundred-pound loss in XVI; Lupin’s two hundred a year in XXI; and one radical thought in the carriage in XXIII.

Chapter I, April 6

I simply looked at him, and said coolly: “I never expected to see you here.” He said, with a loud, coarse laugh: “I like that—if you, why not me?” … Farmerson seized me rather roughly by the collar, and addressing the sheriff, said: “Let me introduce my neighbour, Pooter.” He did not even say “Mister.”

Chapter IV, May 7

The Laurels

The house is the book’s real subject: six rooms and a basement, rented, with the railway at the bottom of the garden and a wall already cracking. Pooter hammers, paints, plants mustard-and-cress, repairs the cornice and alters the stair-carpets, and half the comedy comes from the house resisting. He is happiest doing this and says so plainly. The ending hands him the freehold, which is either the reward the diary has earned or the proof that his life was always in another man’s gift.

The tin-tack and the Venetian blind in chapter I; the enamel paint in III; the chimney-glass bought with the rise in XIV; the cistern and the cornice in XVIII and XXII; Lupin’s opinion that Brickfield Terrace is a bit off in XXIII; and Perkupp’s gift in Chapter the Last.

Chapter II, April 12

My first thought was that I had ruptured an artery, and was bleeding to death, and should be discovered, later on, looking like a second Marat, as I remember seeing him in Madame Tussaud’s. My second thought was to ring the bell, but remembered there was no bell to ring. My third was, that there was nothing but the enamel paint, which had dissolved with boiling water.

Chapter III, April 29

Work at Perkupp’s

Pooter’s standing at the office is his whole claim on the world, and he states its terms twice: twenty-one years of industry and strict attention to the interests of his superiors. The book sets that against Lupin, who arrives late, takes no interest and is paid better for it, and against a market in which a tip on chlorates can make two hundred pounds in a day. Nothing in the diary argues that Pooter is wrong to have worked as he has. It simply keeps the two accounts side by side.

Pitt’s insolence in chapter II; the hundred-pound rise and Lupin’s parody of the announcement in XIV; the collapse of the chlorates in XVI; the sixteen-page letter to Crowbillon in XXI; and Gylterson’s offer of two hundred a year to a young man who has been at it a few weeks.

Imagine my dismay when he replied with a loud guffaw: “It’s no use. If you want the good old truth, I’ve got the chuck!”

Lupin Pooter, Chapter VI, August 6

My boy, as a result of twenty-one years’ industry and strict attention to the interests of my superiors in office, I have been rewarded with promotion and a rise in salary of £100. … “Having been in the firm of Job Cleanands, stock and share-brokers, a few weeks, and not having paid particular attention to the interests of my superiors in office, my Guv’nor, as a reward to me, allotted me £5 worth of shares in a really good thing. The result is, to-day I have made £200.”

Chapter XIV, January 5

Household objects

Objects in Holloway are hostile and very nearly animate. A boot-scraper trips four separate callers and is finally moved by a man who bores through the gas-pipe. A bell rings in the wrong room, then has no handle, then does not exist when it is needed. Enamel paint dissolves in a hot bath. A stylographic pen oozes ink and gets somebody else blamed. The pattern lets Pooter keep his dignity by blaming the equipment, and it is also an exact account of a household run on a small budget.

The scraper and the bells through chapters I and II; the red enamel through III; Gowing’s stick in V; the antimacassar in VII; the blanc-mange in X; the stylographic pen and the cistern in XVIII.

My first thought was that I had ruptured an artery, and was bleeding to death, and should be discovered, later on, looking like a second Marat, as I remember seeing him in Madame Tussaud’s. My second thought was to ring the bell, but remembered there was no bell to ring. My third was, that there was nothing but the enamel paint, which had dissolved with boiling water.

Chapter III, April 29

I was a little vexed at everybody subsequently laughing at some joke which they did not explain, and it was only on going to bed I discovered I must have been walking about all the evening with an antimacassar on one button of my coat-tails.

Chapter VII, August 27

Friendship

Gowing and Cummings come in without knocking, eat the supper, mock the host, and are the fixed points of his week. Nobody in the book says a word about affection. What holds the three together is habit and the side entrance, and the diary keeps score: who called, who did not, who was ill and not visited, whose stick was ruined. It is a truthful picture of male friendship kept up for twenty years on very little, and the book is generous enough to make it look worth having.

The walk to the Cow and Hedge in chapter II; the letters of forgiveness in II; the blackened stick in V; the party at which they eat as though starved in IX; Cummings’s reproaches in XIV and XVIII; and the telegrams sent on the last day.

I thought I was the aggrieved party; but as I freely forgive you, you—feeling yourself aggrieved—should bestow forgiveness on me. … I have copied this verbatim in the diary, because I think it is one of the most perfect and thoughtful sentences I have ever written. I posted the letter, but in my own heart I felt I was actually apologising for having been insulted.

Chapter II, April 17

It’s concerning you both; for doesn’t it seem odd that Gowing’s always coming and Cummings’ always going? … I think this was one of the best jokes I have ever made.

Charles Pooter, Chapter III, April 27
