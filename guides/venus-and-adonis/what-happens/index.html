Venus and Adonis · William Shakespeare

What happens

Lines 1–258 The first wooing

In the early morning Adonis sets out to hunt, and Venus, sick with desire, pulls him from his horse, ties the horse to a branch and pushes him to the ground. She kisses him, argues with him and weeps. She tells him the god of war was once her captive, offers her body as a park for him to graze in, warns him that beauty which is not used will rot, and tells him he has a duty to breed. He blushes, frowns and says little, complains that the sun is burning his face, and as soon as she loosens her hold he makes for his horse.

Lines 1–6
Adonis loves hunting and laughs at love; Venus comes at him like a bold suitor.
Lines 25–60
She tethers his horse, pushes him down and kisses him like a hungry eagle.
Lines 97–114
The god of war was her captive once, led in a chain of roses.
Lines 163–174
Torches are made to light, and beauty is made to be passed on.
Lines 229–240
She offers herself as a park and him as her deer.

Lines 259–324 The horse and the jennet

A breeding mare comes out of a copse. Adonis’s horse breaks his rein and his girths and goes after her, and the narrator describes him point by point, as a painter would. The mare plays cold and kicks at him, then grows kinder, and the two gallop off into the wood together, leaving Adonis on foot.

Lines 259–300
The horse breaks free for the mare, and the narrator paints him feature by feature.
Lines 301–324
The mare is cold, then kind, and the two run off together.

Lines 325–588 The second wooing

Adonis sits down in a temper, and Venus tries again. She kneels, takes his hand, and at last he speaks, telling her to let him go. She holds up the runaway horse as an example of natural desire; he answers that a colt broken too young never grows strong. When he frowns she falls as if dead, and he slaps her cheeks, chafes her lips and kisses her to bring her round. Night comes. He agrees to a goodnight kiss, she pulls him down and kisses him until he is exhausted, and when she asks to meet tomorrow he tells her he is going to hunt the boar.

Lines 325–384
Venus kneels, takes his hand, and Adonis finally speaks.
Lines 463–480
She falls as if dead, and he revives her with a kiss.
Lines 529–588
A parting kiss, a struggle, and the news of tomorrow’s hunt.

Lines 589–816 The boar

At the word boar Venus turns pale and pulls him down on top of her. She describes the boar’s tusks and bristles, imagines Adonis dead beneath it, and begs him to hunt the hare instead, telling the story of poor Wat doubling back to escape the hounds. Then she returns to her argument that beauty must breed. Adonis answers that what she calls love is lust, which has taken love’s name, and he runs off home through the dark.

Lines 589–672
Venus’s fear, the tusks, jealousy the informer, and a vision of Adonis dead.
Lines 673–708
The hunted hare, doubling back to escape the hounds.
Lines 769–816
Adonis answers at length and runs home in the dark.

Lines 817–1026 Night and the hunt

Venus spends the night alone lamenting, answered only by echoes. At dawn she hears the hounds at bay and knows they have found a dangerous quarry. She sees the boar with its mouth red, finds wounded hounds, and curses Death. A distant huntsman’s call gives her hope, and she takes back everything she said about Death and blames the boar instead.

Lines 817–858
Echoes all night, and the lark at dawn.
Lines 877–954
The boar’s red mouth, the wounded hounds, and Venus’s curse on Death.
Lines 985–1026
She takes back the curse, flatters Death, and blames the boar.

Lines 1027–1194 Death and the flower

Running towards a horn, she finds Adonis gored in the flank by the boar. She laments over him, imagines that the boar meant only to kiss him, and prophesies that love will from now on be attended by sorrow. His body melts away, and a purple flower chequered with white springs from his blood. Venus picks it, says she will keep it in her breast, and flies to Paphos in her chariot drawn by doves.

Lines 1027–1122
She finds him gored and imagines the boar meant to kiss him.
Lines 1135–1164
Sorrow on love hereafter shall attend.
Lines 1165–1194
A purple flower from his blood, and Venus’s flight to Paphos.
