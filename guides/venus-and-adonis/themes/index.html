Desire and refusal

Venus’s desire is enormous and inventive, and it meets a steady no. She pulls Adonis from his horse, holds him down and kisses him while he tries to leave, and the poem never lets us forget his refusal, however comic her persistence becomes.

Through the poem Only the horses show desire returned, and Venus’s use of them as an argument does not persuade Adonis.

In the text

Ten kisses short as one, one long as twenty:

Venus, line 22

Venus offers to make time behave for them, with ten kisses as short as one and one as long as twenty. The mirrored numbers show her gift for rhetoric, which she uses all through the poem without ever getting what she wants.

Round-hoof’d, short-jointed, fetlocks shag and long, Broad breast, full eye, small head, and nostril wide,

Narrator, lines 295–296

The horse gets the feature-by-feature praise a sonnet gives a mistress. It also reads like a horse-dealer’s checklist, and the stanza ends by noting that he lacks only a proud rider on his back.

The wooer and the wooed

The poem reverses the usual roles of love poetry. A mature goddess woos a young man with the arguments men used on women, and he blushes, sulks and pleads to be let go, as the lady of a sonnet might.

Through the poem The reversal supplies much of the comedy, and it also lets readers see familiar methods of courtship from the other side.

In the text

Sick-thoughted Venus makes amain unto him, And like a bold-fac’d suitor ’gins to woo him.

Narrator, lines 5–6

The first couplet makes the goddess the wooer and the boy the wooed. A bold-faced suitor was a man’s part, and the two-syllable rhyme of unto him and woo him gives the poem a comic lilt from the start.

Her lips are conquerors, his lips obey, Paying what ransom the insulter willeth;

Narrator, lines 549–550

The kiss becomes a military occupation, with Venus as the conqueror and Adonis paying ransom. An insulter was one who triumphs over a beaten enemy.

Love against lust

Venus calls what she feels love, and Adonis calls it lust, which has usurped love’s name. His speech on the difference, with love like sunshine after rain and lust like tempest after sun, is the poem’s clearest statement of the argument.

Through the poem Venus’s prophecy at the end concedes part of his case: love from now on will be jealous, deceitful and mixed with sorrow.

In the text

Love is a spirit all compact of fire, Not gross to sink, but light, and will aspire.

Venus, lines 149–150

Venus describes love as the lightest of the elements, rising like flame. The poem’s picture of her, sweating, heavy with desire and holding a boy down, makes the claim hard to accept, and in the next stanza she lies on a primrose bank to prove it.

She’s love, she loves, and yet she is not lov’d.

Narrator, line 610

The line uses love three ways: her name, her action and what she cannot get. It sums up both the comedy and the pathos of the poem, in which the goddess of love is the one figure who is not loved.

Beauty, breeding and time

Venus argues that beauty must be used before it fades and that Adonis owes the world a child, the same case the first seventeen of Shakespeare’s sonnets make to a young man. Adonis answers that he is unripe fruit.

Through the poem The boar ends the argument. The beauty Venus urged him to pass on dies with him, and she says black Chaos comes again.

In the text

Seeds spring from seeds, and beauty breedeth beauty; Thou wast begot; to get it is thy duty.

Venus, lines 167–168

Here Venus makes the case Shakespeare’s first seventeen sonnets make to a young man: beauty must be passed on to a child or it is wasted. With the pun on begot and get, being born is a debt.

The colt that’s back’d and burden’d being young, Loseth his pride, and never waxeth strong.

Adonis, lines 419–420

Adonis answers the horse with a horse of his own. He argues that he is too young, like a colt broken to the saddle too early, and turns Venus’s example of animal nature against her.

The hunter hunted

Adonis wants to hunt, and Venus hunts him. The poem is full of predators and prey: an eagle tearing at its catch, a bird in a net, a deer in a park, the hare Wat doubling back from the hounds, and the boar.

Through the poem The hunter ends as the quarry, gored by the boar he chose over Venus, and she imagines the tusk as a kiss.

In the text

Rose-cheeked Adonis hied him to the chase. Hunting he loved, but love he laughed to scorn.

Narrator, lines 3–4

The second line turns on itself: he loved hunting and scorned love. The whole poem follows from that reversal, since Adonis will be pursued by the love he laughs at and killed by the hunt he prefers.

By this, poor Wat, far off upon a hill, Stands on his hinder legs with list’ning ear,

Venus, lines 697–698

Wat was a country name for the hare. Venus means the story as a lesson in safe hunting, but she tells it from the hare’s side, and Adonis will end like the hare, as the hunted one.

Grief and loss

The last third of the poem is grief: a night of lament answered only by echoes, a curse on Death taken back as soon as hope returns, and the discovery of the body. Her feelings swing so violently that the narrator calls love ridiculous in its despair and hope.

Through the poem Her grief ends in withdrawal: she keeps the flower in her breast and flies to Paphos to hide.

In the text

She clepes him king of graves, and grave for kings, Imperious supreme of all mortal things.

Narrator, lines 995–996

Clepes means calls. The reversed phrase king of graves, and grave for kings shows how quickly Venus’s rhetoric can change direction when her hopes do.

So at his bloody view her eyes are fled Into the deep dark cabins of her head.

Narrator, lines 1037–1038

The stanza before compares her eyes to a snail’s tender horns, which shrink back into the shell when touched. The couplet completes the comparison, and the small, exact image slows the poem at the moment of discovery, before the grief breaks out.

Transformation

As in Ovid, the story ends in a metamorphosis. Adonis’s body melts away and a purple and white flower springs from his blood, and Venus treats the flower as his child and keeps it in her breast.

Through the poem Shakespeare adds a second transformation, of love itself, which after Venus’s prophecy will always be mixed with sorrow.

In the text

They bid thee crop a weed, thou pluck’st a flower.

Venus, line 946

Venus accuses Death of picking the wrong plant. The line anticipates the ending, where Adonis really does become a flower and Venus plucks it.

And in his blood that on the ground lay spill’d, A purple flower sprung up, chequer’d with white,

Narrator, lines 1167–1168

His red and white, the blush and pallor the poem has dwelt on since its first stanzas, become the colours of a flower. In Ovid, Venus sprinkles nectar on the blood to raise the flower; here it springs up by itself.
