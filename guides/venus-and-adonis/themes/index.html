Venus and Adonis · William Shakespeare

Themes

Desire and refusal

Venus’s desire is enormous and inventive, and it meets a steady no. She pulls Adonis from his horse, holds him down and kisses him while he tries to leave, and the poem never lets us forget his refusal, however comic her persistence becomes.

Only the horses show desire returned, and Venus’s use of them as an argument does not persuade Adonis.

Rose-cheeked Adonis hied him to the chase. Hunting he loved, but love he laughed to scorn.

Narrator, lines 3–4

Sick-thoughted Venus makes amain unto him, And like a bold-fac’d suitor ’gins to woo him.

Narrator, lines 5–6

The wooer and the wooed

The poem reverses the usual roles of love poetry. A mature goddess woos a young man with the arguments men used on women, and he blushes, sulks and pleads to be let go, as the lady of a sonnet might.

The reversal supplies much of the comedy, and it also lets readers see familiar methods of courtship from the other side.

Sick-thoughted Venus makes amain unto him, And like a bold-fac’d suitor ’gins to woo him.

Narrator, lines 5–6

She red and hot as coals of glowing fire, He red for shame, but frosty in desire.

Narrator, lines 35–36

Love against lust

Venus calls what she feels love, and Adonis calls it lust, which has usurped love’s name. His speech on the difference, with love like sunshine after rain and lust like tempest after sun, is the poem’s clearest statement of the argument.

Venus’s prophecy at the end concedes part of his case: love from now on will be jealous, deceitful and mixed with sorrow.

Love is a spirit all compact of fire, Not gross to sink, but light, and will aspire.

Venus, lines 149–150

She’s love, she loves, and yet she is not lov’d.

Narrator, line 610

Beauty, breeding and time

Venus argues that beauty must be used before it fades and that Adonis owes the world a child, the same case the first seventeen of Shakespeare’s sonnets make to a young man. Adonis answers that he is unripe fruit.

The boar ends the argument. The beauty Venus urged him to pass on dies with him, and she says black Chaos comes again.

Narcissus so himself himself forsook, And died to kiss his shadow in the brook.

Venus, lines 161–162

Seeds spring from seeds, and beauty breedeth beauty; Thou wast begot; to get it is thy duty.

Venus, lines 167–168

The hunter hunted

Adonis wants to hunt, and Venus hunts him. The poem is full of predators and prey: an eagle tearing at its catch, a bird in a net, a deer in a park, the hare Wat doubling back from the hounds, and the boar.

The hunter ends as the quarry, gored by the boar he chose over Venus, and she imagines the tusk as a kiss.

Rose-cheeked Adonis hied him to the chase. Hunting he loved, but love he laughed to scorn.

Narrator, lines 3–4

Even as an empty eagle, sharp by fast, Tires with her beak on feathers, flesh and bone,

Narrator, lines 55–56

Grief and loss

The last third of the poem is grief: a night of lament answered only by echoes, a curse on Death taken back as soon as hope returns, and the discovery of the body. Her feelings swing so violently that the narrator calls love ridiculous in its despair and hope.

Her grief ends in withdrawal: she keeps the flower in her breast and flies to Paphos to hide.

Look how a bright star shooteth from the sky, So glides he in the night from Venus’ eye.

Narrator, lines 815–816

She clepes him king of graves, and grave for kings, Imperious supreme of all mortal things.

Narrator, lines 995–996

Transformation

As in Ovid, the story ends in a metamorphosis. Adonis’s body melts away and a purple and white flower springs from his blood, and Venus treats the flower as his child and keeps it in her breast.

Shakespeare adds a second transformation, of love itself, which after Venus’s prophecy will always be mixed with sorrow.

They bid thee crop a weed, thou pluck’st a flower.

Venus, line 946

And in his blood that on the ground lay spill’d, A purple flower sprung up, chequer’d with white,

Narrator, lines 1167–1168
