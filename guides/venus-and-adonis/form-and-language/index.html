Venus and Adonis · William Shakespeare

Form and language

The six-line stanza

A stanza of four alternately rhyming lines followed by a rhyming couplet (ababcc).

All 199 stanzas use it. The first four lines set out a picture or an argument and the couplet turns it, often with a joke, so the poem moves forward six lines at a time.

Sick-thoughted Venus makes amain unto him, And like a bold-fac’d suitor ’gins to woo him.

Narrator, lines 5–6

Extended simile

A comparison worked out over several lines.

The narrator compares Venus to a starving eagle, Adonis to a bird in a net and a dive-dapper ducking under a wave, and Venus’s eyes to a snail’s horns shrinking back, often introducing the comparison with Look how.

Even as an empty eagle, sharp by fast, Tires with her beak on feathers, flesh and bone,

Narrator, lines 55–56

The blazon

A list praising a body feature by feature.

Venus lists her own perfections to Adonis, and the narrator lists the horse’s, from round hoof to tender hide, in the same form.

Round-hoof’d, short-jointed, fetlocks shag and long, Broad breast, full eye, small head, and nostril wide,

Narrator, lines 295–296

Apostrophe

Addressing someone absent or dead, or a thing or idea, as if it could hear.

Venus harangues Death as a grim-grinning ghost and then apologises to it. She also addresses the rising sun, and at the end the flower.

They bid thee crop a weed, thou pluck’st a flower.

Venus, line 946

Personification

Giving human qualities to an idea or thing.

Jealousy is an informer and a spy, the sun wishes he were in Adonis’s place beside Venus, and Venus tells Adonis that if he falls in the dark it will be the earth, in love with him, tripping him for a kiss.

This sour informer, this bate-breeding spy, This canker that eats up love’s tender spring,

Venus, lines 655–656

Classical allusion

A reference to the myths and literature of Greece and Rome.

The whole poem retells Ovid, and Venus’s arguments call on Mars and Narcissus, and the narrator finds her worse off than Tantalus. It ends with her flight to Paphos, her sacred city.

Leading him prisoner in a red rose chain:

Venus, line 110

Antithesis

Opposed ideas set against each other in balanced phrases.

The argument is built from oppositions: heat and frost, red for desire and red for shame, love and lust, sunshine and tempest.

Rose-cheeked Adonis hied him to the chase. Hunting he loved, but love he laughed to scorn.

Narrator, lines 3–4

Extended conceit

A comparison developed at length, often between unlike things.

Venus maps her body as a deer park with hills, fountains and brakes, and the goodnight kiss becomes a siege in which her lips conquer and his pay ransom.

I’ll be a park, and thou shalt be my deer;

Venus, line 231

Animal imagery

Comparisons between people and animals.

Eagle, dive-dapper, bird, deer, colt, hare, snail and boar all stand in for the two lovers, and the horses act out the desire Adonis refuses.

Even as an empty eagle, sharp by fast, Tires with her beak on feathers, flesh and bone,

Narrator, lines 55–56

Red and white

Colours used as a code for feeling, especially the blush and the pallor of a face.

Adonis blushes red with shame and Venus with desire, she turns pale at the word boar, his wound drenches lily white in purple, and the flower that grows from his blood is purple chequered with white.

She red and hot as coals of glowing fire, He red for shame, but frosty in desire.

Narrator, lines 35–36

Prophecy

A prediction spoken within the work.

Venus’s curse at the end, stanza after stanza beginning It shall, foretells that love will always be joined with jealousy, deceit and sorrow.

Since thou art dead, lo here I prophesy, Sorrow on love hereafter shall attend:

Venus, lines 1135–1136

Wordplay

Puns and double meanings.

Deer and dear, begot and get, will three times in one couplet, and ‘king of graves’ reversed as ‘grave for kings’.

Seeds spring from seeds, and beauty breedeth beauty; Thou wast begot; to get it is thy duty.

Venus, lines 167–168
