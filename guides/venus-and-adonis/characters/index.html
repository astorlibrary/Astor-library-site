Venus and Adonis · William Shakespeare

Characters

Venus

Goddess of love; first appears Line 5

The goddess of love, who cannot make one boy love her. She pulls Adonis from his horse, holds him down, kisses him, and argues with every means she has: flattery, tears, a faint, the example of Mars, and the claim that beauty must breed. When he is killed she grieves, prophesies that love will always be joined with sorrow, and takes the flower that grows from his blood.

Adonis

A young hunter; first appears Line 3

A beautiful young man who loves hunting and laughs at love. He blushes, frowns and says little, and when he does speak he argues that he is too young and that what Venus calls love is lust. He goes to hunt the boar against her warning and is killed by it, and his blood becomes a purple and white flower.

The narrator

The voice telling the story; first appears Line 1

The voice that tells the story, with relish and some irony. The narrator pities and teases Venus, draws long similes from hunting and country life, and describes Adonis’s horse as a painter would.

The stallion

Adonis’s horse; first appears Line 13

Tied to a branch while Venus woos his master, he breaks his rein at the sight of a breeding mare, chases her and runs off with her into the wood. He does everything Adonis will not, and Venus holds him up as an example.

The jennet

A breeding mare; first appears Line 260

She comes out of a copse and attracts Adonis’s horse. At first she puts on a show of coldness and kicks at him, then grows kinder and runs off into the wood with him.

The boar

The beast that kills Adonis; first appears Line 410

Venus describes him before he appears: a battle of bristles on his back, tusks that are never sheathed, eyes like glow-worms, and a snout that digs graves. At dawn his mouth is red with blood, and he has gored Adonis in the groin. Venus imagines that he only meant to kiss him.

Wat

The hare in Venus’s story; first appears Line 697

The hare Venus describes when she begs Adonis to hunt something harmless. Chased by hounds, he doubles back and runs among sheep, rabbits and deer to hide his scent, and stops on a hill to listen like a sick man hearing the passing bell.

Death

Addressed by Venus; first appears Line 931

Finding the hounds wounded, Venus turns on Death as a grim-grinning ghost that has killed Adonis by mistake, aiming at old age and hitting a child. When she hears a huntsman and hopes Adonis is alive, she begs Death’s pardon and flatters him as king of graves.
