Moby-Dick · Herman Melville

Themes

Monomania

The book names Ahab’s condition exactly: one idea has taken the place of every other, and the rest of the mind still works normally in its service. That is why he is so persuasive. The danger is that he can run a ship, read a chart and win an argument while raving.

Shown as leadership on the quarter-deck in Chapter 36, traced to its injury in Chapter 41, and finally described in Chapter 132 as something that commands him.

Delight is to him⁠—a far, far upward, and inward delight⁠—who against the proud gods and commodores of this earth, ever stands forth his own inexorable self.

Father Mapple, Chapter 9

“I will have no man in my boat,” said Starbuck, “who is not afraid of a whale.”

Starbuck, Chapter 26

Knowledge and its failures

Ishmael classifies whales as if they were books, measures a skeleton, reads pictures, quotes authorities and admits at every stage that the system does not hold. The novel is an encyclopaedia that keeps publishing its own errata, and the joke is serious: the thing being described is the one thing that will not sit still for description.

From the Etymology and Extracts before the story begins, through the mock-bibliographic Cetology of Chapter 32, to the flat statement in Chapter 86 that the whale’s face cannot be read at all.

Call me Ishmael. Some years ago⁠—never mind how long precisely⁠—having little or no money in my purse, and nothing particular to interest me on shore, I thought I would sail about a little and see the watery part of the world.

But as in landlessness alone resides highest truth, shoreless, indefinite as God⁠—so, better is it to perish in that howling infinite, than be ingloriously dashed upon the lee, even if that were safety!

Whiteness and meaning

A whole chapter is given to a colour, and it concludes that white terrifies because it is the absence of colour and also the sum of all colours. That is a statement about interpretation, more than about whales: the novel’s central object is blank enough to hold whatever is put into it, and that is the danger.

Chapter 42 sets the problem; the doubloon in Chapter 99 demonstrates it, with one man after another, from Ahab to Pip, reading the same small coin his own way.

“Vengeance on a dumb brute!” cried Starbuck, “that simply smote thee from blindest instinct! Madness! To be enraged with a dumb thing, Captain Ahab, seems blasphemous.”

Starbuck, Chapter 36

All visible objects, man, are but as pasteboard masks. But in each event⁠—in the living act, the undoubted deed⁠—there, some unknown but still reasoning thing puts forth the mouldings of its features from behind the unreasoning mask. If man will strike, strike through the mask!

Ahab, Chapter 36

Work at sea

Before the Pequod is a symbol it is a factory. Melville itemises the lay system that pays a sailor in fractions, the tubs of line that can take a man’s arm off, the stink of the try-works and the market price of oil. Ahab’s revenge is only possible because he controls somebody else’s capital and thirty men’s wages.

Established in the bargaining of Chapter 16, sustained through the cutting-in chapters, and turned political in Chapter 89, where the law of fast-fish and loose-fish is applied to empires.

Who ain’t a slave? Tell me that.

Fellowship

The warmest passages in the book are about shared work and shared beds: two strangers under one counterpane, a monkey-rope tying two lives together, a crew squeezing lumps of sperm until they are squeezing each other’s hands. Against this the novel sets a captain who cannot bear to be looked at.

Begins as comedy in the Spouter-Inn, becomes a working principle in the monkey-rope chapter, and reaches its clearest statement in Chapter 94 before the try-works puts the fire back.

Better sleep with a sober cannibal than a drunken Christian.

Chapter 3

No more my splintered heart and maddened hand were turned against the wolfish world. This soothing savage had redeemed it.

Chapter 10

Fate, chance and free will

The book states the problem as a weaver’s loom: necessity is the warp, free will the shuttle, chance the blow that settles where the thread lies. Ahab argues both sides of it, insisting at one point that nothing can swerve him and later asking who is doing the pushing.

Asserted as iron rails in Chapter 37, set out as an image in Chapter 47, and shaken in Chapter 132 when Ahab asks whether Ahab is Ahab.
