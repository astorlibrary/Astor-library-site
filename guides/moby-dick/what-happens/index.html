Moby-Dick · Herman Melville

What happens

Chapters 1–22 New Bedford, Queequeg and Nantucket

Ishmael goes to sea to keep himself from stepping into the street and knocking people’s hats off. He shares a bed with a tattooed harpooneer, hears Father Mapple preach on Jonah from a pulpit shaped like a ship’s bow, and signs articles with two Quaker owners who haggle over his lay while Ahab keeps to his house, unseen.

Chapter 1
Ishmael explains why water draws landsmen to the ends of piers, and why he ships as a sailor and not a passenger.
Chapter 3
A night of terror at sharing a bed with a cannibal ends in the calmest sleep Ishmael has had.
Chapter 9
Father Mapple makes Jonah a story about flight, punishment and obedience, and preaches it to men about to sail.
Chapter 16
Peleg and Bildad bargain over Ishmael’s share of the profits and let slip that the captain is not himself.

Chapters 23–40 Ahab’s oath on the quarter-deck

The Pequod clears the harbour on Christmas Day with the captain invisible. When Ahab finally appears he nails a gold doubloon to the mast, tells the crew what the voyage is actually for, and has them drink to the death of the white whale. Starbuck objects once, on the record, and is overruled by a speech.

Chapter 26
Starbuck is introduced as a man whose courage is an instrument more than a temperament.
Chapter 36
Ahab nails up the doubloon, names the whale, and makes a business voyage into a private war.
Chapter 37
Alone in the cabin, Ahab describes his own will as rolling stock on rails it cannot leave.
Chapter 40
The crew sings and quarrels in a scene printed as a play, with stage directions and no narrator.

Chapters 41–47 Moby Dick, and the whiteness of the whale

The narrative stops to explain the enemy. Ishmael gathers the rumours that make Moby Dick ubiquitous and immortal, sets out Ahab’s transfer of every grievance onto one animal, and then devotes a whole chapter to the colour white, which he cannot make mean anything and cannot make mean nothing.

Chapter 41
The whale’s reputation is assembled out of sailors’ talk, and Ahab’s monomania is dated to the loss of his leg.
Chapter 42
Ishmael tries to explain why the colour frightens him and produces a chapter about the failure of symbols.
Chapter 44
Ahab plots currents and seasons to predict where one animal will be in an ocean.

Chapters 48–92 Whaling work, and the gams

Boats are lowered, whales are killed, and the book becomes an account of work: the line coiled in tubs where one snag will take a man overboard, the cutting-in, the head hoisted against the rail, the blubber rendered down. Gams with other ships bring news, and each captain Ahab meets refuses or fails to give him what he wants.

Chapter 60
The whale-line is described as a thing that sits in loops around every man in the boat.
Chapter 87
Inside a panicked herd the boats drift into a calm centre where whales are nursing their young.
Chapter 89
A rule of whaling property is extended, without comment, to nations, colonies and human beings.

Chapters 93–110 Pip overboard, the try-works alight, the coin read aloud

The cabin boy jumps from a boat and is left in the open sea long enough to lose his reason, which the book treats as a form of sight. Ishmael squeezes sperm until he loves everyone aboard, warns himself against staring into the try-works fire at night, and watches every officer in turn read the doubloon and find himself in it.

Chapter 93
Pip is abandoned in the water; the ship picks him up by chance, and from then on the crew say he is out of his mind.
Chapter 94
Working sperm back to liquid produces the most unguarded happiness in the book.
Chapter 96
The ship rendering blubber at night looks like something sailing out of hell, and Ishmael nearly steers it onto a course by staring.
Chapter 99
Ahab, Starbuck, Stubb, Flask, the Manxman, Queequeg, Fedallah and Pip each interpret the same coin.

Chapters 111–132 The quadrant, the compass, the coffin and the Rachel

Ahab smashes the quadrant, remagnetises the compass needles after a typhoon, and will not give the Rachel’s captain a day’s search for his own lost son. The carpenter caulks Queequeg’s coffin as a life-buoy. On one clear morning Ahab looks into Starbuck’s eyes, hears him plead for home, and does not turn back.

Chapter 119
St Elmo’s fire burns at the mastheads and Ahab addresses the lightning as a personal acquaintance.
Chapter 128
A captain begs for help searching for his missing boat and his son; Ahab says no and sails on.
Chapter 132
Ahab weeps into the sea and wonders what is commanding him, while Starbuck steals away in despair.

Chapters 133–135 and the Epilogue The three-day chase, and Ishmael’s survival

The whale is raised at last and fought for three days. It stoves the boats, drowns Fedallah in the lines, and finally runs at the ship itself and sinks it. Ahab goes down fastened to his own harpoon rope. Ishmael, thrown from Ahab’s boat on the last day, floats out of the vortex on the coffin.

Chapter 135
The Pequod is holed and sinks with the sky-hawk nailed to her mast; Ahab is carried off by his own line.
Epilogue
The narrator explains how he alone survived, and which ship picked him up.
