Frankenstein · Mary Shelley

Form and language

Frame narrative

A story enclosed within another story, often several layers deep.

Walton encloses Victor, Victor encloses the creature, and the creature encloses the De Laceys’ history. Every claim reaches the reader through at least one person with a reason to shape it.

One man’s life or death were but a small price to pay for the acquirement of the knowledge which I sought; for the dominion I should acquire and transmit over the elemental foes of our race.

Robert Walton, Letter IV

Epistolary form

A story built out of letters and documents, with no outside narrator.

Walton’s letters, Elizabeth’s and Alphonse’s letters, and Victor’s own laboratory journal read by the creature: the book is a bundle of papers addressed to people who mostly cannot reply.

I shall satiate my ardent curiosity with the sight of a part of the world never before visited, and may tread a land never before imprinted by the foot of man.

Robert Walton, Letter I

The Romantic sublime

The effect of vastness, mountains, storms, ice, that overwhelms the observer and enlarges him.

Victor uses Alpine scenery as medicine and it fails every time; the sublime in this novel produces the creature, never consolation.

Allusion

A deliberate reference to another text, used to borrow its argument.

The epigraph is Adam’s complaint to God from Paradise Lost, and the creature reads Milton as true history. Volney supplies him with politics and Goethe with grief.

Remember, that I am thy creature; I ought to be thy Adam; but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed.

The creature, Chapter X

Doubling

Two figures constructed so that each one completes or exposes the other.

Victor and the creature share a vocabulary, a solitude and a pursuit, and by the last chapter it is hard to say which of them is hunting the other.

I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend. Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous.

The creature, Chapter X

Retrospective narration

An account given after the event by a narrator who already knows how it ends.

Victor tells the story dying, which lets him load ordinary evenings with foreboding and convert decisions into destiny before the reader can examine them.

The world was to me a secret which I desired to divine.

Victor Frankenstein, Chapter II
