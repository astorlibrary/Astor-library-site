Frankenstein · Mary Shelley

What happens

Letters I-IV Walton’s letters

Robert Walton, sailing north in search of a passage and a friend, writes to a sister whose replies we never see. Ice closes around the ship, a huge figure crosses it on a sledge, and the next day the crew pulls aboard a starving European who has been chasing him. Everything that follows is inside a letter.

Letter I
Walton, at St Petersburgh, describes an expedition he expects to make him famous.
Letter II
At Archangel he complains that he has no friend, and no one to correct him.
Letter III
A short, confident note sent from open sea.
Letter IV
The ship is frozen in; the sledge is sighted; Victor Frankenstein comes aboard and begins to talk.

Chapters I-V A Geneva childhood, a university, and one night in November

Victor describes an upbringing so protected that his own account can find nothing in it to blame. At Ingolstadt he moves from discredited alchemists to modern chemistry, works alone for two years, and animates the thing he has assembled. The success lasts one sentence; the horror begins in the next.

Chapters I-II
The Frankenstein household, Elizabeth’s arrival, and a boy’s reading in Agrippa and Paracelsus.
Chapter III
His mother dies; at Ingolstadt, Waldman’s lecture redirects him towards modern chemistry.
Chapter IV
Two years in charnel houses and dissecting rooms; he breaks off his account to warn Walton.
Chapter V
The creature opens its eye; Victor flees, falls ill, and is nursed by Clerval.

Chapters VI-X A child is killed and a servant is hanged for it

News from Geneva: William is dead. Victor sees the creature by lightning on the way home and says nothing while Justine Moritz is tried, persuaded to confess and executed. This is the section that makes the novel a moral problem, because Victor’s silence costs a life and he presents it as his own suffering.

Chapters VI-VII
Clerval’s friendship, then the letter announcing William’s murder; a figure seen in a storm near Plainpalais.
Chapter VIII
Justine’s trial and execution; Victor keeps what he knows to himself.
Chapters IX-X
Grief-stricken travel in the Alps; on the Mer de Glace the creature approaches and asks to be heard.

Chapters XI-XVI The creature’s story

For six chapters the novel hands its voice to the thing it has been calling a wretch. It describes sensation before language, a year of watching the De Lacey family through a chink, an education gathered from Volney, Milton and Goethe, and the moment Felix beats it out of the cottage. It has been benevolent and is becoming something else.

Chapters XI-XII
First sensations, fire, hunger, flight from villages, and the hovel beside the De Lacey cottage.
Chapters XIII-XIV
Safie’s arrival gives the creature language lessons by proxy, and the De Laceys’ own history.
Chapter XV
Three books, and Victor’s laboratory journal; the blind old man listens until his family returns.
Chapter XVI
The cottage is abandoned; the creature saves a girl and is shot for it, then meets William.

Chapters XVII-XXIII The promised companion, and Elizabeth’s murder

The creature asks for one thing: a female of its own kind, and exile. Victor agrees, travels to England and Scotland, and tears the half-made companion apart with the creature watching at the window. The threat that follows is answered by a wedding, which is exactly what the threat was designed to produce.

Chapters XVII-XVIII
The bargain is struck; Victor delays for months, then leaves for England with Clerval.
Chapter XIX
London, Oxford, Scotland, and a hut on a remote Orkney island.
Chapter XX
The second creature is destroyed; the creature promises to be with him on his wedding night.
Chapters XXI-XXIII
Clerval is found strangled and Victor is charged; released, he marries Elizabeth, who dies that night.

Chapter XXIV and Walton’s close The chase north, and Victor’s death

Victor chases the creature north until the sea breaks the ice between them, and dies on Walton’s ship still recommending ambition in the same breath as warning against it. The creature comes aboard to grieve over him, tells Walton what the pursuit cost on its side, and leaves. Nobody is left to settle who was right.

Chapter XXIV
The pursuit down the Rhône, across the Black Sea and Russia, and out over the ice.
Walton, in continuation
Victor rouses the mutinous crew and dies; the creature is found grieving over the body and speaks for itself one last time.
