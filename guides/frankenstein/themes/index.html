Creation and responsibility

The novel spends little time on whether Victor should have made the creature and a great deal on what he owed it afterwards. The abandonment takes one sentence and is never revisited as a decision; every later disaster is traceable to it, and the creature argues the point better than anyone else in the book.

Through the book A boast in Chapter IV, a flight in Chapter V, an unanswerable case on the Mer de Glace, and a demand for a companion that Victor grants and then breaks in the Orkneys.

In the text

A new species would bless me as its creator and source; many happy and excellent natures would owe their being to me. No father could claim the gratitude of his child so completely as I should deserve theirs.

Victor Frankenstein, Chapter IV

Victor imagines fatherhood as a debt owed to him: blessing and gratitude from a new species, and nothing he will owe in return.

I had desired it with an ardour that far exceeded moderation; but now that I had finished, the beauty of the dream vanished, and breathless horror and disgust filled my heart.

Victor Frankenstein, Chapter V

Nothing about the creature changes between one sentence and the next. Victor stops wanting it, and every later disaster follows from that turning away.

William, Justine, and Henry⁠—they all died by my hands.

Victor Frankenstein, Chapter XXII

At last Victor puts himself in the sentence as the cause. His father hears it as raving, so the confession costs him nothing.

Loneliness

Everyone in this book is alone in a slightly different way. Walton has no friend, Victor works two years in a room nobody enters, the creature watches a family through a hole in a wall, and Margaret Saville receives every word and answers none. The plot turns on a request for company being refused.

Through the book Walton states the want in Letter II; the creature makes it a formal demand in Chapter XVII; Victor destroys the answer in Chapter XX and is hunted for it.

In the text

I have no friend, Margaret: when I am glowing with the enthusiasm of success, there will be none to participate my joy; if I am assailed by disappointment, no one will endeavour to sustain me in dejection.

Robert Walton, Letter II

Walton tells his sister, the one person who loves him, that he has nobody, and does not see the irony.

I am malicious because I am miserable. Am I not shunned and hated by all mankind?

The creature, Chapter XVII

The creature puts the cause in the present tense, and his question shifts the burden onto everyone who has shunned him.

It is well. I go; but remember, I shall be with you on your wedding night.

The creature, Chapter XX

The creature means to take from Victor what Victor took from him: a companion, on the night she would be his.

Knowledge and ambition

Shelley separates the pursuit of knowledge from its use with unusual care. Victor’s science works. The novel’s warnings are all about appetite, never method, and its most quoted caution, ‘Learn from me, if not by my precepts, at least by my example’, comes from a man who cannot resist telling the story that will teach it.

Through the book Alchemy in Chapter II, Waldman’s lecture in Chapter III, a two-year secret in Chapter IV, and a deathbed in which the warning is immediately half-withdrawn.

In the text

The world was to me a secret which I desired to divine.

Victor Frankenstein, Chapter II

“Divine” means both to work something out and to act as a god. The appetite is there in a boy, long before anything is made.

Learn from me, if not by my precepts, at least by my example, how dangerous is the acquirement of knowledge, and how much happier that man is who believes his native town to be the world, than he who aspires to become greater than his nature will allow.

Victor Frankenstein, Chapter IV

The warning comes from a man telling his story at length. He withholds the method and keeps the glamour.

Farewell, Walton! Seek happiness in tranquillity, and avoid ambition, even if it be only the apparently innocent one of distinguishing yourself in science and discoveries.

Victor Frankenstein, Chapter XXIV

The nearest the novel comes to a moral, and Victor withdraws it in his next breath: another man might yet succeed.

Nature and the sublime

Mountains, storms and ice do real work here. Victor goes to the Alps to be soothed and meets the creature there; the glacier that is supposed to console him produces his accuser. The one landscape that answers him properly is the Arctic, which stops him.

Through the book A blasted oak in Chapter II, the Mer de Glace in Chapter X, the Orkneys in Chapter XIX, and an ice field that finally holds both pursuer and pursued.

Education

The creature’s own narrative is a short course in eighteenth-century theories of mind: sensation first, then language, then books, then a sense of injustice. He tells us plainly that he was benevolent and good and that misery altered him. The novel gives him the evidence to prove it before it lets him kill anyone.

Through the book Six chapters of self-education end with Felix’s stick; the first murder follows the discovery that a small child has already learned to scream at him.

In the text

I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend. Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous.

The creature, Chapter X

The novel’s theory of character in two clauses: virtue is a condition, and changing the conditions could restore it.

Accursed creator! Why did you form a monster so hideous that even you turned from me in disgust?

The creature, Chapter XV

He learned how he was made from Victor’s own notes. His charge is that he was made, and then looked at with disgust.

I, the miserable and the abandoned, am an abortion, to be spurned at, and kicked, and trampled on.

The creature, Chapter XXIV

The creature’s last speech uses the words of the people who drove him off, which leaves the reader to decide who taught him them.

Justice and testimony

Justine is hanged on circumstantial evidence while the one man who knows the truth stays silent, sure that his story would be taken for madness. Victor is later held for Clerval’s murder on evidence of the same kind, and in the De Laceys’ history a Paris court condemns Safie’s father for his religion and wealth. The Geneva court that convicts Justine is honest and wrong; the Paris court is simply unjust.

Through the book Justine’s trial in Chapter VIII, Safie’s father in Chapter XIV, Victor’s arrest at the end of Chapter XX, and a frame narrative that finally leaves the whole case in an explorer’s private letters.

In the text

You must create a female for me, with whom I can live in the interchange of those sympathies necessary for my being. This you alone can do; and I demand it of you as a right which you must not refuse to concede.

The creature, Chapter XVII

He asks for a companion in the language of contract, as a right owed to him. Six chapters of evidence have built to the claim.

He was soon borne away by the waves, and lost in darkness and distance.

Robert Walton, Chapter XXIV

No body and no witness: the creature announces his own death, and the book closes on testimony nobody can check.
