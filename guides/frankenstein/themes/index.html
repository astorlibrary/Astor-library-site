Frankenstein · Mary Shelley

Themes

Creation and responsibility

The novel spends little time on whether Victor should have made the creature and a great deal on what he owed it afterwards. The abandonment takes one sentence and is never revisited as a decision; every later disaster is traceable to it, and the creature argues the point better than anyone else in the book.

A boast in Chapter IV, a flight in Chapter V, an unanswerable case on the Mer de Glace, and a demand for a companion that Victor grants and then breaks in the Orkneys.

One man’s life or death were but a small price to pay for the acquirement of the knowledge which I sought; for the dominion I should acquire and transmit over the elemental foes of our race.

Robert Walton, Letter IV

Learn from me, if not by my precepts, at least by my example, how dangerous is the acquirement of knowledge, and how much happier that man is who believes his native town to be the world, than he who aspires to become greater than his nature will allow.

Victor Frankenstein, Chapter IV

Loneliness

Everyone in this book is alone in a slightly different way. Walton has no friend, Victor works two years in a room nobody enters, the creature watches a family through a hole in a wall, and Margaret Saville receives every word and answers none. The plot turns on a request for company being refused.

Walton states the want in Letter II; the creature makes it a formal demand in Chapter XVII; Victor destroys the answer in Chapter XX and is hunted for it.

I have no friend, Margaret: when I am glowing with the enthusiasm of success, there will be none to participate my joy; if I am assailed by disappointment, no one will endeavour to sustain me in dejection.

Robert Walton, Letter II

You must create a female for me, with whom I can live in the interchange of those sympathies necessary for my being. This you alone can do; and I demand it of you as a right which you must not refuse to concede.

The creature, Chapter XVII

Knowledge and ambition

Shelley separates the pursuit of knowledge from its use with unusual care. Victor’s science works. The novel’s warnings are all about appetite, never method, and its most quoted caution, ‘Learn from me, if not by my precepts, at least by my example’, comes from a man who cannot resist telling the story that will teach it.

Alchemy in Chapter II, Waldman’s lecture in Chapter III, a two-year secret in Chapter IV, and a deathbed in which the warning is immediately half-withdrawn.

I shall satiate my ardent curiosity with the sight of a part of the world never before visited, and may tread a land never before imprinted by the foot of man.

Robert Walton, Letter I

One man’s life or death were but a small price to pay for the acquirement of the knowledge which I sought; for the dominion I should acquire and transmit over the elemental foes of our race.

Robert Walton, Letter IV

Nature and the sublime

Mountains, storms and ice do real work here. Victor goes to the Alps to be soothed and meets the creature there; the glacier that is supposed to console him produces his accuser. The one landscape that answers him properly is the Arctic, which stops him.

A blasted oak in Chapter II, the Mer de Glace in Chapter X, the Orkneys in Chapter XIX, and an ice field that finally holds both pursuer and pursued.

Education

The creature’s own narrative is a short course in eighteenth-century theories of mind: sensation first, then language, then books, then a sense of injustice. He tells us plainly that he was benevolent and good and that misery altered him. The novel gives him the evidence to prove it before it lets him kill anyone.

Six chapters of self-education end with Felix’s stick; the first murder follows the discovery that a small child has already learned to scream at him.

I had desired it with an ardour that far exceeded moderation; but now that I had finished, the beauty of the dream vanished, and breathless horror and disgust filled my heart.

Victor Frankenstein, Chapter V

Remember, that I am thy creature; I ought to be thy Adam; but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed.

The creature, Chapter X

Justice and testimony

Justine is hanged on circumstantial evidence while the one man who knows the truth stays silent, sure that his story would be taken for madness. Victor is later held for Clerval’s murder on evidence of the same kind, and in the De Laceys’ history a Paris court condemns Safie’s father for his religion and wealth. The Geneva court that convicts Justine is honest and wrong; the Paris court is simply unjust.

Justine’s trial in Chapter VIII, Safie’s father in Chapter XIV, Victor’s arrest at the end of Chapter XX, and a frame narrative that finally leaves the whole case in an explorer’s private letters.

You must create a female for me, with whom I can live in the interchange of those sympathies necessary for my being. This you alone can do; and I demand it of you as a right which you must not refuse to concede.

The creature, Chapter XVII

Beware; for I am fearless, and therefore powerful.

The creature, Chapter XX
