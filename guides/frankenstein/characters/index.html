Frankenstein · Mary Shelley

Characters

Victor Frankenstein

Genevese student of natural philosophy; first appears Letter IV

He gives two years to the problem of animating dead matter and about ten seconds to the question of what he will do afterwards. His narration is the novel’s central difficulty: it is fluent, self-accusing and consistently arranged so that the things he chose look like things that happened to him.

The creature

The being Victor assembles and abandons; first appears Chapter V

Nameless on purpose, and called daemon, wretch and fiend by everyone who describes him, including himself. He is the best reader in the book and the best speaker in it, and he makes his case for a companion in the formal language of rights before he makes his case for revenge.

Robert Walton

Arctic explorer, the outermost narrator; first appears Letter I

An amateur with money, no formal education and a plan to reach the pole, who writes home that he wants a friend more than he wants the passage. He is offered the story as a warning and never says whether he has taken it; it is his crew that makes him turn the ship round.

Elizabeth Lavenza

Taken into the Frankenstein household as a child; Victor’s betrothed; first appears Chapter I

Presented to Victor as a gift when they are children, and described by him for the rest of his life as a possession he was given. Her longest piece of writing offers to release him from the engagement; he marries her anyway, knowing what has been promised for the wedding night.

Henry Clerval

Victor’s friend from childhood; first appears Chapter II

The alternative Victor might have been: a student of languages and poetry who nurses his friend through a breakdown without ever asking what caused it. He is strangled on a beach in Ireland because he is travelling with the wrong man, and his death produces the book’s second wrongful arrest.

Justine Moritz

Servant in the Frankenstein house; first appears Chapter VI

Tried for William’s murder on the evidence of a miniature planted in her pocket, and convicted after a confessor persuades her that a lie will save her soul. Victor sits in the courtroom knowing who did it. The novel’s clearest statement about what respectable silence costs is made by a hanging.

The De Lacey family

Exiled French cottagers in Germany; first appears Chapter XI

A blind father, a son and a daughter living in poverty after a political disgrace, watched for months through a chink by a being who chops their firewood at night. They teach it language, history and sympathy without knowing it exists, and then Felix beats it with a stick.

Margaret Saville

Walton’s sister in England, the reader of every word; first appears Letter I

She never speaks, never writes back and never appears, and the entire novel is addressed to her. Her initials, M. W. S., are Mary Shelley’s own, and her silence is structural: every narrator in the book is finally talking to a woman at home who cannot interrupt them.
