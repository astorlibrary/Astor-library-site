Frankenstein · Mary Shelley

Context

Dates

1792
Mary Wollstonecraft publishes A Vindication of the Rights of Woman The author’s mother, whose arguments about education and sympathy run through the creature’s narrative.
1793
William Godwin publishes Political Justice The author’s father, and the source of the novel’s habit of settling obligations by argument.
1797
Mary Wollstonecraft Godwin is born in London Her mother died within days, of complications following the birth.
1803
Giovanni Aldini’s galvanic demonstrations in London Electrical experiments on the body of a hanged man at the Royal College of Surgeons, widely reported.
1816
Mary Shelley begins the story at the Villa Diodati Byron proposes a ghost-story competition beside Lake Geneva; Shelley, then eighteen, begins the tale.
1818
Frankenstein published anonymously in three volumes Dedicated to Godwin, with a preface written by Percy Bysshe Shelley, which led several reviewers to assume he was the author.
1823
Presumption; or, The Fate of Frankenstein opens in London Richard Brinsley Peake’s version gives the creature no speech at all, and it was the first of many adaptations to take his argument away.
1823
A second edition names Mary Shelley as the author Published in two volumes while she was abroad, and seen through the press by her father.
1831
The revised one-volume edition for Bentley’s Standard Novels Shelley adds the introduction about the Diodati summer and alters the novel’s account of Elizabeth, chance and fate. This is the text used here.
1851
Mary Shelley dies in London She had outlived Percy Shelley by nearly thirty years and all but one of her children.
1910
Edison Studios releases the first screen Frankenstein A single reel, long thought lost, in which the creature is made in a vat and not on a table.
1931
James Whale’s Frankenstein for Universal Boris Karloff’s flat-headed, inarticulate creature becomes the popular image of a character who, in the novel, argues fluently for his rights.

Places

Geneva
The Frankenstein household, and the city whose courts hang Justine.
Cologny, Lake Geneva
The Villa Diodati, where the ghost-story competition of 1816 produced the novel.
Ingolstadt
The university where Victor works alone for two years and animates the creature.
Chamonix and the Mer de Glace
The glacier where the creature finally makes his case, in the novel’s spelling Chamounix.
Orkney
The remote island hut where Victor begins and then destroys the female companion.
Archangel
Walton’s last port before the ice, and the place from which he writes that he has no friend.

Critics

Walter Scott

Blackwood’s Edinburgh Magazine (1818)

Scott reviewed the novel warmly, praising its power and the plainness of its style. The book had been published anonymously, and he assumed that the author was Percy Bysshe Shelley.

Ellen Moers

Literary Women (1976)

In her chapter on the “Female Gothic”, Moers reads Frankenstein as a birth myth: a story of creation followed by revulsion and abandonment. She links it with Mary Shelley’s experience of pregnancy and of the death of her first child in 1815.

Sandra M. Gilbert and Susan Gubar

The Madwoman in the Attic (1979)

Gilbert and Gubar read the novel through Paradise Lost, which the creature reads. They argue that he is at once Adam, Satan and, as an outcast made to be the companion of a being who rejects him, an image of Eve.

Chris Baldick

In Frankenstein’s Shadow (1987)

Baldick traces the image of the monster in political writing before and after the novel, from Edmund Burke on the French Revolution onwards. He shows how “Frankenstein” became a byword for any creation that turns on its maker.
