Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass · Frederick Douglass

Context

Dates

1818
Douglass born Frederick Bailey in Talbot County, Maryland The date is now generally given as February 1818, though the book opens by saying he has never seen a record of it.
1826
Sent from the Eastern Shore to Baltimore He was about eight. The move put him in Hugh and Sophia Auld’s house, where the lessons began.
1833
The fight with Edward Covey Dated in the book to one of the hottest days of August, after six months of being hired out to a man paid in labour to break people.
1838
Escape to New York, and marriage to Anna Murray The book gives the marriage certificate in full and says nothing whatever about the journey.
1841
First speech to a white audience, at Nantucket On 11 August, at an anti-slavery convention; Garrison’s preface begins from that meeting.
1845
The Narrative published in Boston Issued by the Anti-Slavery Office with Garrison’s preface and Wendell Phillips’s letter attached.
1846
British supporters purchase his legal freedom Raised during a lecture tour of Britain and Ireland undertaken partly because the book had made him traceable.
1847
Douglass founds The North Star in Rochester His own paper, and the beginning of his separation from Garrison’s wing of the movement.
1855
Douglass publishes My Bondage and My Freedom A longer second autobiography, written without a white sponsor’s preface and less willing to be a specimen.
1863
The Emancipation Proclamation Douglass recruited for the Union army and pressed Lincoln on the pay and treatment of black soldiers.
1881
Douglass publishes Life and Times of Frederick Douglass The third autobiography, which finally describes how the escape was made.
1895
Douglass dies in Washington On 20 February, after a lifetime of public office, journalism and speaking.

Places

Easton, Talbot County, Maryland
The county town twelve miles from Tuckahoe, where Douglass was born, and where he was later held in jail after the failed escape.
Baltimore
The Aulds’ house, the alphabet, the shipyards, and the city he escaped from in 1838.
St Michaels, Maryland
Thomas Auld’s town, where the Sabbath school was broken up by class leaders.
Chesapeake Bay
The water whose sails Douglass addressed from the bank, and the route north he planned by canoe.
New Bedford, Massachusetts
The whaling port where he was first paid for his own labour and took the name Douglass.
Nantucket
The 1841 anti-slavery convention at which he spoke for the first time to a white audience.

Critics

Margaret Fuller

New-York Daily Tribune (1845)

Reviewing the book on 10 June 1845, Fuller wrote that, considered merely as a narrative, she had never read one more simple, true, coherent and warm with genuine feeling, and praised Douglass’s courage in naming persons, times and places at obvious danger to himself.

William L. Andrews

To Tell a Free Story (1986)

Andrews read the slave narratives as a form in which the writer had to satisfy white sponsors and readers while claiming the authority to tell their own story, and showed how Douglass takes control of his.
