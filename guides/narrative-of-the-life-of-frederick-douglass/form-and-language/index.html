Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass · Frederick Douglass

Form and language

Retrospective narration

A narrator telling their own earlier life, with the distance between the two selves doing the judging.

Douglass writes as a man on a lecture circuit remembering a child who could not read, and frequently marks the distance: he tells us what he did not then understand, and what he has since learned to call it.

I have no accurate knowledge of my age, never having seen any authentic record containing it.

Chapter I

Direct address

A narrator or speaker turning outward to address the reader or the audience by name.

He addresses northern readers who have taken the singing of the enslaved for happiness, and tells them they have made a greater mistake than they know.

This they would sing, as a chorus, to words which to many would seem unmeaning jargon, but which, nevertheless, were full of meaning to themselves.

Chapter II

Apostrophe

Speech addressed to something that cannot answer.

On the banks of the Chesapeake he speaks to the sails on the bay, and the speech is a rehearsal for the public speaker he will become, and the moment he first says out loud that he will run.

You are loosed from your moorings, and are free; I am fast in my chains, and am a slave!

Chapter X

Catalogue

A list allowed to run on past the point of usefulness, so that the accumulation itself becomes the effect.

The monthly allowance of pork and meal, the annual clothing, the men appraised beside the swine: Douglass repeatedly stops telling a story and lists quantities, because the list is the thing being described.

No words, no tears, no prayers, from his gory victim, seemed to move his iron heart from its bloody purpose.

Chapter I

Scriptural argument

Using scripture as the ground of an argument.

The appendix works by quotation and parody, and Douglass thanks his New Bedford host, Nathan Johnson, in the words of Matthew 25.

The silver trump of freedom had roused my soul to eternal wakefulness.

Chapter VII

Deliberate omission

Withholding information the reader expects, and saying why.

The escape itself is not described. Douglass explains the reasoning (a route made public is a route closed), and the gap in the story shows what testimony owes to the people still inside slavery.

I have never approved of the very public manner in which some of our western friends have conducted what they call the underground railroad
