Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass · Frederick Douglass

What happens

Preface and Letter Garrison’s preface and Phillips’s letter

Garrison recalls hearing Douglass speak at Nantucket and insists the book is entirely his own work. Phillips writes about the danger of printing names and places while the law can still take a man back. Both documents assume a reader who needs persuading that the author is real.

Preface
An account of the 1841 Nantucket convention, and a guarantee that nothing in the book has been exaggerated.
Letter
Publishing under his own name, in a country with a fugitive slave law, exposes Douglass to recapture.

Chapters I–IV Tuckahoe, and the great house farm

Douglass records a birth without a date, a mother he saw perhaps five times, and the whipping of his aunt, which he watched from a closet. He then widens the lens: the allowance of food and clothing, the songs on the road to the great house farm, and killings by overseers and owners that nobody was tried for.

Chapter I
No record of his age, no acknowledged father, and the law that makes a child follow its mother’s condition.
Chapter II
Colonel Lloyd’s plantation as an economy, and the singing that northern readers have misheard as contentment.
Chapters III–IV
Severe’s cursing, Gore’s shooting of Demby, and other killings that nobody was tried for.

Chapters V–VII Baltimore, and learning to read

Sent to Hugh Auld’s house in Baltimore, Douglass is taught his letters by Sophia Auld until her husband stops the lessons and explains, in front of the pupil, exactly why reading is dangerous. Douglass takes the explanation as a syllabus and continues on the street, in a shipyard and out of a borrowed schoolbook.

Chapter V
A sloop down the Miles River, and a household where he is fed enough to notice the difference.
Chapter VI
Hugh Auld forbids the lessons and supplies the reason; Sophia Auld begins to change.
Chapter VII
Bread traded for lessons, a schoolbook of speeches, and the discovery that reading brings no relief with it.

Chapters VIII–IX The valuation, and St Michael’s

Captain Anthony’s death sends Douglass back to be appraised alongside the horses and swine, and the valuation is the book’s clearest statement of what the law thinks he is. At St Michael’s under Thomas Auld he is hungry again, and a Sabbath school for the enslaved is broken up by men who hold church office.

Chapter VIII
Men, women, children and animals ranked together for division between heirs.
Chapter IX
Thomas Auld’s conversion at a camp meeting makes him harder, and the Sabbath school is dispersed with sticks.

Chapter X Covey, and the fight

Hired out to Edward Covey, Douglass is worked and beaten until the reading stops and the wish for freedom goes quiet. In August 1833 he refuses to be whipped, and the two men fight for the better part of two hours. The following year, at William Freeland’s, he keeps a Sabbath school of over forty scholars and plans an escape that fails.

Chapter X, first half
A man the enslaved called the snake, and six months that reduce Douglass to what he calls a brute.
Chapter X, the fight
He repels Covey by force, and is never whipped again in the four years that remain.
Chapter X, Freeland’s
Forty scholars on Sundays, a forged pass, a betrayal, and Easton jail.

Chapter XI Escape, and New Bedford

Douglass states plainly that he will not describe how he escaped, because the route belongs to people still using it. What he does describe is arrival: the loneliness of New York, the marriage to Anna Murray, the choice of a surname, and the shock of finding New Bedford wealthier than Maryland without owning anybody.

Chapter XI, the withholding
The escape is omitted on principle; the underground railroad, he argues, has been made too public already.
Chapter XI, New Bedford
Stowing a sloop with oil for wages that are his own, and taking the name Douglass from The Lady of the Lake.
Chapter XI, Nantucket
He speaks to a white audience for the first time and is taken up by the anti-slavery societies.

Appendix Slaveholding religion and the religion of Christ

Anticipating the charge that he has attacked Christianity, Douglass draws the distinction himself and makes it absolute: the religion of the slaveholder and the religion of Christ cannot both be accepted. The prose turns to parody and catalogue, and the book ends with a signature instead of a scene.

Appendix
A hymn parody, a list of the slaveholding church’s habits, and a closing pledge signed with his chosen name.
