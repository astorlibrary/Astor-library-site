Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass · Frederick Douglass

Characters

Frederick Douglass

The author, narrator and subject: a real man, writing about his own life; first appears Chapter I

He is the child in the story and the practised speaker telling it, and the gap between the two is where the book does its work. He withholds as carefully as he discloses, and the control of tone is itself evidence against the claim that an enslaved man could not have written the book.

Captain Anthony

Douglass’s first owner, and Colonel Lloyd’s clerk and superintendent; first appears Chapter I

A middling man and no magnate: he manages another man’s plantation and owns a few farms of his own. Douglass never learns his first name, records the rumour that he was his father without settling it, and uses the whipping of Aunt Hester to introduce him.

Colonel Edward Lloyd

Owner of the home plantation on the Wye; first appears Chapter II

He is the scale against which everything in the first four chapters is measured: hundreds of people, a fleet of sloops, a garden that visitors travel to see. Douglass reports that he did not know his own slaves by sight, which the book presents as the natural product of owning that many.

Sophia Auld

Hugh Auld’s wife, in Baltimore; first appears Chapter V

She had never owned anyone before, and treated a child as a child, which in this household was the anomaly. Douglass tracks her decline with more care than he gives any other white person in the book: the teaching stops, then the kindness, and finally she is the one snatching a newspaper out of his hands.

Hugh Auld

Baltimore shipbuilder, in whose house Douglass lived; first appears Chapter V

He stops the lessons and, in doing so, delivers the clearest analysis of slavery in the book: that literacy and bondage are incompatible. He means it as a household instruction and Douglass receives it as a plan, which is the single most consequential misunderstanding in the Narrative.

Thomas Auld

Douglass’s legal owner after the division of the estate, at St Michael’s; first appears Chapter VIII

Douglass presents him as a man who was cruel without being good at it, and who became worse after a camp-meeting conversion gave his cruelty religious sanction. It is Thomas Auld who hires Douglass out to Covey for a year, having failed to manage him himself.

Edward Covey

A poor farmer who took in men to break them; first appears Chapter X

His trade is reputation: he gets free labour because slaveholders send him people to be subdued, and he keeps the reputation by never letting a fight become public. That is why the two hours in the stable yard finish him: he cannot have Douglass whipped in Easton without advertising his own failure.

William Freeland

Farmer near St Michael’s, Douglass’s employer in 1835; first appears Chapter X

He is the best enslaver in the book, and the book treats that as a problem: he feeds people properly, works them reasonable hours, and makes no religious claims. Douglass plans his escape from this farm, and says so in order to close the argument that kindness answers the objection to slavery.

William Lloyd Garrison

Editor of The Liberator, and author of the preface; first appears Preface

He heard Douglass speak at Nantucket in 1841 and put him on the anti-slavery lecture circuit. His preface insists the book is wholly Douglass’s own work, which was necessary and is also the first sign of a relationship in which a black author keeps being introduced by white men.
