Literacy

Reading enters the book as a domestic kindness, is reclassified as a threat within a chapter, and is thereafter pursued as a criminal activity: bread traded for lessons, copybooks worked over at night, a Sabbath school held in a house whose owner cannot be named. Douglass is careful to say it brought no comfort with it.

Through the book Sophia Auld’s lessons in Chapter VI, Hugh Auld’s prohibition and its unintended instruction, the torment of Chapter VII, and finally Douglass teaching forty others at Freeland’s in Chapter X.

In the text

It would forever unfit him to be a slave. He would at once become unmanageable, and of no value to his master.

Hugh Auld, Chapter VI

The argument against literacy is put in the language of property maintenance (fitness, manageability, value), and it is the most accurate sentence any enslaver speaks in the book. Auld believes he is protecting an asset, and he is in fact reading out the instructions.

They came because they wished to learn. Their minds had been starved by their cruel masters. They had been shut up in mental darkness.

Chapter X

The metaphor is deliberately physical (‘starved’, ‘shut up’), and it matches the accounting of pork and meal earlier in the book, so that a withheld education is placed in the same ledger as a withheld ration. Every one of those scholars risked thirty-nine lashes to attend, which Douglass mentions without adjective.

Identity and documents

The book begins with an absence of paperwork (no birth record, no acknowledged father, no fixed name) and ends with a surname chosen for him by his host over a breakfast table in New Bedford, which he then prints on a title page. In between, the only document that records him accurately is a valuation listing him with the horses.

Through the book From the missing record of Chapter I, through the appraisal in Chapter VIII, to the series of names (Bailey, Stanley, Johnson, Douglass) in Chapter XI.

In the text

I have no accurate knowledge of my age, never having seen any authentic record containing it.

Chapter I

An autobiography conventionally opens with a date, and this one opens with the absence of one. The words are the language of evidence, accurate, authentic, record, so the first thing Douglass establishes is a gap in the documents, and the rest of the chapter shows that the gap was arranged.

I must hold on to that, to preserve a sense of my identity.

Chapter XI

He has been Bailey, Stanley and Johnson within a fortnight, and the surname that reaches the title page is picked out of Walter Scott by a man he has just met. Against all that, the retained forename is the whole of the continuity he has, and he says so in the vocabulary of ownership: hold on, preserve.

Resistance and the body

Douglass makes the decisive event of his life a physical one, and he does not soften it into a metaphor: two hours of fighting in a yard, after which he is never whipped again. He presents it as getting back something taken from him, and he dates it.

Through the book Endurance in Chapters VIII and IX, collapse under Covey in the first half of Chapter X, the fight in August 1833, and the failed Easter escape plan at Freeland’s.

In the text

You are loosed from your moorings, and are free; I am fast in my chains, and am a slave!

Chapter X

The clause structure is a pair of balanced opposites, and the man speaking it has nobody to hear him but the ships. It is the first speech Douglass ever reports himself making, and it is already built like a platform address: the future lecturer is audible in it years before he has an audience.

I felt like one who had escaped a den of hungry lions.

Chapter XI

He quotes his own younger self instead of describing the feeling, and then undercuts it straight away: within a paragraph the elation subsides into insecurity and loneliness among thousands of strangers. The structure withholds the ending that an escape story is expected to supply.

The slaveholding religion

The worst men in the book are the pious ones, and Douglass builds the case in instalments: Thomas Auld gets crueller after his conversion, class leaders break up a Sabbath school with sticks, and the appendix finally names the pattern. His point is that religion has been captured.

Through the book The camp meeting in Chapter IX, the destruction of the school at St Michael’s, the Sunday piety of Covey in Chapter X, and the systematic distinction drawn in the appendix.

In the text

Thus ended our little Sabbath school in the pious town of St. Michael’s.

Chapter IX

One flat sentence, and the whole weight rests on the word ‘pious’, which says as much as a page of argument would. Douglass rarely raises his voice at moments like this; he lets the adjective sit next to the facts and leaves the reader to make the collision.

It was a glorious resurrection, from the tomb of slavery, to the heaven of freedom.

Chapter X

He takes the central image of the religion his enslavers profess and applies it to an act of physical violence against one of them. That is the argument of the appendix made in a single phrase, four chapters early, and it is why the book cannot be read as a quarrel with Christianity.

Evidence and testimony

Douglass writes as a witness in a proceeding that has no court: he gives names, counties and dates, records one killing after another that produced no charge, and knows that his evidence will be disbelieved on grounds of who he is. The form of the book is shaped by the standard of proof his readers will apply.

Through the book The murders of Demby and of Mrs Hicks’s girl in Chapter IV, the preface and letter that exist to authenticate him, and the closing pledge in the appendix to rely on the power of truth.

In the text

Mr. Douglass has very properly chosen to write his own Narrative, in his own style, and according to the best of his ability, rather than to employ someone else. It is, therefore, entirely his own production

William Lloyd Garrison, Preface

The sentence is a defence, which means it tells you what the accusation was. Garrison has to certify the authorship of a book whose author is standing beside him, and the very act of certifying reproduces the assumption it answers: the reader is asked to trust a white man about a black man’s prose.

Slaves sing most when they are most unhappy. The songs of the slave represent the sorrows of his heart; and he is relieved by them, only as an aching heart is relieved by its tears.

Chapter II

Here Douglass acts as an expert witness on a disputed piece of evidence, and wins by explaining the mechanism. The comparison with weeping is exact: he admits the songs give relief, and the admission makes his correction hard to dismiss.

Kinship broken by sale

Mothers are hired out twelve miles away, children are separated before they can remember, grandmothers are turned out in the woods when they can no longer work. Douglass argues that these cruelties are how an owner secures a claim, and he includes the law that makes a child inherit its mother’s condition.

Through the book The night visits and unmourned death of his mother in Chapter I, the fate of his grandmother in Chapter VIII, and his own refusal in Chapter XI to endanger anyone still held.

In the text

slaveholders have ordained, and by law established, that the children of slave women shall in all cases follow the condition of their mothers

Chapter I

He does not pursue the rumour; he cites the statute instead, and the shift from gossip to legislation is the whole method of the book in one move. Naming the law also names the incentive: a rule that makes a man’s children his property makes a private crime into an investment.

That cheerful eye, under the influence of slavery, soon became red with rage; that voice, made all of sweet accord, changed to one of harsh and horrid discord; and that angelic face gave place to that of a demon.

Chapter VI

Three parallel clauses, each pairing a feature of Sophia Auld with what it became: eye, voice, face. The cause named in the sentence is slavery, and she is what it acts on, which is the book’s consistent claim: ownership damages the owner too.
