Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass · Frederick Douglass

Themes

Literacy

Reading enters the book as a domestic kindness, is reclassified as a threat within a chapter, and is thereafter pursued as a criminal activity: bread traded for lessons, copybooks worked over at night, a Sabbath school held in a house whose owner cannot be named. Douglass is careful to say it brought no comfort with it.

Sophia Auld’s lessons in Chapter VI, Hugh Auld’s prohibition and its unintended instruction, the torment of Chapter VII, and finally Douglass teaching forty others at Freeland’s in Chapter X.

This they would sing, as a chorus, to words which to many would seem unmeaning jargon, but which, nevertheless, were full of meaning to themselves.

Chapter II

It would forever unfit him to be a slave. He would at once become unmanageable, and of no value to his master.

Hugh Auld, Chapter VI

Identity and documents

The book begins with an absence of paperwork (no birth record, no acknowledged father, no fixed name) and ends with a surname chosen for him by his host over a breakfast table in New Bedford, which he then prints on a title page. In between, the only document that records him accurately is a valuation listing him with the horses.

From the missing record of Chapter I, through the appraisal in Chapter VIII, to the series of names (Bailey, Stanley, Johnson, Douglass) in Chapter XI.

Mr. Douglass has very properly chosen to write his own Narrative, in his own style, and according to the best of his ability, rather than to employ someone else. It is, therefore, entirely his own production

William Lloyd Garrison, Preface

I have no accurate knowledge of my age, never having seen any authentic record containing it.

Chapter I

Resistance and the body

Douglass makes the decisive event of his life a physical one, and he does not soften it into a metaphor: two hours of fighting in a yard, after which he is never whipped again. He presents it as getting back something taken from him, and he dates it.

Endurance in Chapters VIII and IX, collapse under Covey in the first half of Chapter X, the fight in August 1833, and the failed Easter escape plan at Freeland’s.

It was the bloodstained gate, the entrance to the hell of slavery, through which I was about to pass.

The slaveholding religion

The worst men in the book are the pious ones, and Douglass builds the case in instalments: Thomas Auld gets crueller after his conversion, class leaders break up a Sabbath school with sticks, and the appendix finally names the pattern. His point is that religion has been captured.

The camp meeting in Chapter IX, the destruction of the school at St Michael’s, the Sunday piety of Covey in Chapter X, and the systematic distinction drawn in the appendix.

That cheerful eye, under the influence of slavery, soon became red with rage; that voice, made all of sweet accord, changed to one of harsh and horrid discord; and that angelic face gave place to that of a demon.

Chapter VI

Thus ended our little Sabbath school in the pious town of St. Michael’s.

Chapter IX

Evidence and testimony

Douglass writes as a witness in a proceeding that has no court: he gives names, counties and dates, records one killing after another that produced no charge, and knows that his evidence will be disbelieved on grounds of who he is. The form of the book is shaped by the standard of proof his readers will apply.

The murders of Demby and of Mrs Hicks’s girl in Chapter IV, the preface and letter that exist to authenticate him, and the closing pledge in the appendix to rely on the power of truth.

Mr. Douglass has very properly chosen to write his own Narrative, in his own style, and according to the best of his ability, rather than to employ someone else. It is, therefore, entirely his own production

William Lloyd Garrison, Preface

slaveholders have ordained, and by law established, that the children of slave women shall in all cases follow the condition of their mothers

Chapter I

Kinship broken by sale

Mothers are hired out twelve miles away, children are separated before they can remember, grandmothers are turned out in the woods when they can no longer work. Douglass argues that these cruelties are how an owner secures a claim, and he includes the law that makes a child inherit its mother’s condition.

The night visits and unmourned death of his mother in Chapter I, the fate of his grandmother in Chapter VIII, and his own refusal in Chapter XI to endanger anyone still held.
