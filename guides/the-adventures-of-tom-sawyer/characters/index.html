The Adventures of Tom Sawyer · Mark Twain

Characters

Tom Sawyer

A boy in the care of his dead mother’s sister; first appears Chapter 1

He is a showman first and a hero second, and the book is unusually clear that the two are often the same thing: he returns from the dead for the effect, takes a whipping for Becky in front of an audience, and tells the truth in court only when it can be done at the last possible dramatic moment. His courage is real, and it is always staged.

Huckleberry Finn

Son of the town drunkard; first appears Chapter 6

He sleeps in empty hogsheads, comes and goes as he likes, and is envied by every boy whose mother has forbidden him. Twain gives him the novel’s only sustained resistance to the town’s values, and the last chapter is an argument he loses, for now; one book later he wins it.

Aunt Polly

Tom’s guardian, his dead mother’s sister; first appears Chapter 1

She believes in duty, the rod and patent medicines, and is defeated in advance by her own affection: the novel’s first long speech is her explaining to herself why she will not punish him. Her grief in the funeral chapters is the strongest feeling in the book and is played, uncomfortably, for comedy.

Becky Thatcher

The judge’s daughter, newly arrived in St Petersburg; first appears Chapter 3

She is courted, jilted, insulted and reconciled within a few chapters, and then given the novel’s longest ordeal in the cave, where she is the one who runs out of strength first. Twain grants her one real moment of moral weight (the torn schoolbook and Tom’s whipping) and then returns her to the plot as somebody to be rescued.

Injun Joe

The murderer of Dr Robinson; first appears Chapter 9

He is the book’s engine of fear and the clearest sign of its limits: a character built almost entirely out of period assumptions about mixed and Native ancestry, given a grudge, a knife and no inner life. His death behind the sealed cave door is the most disturbing scene Twain wrote for children, and the town treats it as a spectacle.

Muff Potter

The town drunk, tried for murder; first appears Chapter 9

He is persuaded by the real killer that he did it himself, and thanks the two boys who are letting him hang for the small kindnesses they smuggle through the jail window. His gratitude is what finally breaks Tom’s oath, which makes him the novel’s most effective moral argument.

Sid

Tom’s half-brother; first appears Chapter 1

He is quiet, clean and an informer, and exists to show what the town actually rewards. Twain uses him to keep the reader from mistaking the novel for a morality tale: the model boy is the one nobody, including the narrator, likes.

The Widow Douglas

The kindest and richest person in St Petersburg; first appears Chapter 5

She is marked for mutilation by Injun Joe because her dead husband once had him horsewhipped, and she is saved by a boy who has no reason to care except that she was once kind to him. Her reward for Huck is to civilise him, which the novel presents as real generosity and also a kind of confinement.
