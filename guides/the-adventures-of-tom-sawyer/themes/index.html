The Adventures of Tom Sawyer · Mark Twain

Themes

Work and play

The whitewashing chapter states a general law, that work is whatever you are obliged to do, and the rest of the novel puts it to use. Pirating, robbing, digging for treasure and being dead are all games until somebody starts firing a cannon over the river or counting out real gold, at which point the game does not stop; it simply acquires consequences.

A fence sold in Chapter 2, a pirate camp in Chapter 13, a robber gang still being planned in Chapter 35, with a murder trial, a manhunt and a cave in between.

“Like it? Well, I don’t see why I oughtn’t to like it. Does a boy get a chance to whitewash a fence every day?”

Tom Sawyer, Chapter 2

He had discovered a great law of human action, without knowing it⁠—namely, that in order to make a man or a boy covet a thing, it is only necessary to make the thing difficult to attain.

Chapter 2

Conscience

Tom’s oath makes silence a point of honour and lets a man go to trial for a murder two children watched. Twain writes conscience as physical symptoms (sleeplessness, a week of dread, inquests on dead cats that will not leave him alone), and when the confession comes it is drawn out by gratitude more than principle.

A blood oath in Chapter 10, a smuggled plug of tobacco through a jail grating in Chapter 11, and a witness box in Chapter 23 where the truth arrives at the last possible moment.

Every time I let him off, my conscience does hurt me so, and every time I hit him my old heart most breaks.

Aunt Polly, Chapter 1

“Tom, we got to keep mum. You know that. That Injun devil wouldn’t make any more of drownding us than a couple of cats, if we was to squeak ’bout this and they didn’t hang him.

Huckleberry Finn, Chapter 10

Respectability

The town runs on visible virtue: Sunday-school tickets that can be traded as well as earned, examination compositions copied from the same few moral endings, and mothers who forbid their sons to speak to the boy they all envy. Twain does not despise the village, but he never lets the reader confuse its standards with goodness.

A Bible won by commerce in Chapter 4, a model boy loathed in Chapter 1, and in the last chapter the same standards applied to Huck with a hairbrush and a clock.

He was not the Model Boy of the village. He knew the model boy very well though⁠—and loathed him.

Chapter 1

Aunt Polly, Chapter 1

Superstition

The boys’ world is governed by an elaborate system of charms, oaths and cures that they discuss with the precision of theologians, and the book takes it seriously as a working technology of childhood. It is also what puts them in the graveyard at midnight, so the novel’s central crime enters through a folk remedy for warts.

Dead cats and bean-burying in Chapter 6, an oath written in blood in Chapter 10, and the marked candles, superstitions and omens that structure the treasure hunt.

“Say⁠—what is dead cats good for, Huck?”

Tom Sawyer, Chapter 6

Huckleberry Finn, Chapter 10

Class and race in St Petersburg

The town is a slaveholding one, and its hierarchy is reported as ordinary fact: enslaved people do the work in the background, and the man the plot needs as its monster is identified almost entirely by his ancestry. A modern reading has to hold two things together: the accuracy of the record and the assumptions of the telling.

Present from the first chapters as the town’s ordinary furniture, concentrated in the treatment of Injun Joe, and left unexamined by an ending in which the whole village comes to see him buried.

Huckleberry was cordially hated and dreaded by all the mothers of the town, because he was idle and lawless and vulgar and bad⁠—and because all their children admired him so, and delighted in his forbidden society, and wished they dared to be like him.

Chapter 6

Injun Joe’s bowie-knife lay close by, its blade broken in two. The great foundation-beam of the door had been chipped and hacked through, with tedious labor; useless labor, too, it was, for the native rock formed a sill outside it

Chapter 33

Growing up

Twain ends the book with a note explaining that it must stop here, because to go on would be to write the history of a man. The novel is built to that boundary: it can hold murder, perjury and starvation, but not marriage, work or the future, and everything in it is dated by an adult who knows the town is gone.

Signalled by the Preface’s claim on real events, sustained by a narrator who keeps stepping outside the summer, and stated outright in the Conclusion.

Most of the adventures recorded in this book really occurred; one or two were experiences of my own, the rest those of boys who were schoolmates of mine.

Preface
