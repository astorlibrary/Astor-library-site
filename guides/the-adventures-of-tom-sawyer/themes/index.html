Work and play

The whitewashing chapter states a general law, that work is whatever you are obliged to do, and the rest of the novel puts it to use. Pirating, robbing, digging for treasure and being dead are all games until somebody starts firing a cannon over the river or counting out real gold, at which point the game does not stop; it simply acquires consequences.

Through the book A fence sold in Chapter 2, a pirate camp in Chapter 13, a robber gang still being planned in Chapter 35, with a murder trial, a manhunt and a cave in between.

In the text

“Like it? Well, I don’t see why I oughtn’t to like it. Does a boy get a chance to whitewash a fence every day?”

Tom Sawyer, Chapter 2

The rhetorical question does all the work: scarcity is invented out of nothing, and Ben is converted in four lines. Tom never claims the job is pleasant, only that it is rare, and the next paragraph makes that distinction a law of human action.

The pipe dropped from the fingers of the Red-Handed, and he slept the sleep of the conscience-free and the weary.

Chapter 13

The boys’ chosen titles are used straight-faced by the narrator, so that ‘the Red-Handed’ and ‘conscience-free’ sit in the same clause. The joke has an edge: Huck, who has no conscience to trouble him about running away, is the one of the three with a murder on his mind and can still sleep.

Conscience

Tom’s oath makes silence a point of honour and lets a man go to trial for a murder two children watched. Twain writes conscience as physical symptoms (sleeplessness, a week of dread, inquests on dead cats that will not leave him alone), and when the confession comes it is drawn out by gratitude more than principle.

Through the book A blood oath in Chapter 10, a smuggled plug of tobacco through a jail grating in Chapter 11, and a witness box in Chapter 23 where the truth arrives at the last possible moment.

In the text

“Tom, we got to keep mum. You know that. That Injun devil wouldn’t make any more of drownding us than a couple of cats, if we was to squeak ’bout this and they didn’t hang him.

Huckleberry Finn, Chapter 10

Huck’s reasoning is practical and correct, and it does more to keep them quiet than the oath they write in blood a few minutes later. The simile about cats returns the reader to the dead cat that took them to the graveyard; Twain often brings a comic prop back as a threat.

“Speak out, my boy⁠—don’t be diffident. The truth is always respectable. What did you take there?”

Chapter 23

The lawyer’s maxim runs straight into the answer, a dead cat, and the court laughs. Twain places the book’s most solemn statement about truth one line before its silliest object, which is his standard method for keeping a moral from hardening into a lesson.

Respectability

The town runs on visible virtue: Sunday-school tickets that can be traded as well as earned, examination compositions copied from the same few moral endings, and mothers who forbid their sons to speak to the boy they all envy. Twain does not despise the village, but he never lets the reader confuse its standards with goodness.

Through the book A Bible won by commerce in Chapter 4, a model boy loathed in Chapter 1, and in the last chapter the same standards applied to Huck with a hairbrush and a clock.

In the text

He was not the Model Boy of the village. He knew the model boy very well though⁠—and loathed him.

Chapter 1

The capital letters make the Model Boy an office, and the drop to lower case in the second sentence demotes him. ‘Loathed’ is much stronger than the joke needs, and it puts the book on Tom’s side early: the village’s system of rewards is hated by a boy who lives under it.

“Don’t talk about it, Tom. I’ve tried it, and it don’t work; it don’t work, Tom. It ain’t for me; I ain’t used to it.

Huckleberry Finn, Chapter 35

The repetition is exhaustion, and the complaint that follows is entirely about the body: washing, combing, clothes that let no air through, meals by the clock. Huck makes the only sustained case in the novel against what the town calls kindness, and Tom defeats it with an argument about club membership.

Superstition

The boys’ world is governed by an elaborate system of charms, oaths and cures that they discuss with the precision of theologians, and the book takes it seriously as a working technology of childhood. It is also what puts them in the graveyard at midnight, so the novel’s central crime enters through a folk remedy for warts.

Through the book Dead cats and bean-burying in Chapter 6, an oath written in blood in Chapter 10, and the marked candles, superstitions and omens that structure the treasure hunt.

In the text

“Say⁠—what is dead cats good for, Huck?”

Tom Sawyer, Chapter 6

The question is asked in the tone of one specialist consulting another, and the discussion that follows (spunk-water, bean-burying, the exact words to say) has the structure of a technical seminar. It is also the turning point of the plot: this conversation takes two children into a graveyard at midnight.

“Huck Finn and Tom Sawyer swears they will keep mum about This and They wish They may Drop down dead in Their Tracks if They ever Tell and Rot.”

Chapter 10

The capital letters are the boys’ idea of what a legal document looks like, copied from nothing they have ever read properly. The document is a game and a binding contract: at this point in the novel play and consequence can no longer be separated, and a man goes to trial because two children have signed a shingle.

Class and race in St Petersburg

The town is a slaveholding one, and its hierarchy is reported as ordinary fact: enslaved people do the work in the background, and the man the plot needs as its monster is identified almost entirely by his ancestry. A modern reading has to hold two things together: the accuracy of the record and the assumptions of the telling.

Through the book Present from the first chapters as the town’s ordinary furniture, concentrated in the treatment of Injun Joe, and left unexamined by an ending in which the whole village comes to see him buried.

In the text

Huckleberry was cordially hated and dreaded by all the mothers of the town, because he was idle and lawless and vulgar and bad⁠—and because all their children admired him so, and delighted in his forbidden society, and wished they dared to be like him.

Chapter 6

The sentence gives the official reason and the real one in the same breath, joined by an ‘and’ that pretends they are the same kind of thing. Notice the order of the adjectives, idle first and bad last: the town minds idleness before it minds wickedness.

Injun Joe’s bowie-knife lay close by, its blade broken in two. The great foundation-beam of the door had been chipped and hacked through, with tedious labor; useless labor, too, it was, for the native rock formed a sill outside it

Chapter 33

The passage is written as a forensic reconstruction (knife, beam, sill, the hollow worn in stone by dripping water), and the repetition of ‘labor’ makes the dying man’s work sound like the whitewashing chapter turned inside out. Twain then has the town hold a picnic at the cave mouth for the funeral, which is the harshest judgement in the book and is delivered without comment.

Growing up

Twain ends the book with a note explaining that it must stop here, because to go on would be to write the history of a man. The novel is built to that boundary: it can hold murder, perjury and starvation, but not marriage, work or the future, and everything in it is dated by an adult who knows the town is gone.

Through the book Signalled by the Preface’s claim on real events, sustained by a narrator who keeps stepping outside the summer, and stated outright in the Conclusion.

In the text

Most of the adventures recorded in this book really occurred; one or two were experiences of my own, the rest those of boys who were schoolmates of mine.

Preface

The claim is made in the flat voice of a deposition, and it is doing two jobs: it licenses the book’s cruelties as reportage and it warns the reader not to look for a moral design. Twain goes on to say that Tom is made from three boys and so belongs to the composite order of architecture, a joke that admits how much arranging has been done.

“Tom, Tom, we’re lost! we’re lost! We never can get out of this awful place! Oh, why did we ever leave the others!”

Becky Thatcher, Chapter 31

Becky is given the plain statement of the situation while Tom is still constructing reassurances, which reverses the usual distribution of realism in the novel. The exclamation marks and the repetition are the only hysteria the book allows anybody, and it is immediately followed by Tom keeping his voice cheerful: a performance, and the thing that gets them out.
