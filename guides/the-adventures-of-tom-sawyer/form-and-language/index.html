The Adventures of Tom Sawyer · Mark Twain

Form and language

Dialect

Regional speech written phonetically.

Huck’s speech is spelled as it sounds (’pears, widder, hain’t) against a narrator with a full literary vocabulary, and the gap between the two supplies much of the book’s comedy and sympathy.

Every time I let him off, my conscience does hurt me so, and every time I hit him my old heart most breaks.

Aunt Polly, Chapter 1

Narratorial irony

A narrator whose tone judges what it reports without saying so.

The mayor and his wife are introduced “among other unnecessaries”, and the whitewashing law of human action is credited to a great and wise philosopher, “like the writer of this book”.

Most of the adventures recorded in this book really occurred; one or two were experiences of my own, the rest those of boys who were schoolmates of mine.

Preface

Satire of institutions

Comic exposure of the machinery by which a community measures virtue.

The Sunday school, the examination evening and the Cadets of Temperance each get a full set piece, and each is shown rewarding the performance of virtue.

Mock-heroic

Epic apparatus (arms, councils, homecomings) applied to small domestic events.

Three boys on a sandbar style themselves the Black Avenger of the Spanish Main, the Terror of the Seas and the Red-Handed, and the Conclusion measures the whole book against the convention that a novel ends with a marriage.

“Huck Finn and Tom Sawyer swears they will keep mum about This and They wish They may Drop down dead in Their Tracks if They ever Tell and Rot.”

Chapter 10

The comic set piece

A self-contained episode built to a single disaster, complete in one chapter.

The fence, the Bible prize, the Pain-killer and the poodle on the pinch-bug each occupy a chapter, and each is constructed so that Tom’s humiliation and Tom’s triumph are the same event.

“Like it? Well, I don’t see why I oughtn’t to like it. Does a boy get a chance to whitewash a fence every day?”

Tom Sawyer, Chapter 2

Melodrama

Stage-villain plotting (oaths, graveyards, disguises, buried gold) running underneath a realist surface.

A midnight murder, a framed innocent, a masked revenge and a treasure under a cross are imported wholesale from popular drama, and are the reason the summer chapters acquire a body count.

“Tom, we got to keep mum. You know that. That Injun devil wouldn’t make any more of drownding us than a couple of cats, if we was to squeak ’bout this and they didn’t hang him.

Huckleberry Finn, Chapter 10
