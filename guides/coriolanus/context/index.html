Coriolanus · William Shakespeare

Context

Dates

1579
North’s translation of Plutarch’s Lives Thomas North’s English version of Amyot’s French Plutarch, and the source Shakespeare follows most closely of any he ever used.
1595
North’s Plutarch reissued A second edition kept the Lives in print and in circulation through Shakespeare’s Roman plays.
1599
Shakespeare writes Julius Caesar The first of the plays taken from North, and the one that sets up Shakespeare’s method of making Plutarch’s prose into verse.
1606
Shakespeare writes Antony and Cleopatra Usually dated to 1606 or 1607, and the immediate predecessor of this play in the Roman sequence.
1607
The Midland Revolt Risings against enclosure and grain shortage across the midland counties, often linked by scholars to the corn riot with which this play opens.
1608
The play is written Generally dated around this year, which makes it Shakespeare’s last tragedy.
1608
The King’s Men take the Blackfriars An indoor playhouse with a smaller, closer audience, where a long silence between two actors can be made to work.
1608
Plague closes the London playhouses A closure that ran on well into the following year and disrupted the company’s plans for the new theatre.
1623
The First Folio The play is printed first among the tragedies, from a manuscript whose stage directions are unusually full.

Places

Rome
The Forum, the Capitol and the street: almost every political scene in the play.
Antium
Modern Anzio, where Coriolanus arrives in disguise and offers Aufidius his throat. Corioli’s own site is not securely known.
Chaeronea
Plutarch’s home town in Boeotia; his Life of Caius Martius gave the play its plot and much of its wording.
Northampton
At the centre of the 1607 midland risings over enclosure and the price of corn, a year or so before the play was written.
Blackfriars, London
The indoor playhouse the King’s Men took on in 1608, well suited to a tragedy whose turning point is a silence.

Critics

William Hazlitt

Characters of Shakespear’s Plays (1817)

Hazlitt called the play a storehouse of political commonplaces, and argued that the language of poetry naturally falls in with the language of power: the imagination sides with the strong man against the hungry citizens.

T. S. Eliot

“Hamlet and His Problems”, in The Sacred Wood (1920)

Eliot thought Coriolanus might be less interesting than Hamlet but was, with Antony and Cleopatra, Shakespeare’s most assured artistic success.
