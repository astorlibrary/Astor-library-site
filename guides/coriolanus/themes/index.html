Coriolanus · William Shakespeare

Themes

Hunger and the distribution of grain

The play opens with men who want corn at their own price, before any abstraction about liberty, and it never lets the political argument float free of that. Menenius answers a material complaint with a story about the body’s parts working together, which is a good fable and a bad answer, and the play says so by keeping the citizens hungry.

The riot in 1.1 is settled by the grant of tribunes; in 3.1 Coriolanus objects to corn given out free, as was once done in Greece, to men who did not earn it.

Before we proceed any further, hear me speak.

First Citizen, 1.1.1

There was a time when all the body's members Rebell'd against the belly, thus accused it: That only like a gulf it did remain I' the midst o' the body, idle and unactive, Still cupboarding the viand, never bearing Like labour with the rest,

Menenius, 1.1.77

Voices and consent

The word “voices” means votes, and Coriolanus repeats it until it stops meaning anything, which is exactly his problem with it: he hears noise where the constitution hears consent. Rome’s new arrangement requires the powerful to ask, and he would rather starve than crave the hire he thinks he has already earned.

He collects voices in 2.3 with open contempt, the tribunes teach the citizens to withdraw them, and by 3.3 the same voices are a cry of curs that he claims to banish.

Before we proceed any further, hear me speak.

First Citizen, 1.1.1

What's the matter, you dissentious rogues, That, rubbing the poor itch of your opinion, Make yourselves scabs?

Caius Marcius, 1.1.149

The body and the body politic

Rome is described as a body from the first scene, and the metaphor is never allowed to stay comfortable: a belly that hoards, a great toe that leads a rebellion, wounds that must be shown in public, scars counted by a mother at home. What the state wants from Coriolanus is his body as evidence, and that is the one thing he will not surrender.

Menenius’s fable in 1.1 is answered by the demand for wounds in 2.3, and by Volumnia in 5.3 asking him to tread on the womb that bore him.

There was a time when all the body's members Rebell'd against the belly, thus accused it: That only like a gulf it did remain I' the midst o' the body, idle and unactive, Still cupboarding the viand, never bearing Like labour with the rest,

Menenius, 1.1.77

What's the matter, you dissentious rogues, That, rubbing the poor itch of your opinion, Make yourselves scabs?

Caius Marcius, 1.1.149

Mother and son

Volumnia states plainly that she made her son what he is, and the play offers no competing account of where his valour came from. The tragedy is that he obeys her twice: once in 3.2, when she teaches him to act, and once in 5.3, when the acting has to stop.

She counts his wounds in 2.1, coaches him in 3.2, and in 5.3 wins the argument and, by his own statement, kills him with it.

Marcius, I had rather had eleven die nobly for their country than one voluptuously surfeit out of action.

Volumnia, 1.3.19

it more becomes a man Than gilt his trophy: the breasts of Hecuba, When she did suckle Hector, look'd not lovelier Than Hector's forehead when it spit forth blood At Grecian sword, contemning.

Volumnia, 1.3.34

Names

He is given a new name for a city he took and has to be told what it is; he then discovers that the name is a possession which can be used against him. By Act V he will not answer to it, and in the last scene he is killed for insisting on it in front of the men he took it from.

He is hailed Coriolanus in 1.9, cannot recall the name of the man he wanted saved, refuses his own name in 5.1, and dies reciting the deed it commemorates in 5.6.

You common cry of curs! whose breath I hate As reek o' the rotten fens, whose loves I prize As the dead carcasses of unburied men That do corrupt my air, I banish you;

Coriolanus, 3.3.136

There is a world elsewhere.

Coriolanus, 3.3.151

Public performance and integrity

The gown of humility is a costume and the Forum is a stage, and everyone except Coriolanus can see that this is the ordinary condition of public life, with nothing scandalous in it. His refusal to act looks like the play’s only honesty, until his mother points out that he has been playing a part called ‘the man I am’ all along.

He cannot perform in 2.3, agrees to try in 3.2 and fails in 3.3 the moment he is called a traitor; in 5.3 he says he has forgotten his part like a dull actor, and that is when he becomes human.

He that will give good words to thee will flatter Beneath abhorring.

Caius Marcius, 1.1.153

Bid them wash their faces And keep their teeth clean.

Coriolanus, 2.3.55
