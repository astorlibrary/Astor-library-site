Hunger and the distribution of grain

The play opens with men who want corn at their own price, before any abstraction about liberty, and it never lets the political argument float free of that. Menenius answers a material complaint with a story about the body’s parts working together, which is a good fable and a bad answer, and the play says so by keeping the citizens hungry.

Through the play The riot in 1.1 is settled by the grant of tribunes; in 3.1 Coriolanus objects to corn given out free, as was once done in Greece, to men who did not earn it.

In the text

There was a time when all the body’s members Rebell’d against the belly, thus accused it: That only like a gulf it did remain I’ the midst o’ the body, idle and unactive, Still cupboarding the viand, never bearing Like labour with the rest,

Menenius, 1.1.77

The fable answers a complaint about hoarding by conceding the hoarding and calling it digestion. Its rhetorical trick is the opening ‘There was a time’, which shifts the argument out of this year’s harvest into a fable where the belly’s case has already been settled, and the citizens interrupt him twice to say so.

Anger’s my meat; I sup upon myself, And so shall starve with feeding.

Volumnia, 4.2.56

The play’s food imagery finally reaches the patrician household: where the citizens wanted corn, she eats her own rage and expects to die of the diet. It is also an exact description of what she does to her son in 5.3, where feeding on him and destroying him are the same act.

Voices and consent

The word “voices” means votes, and Coriolanus repeats it until it stops meaning anything, which is exactly his problem with it: he hears noise where the constitution hears consent. Rome’s new arrangement requires the powerful to ask, and he would rather starve than crave the hire he thinks he has already earned.

Through the play He collects voices in 2.3 with open contempt, the tribunes teach the citizens to withdraw them, and by 3.3 the same voices are a cry of curs that he claims to banish.

In the text

Bid them wash their faces And keep their teeth clean.

Coriolanus, 2.3.55

Standing in the gown of humility, he answers a question about political speech with a remark about hygiene, and the joke is aimed at the audience. The contempt is for their mouths, the parts that are about to give him what he needs.

You common cry of curs! whose breath I hate As reek o’ the rotten fens, whose loves I prize As the dead carcasses of unburied men That do corrupt my air, I banish you;

Coriolanus, 3.3.136

Everything he hates is airborne (breath, reek, corrupted air), just as their votes are only noise to him. Reversing the sentence is magnificent and changes nothing: a man can banish a city only from himself, and the city goes on without him for two more acts.

The body and the body politic

Rome is described as a body from the first scene, and the metaphor is never allowed to stay comfortable: a belly that hoards, a great toe that leads a rebellion, wounds that must be shown in public, scars counted by a mother at home. What the state wants from Coriolanus is his body as evidence, and that is the one thing he will not surrender.

Through the play Menenius’s fable in 1.1 is answered by the demand for wounds in 2.3, and by Volumnia in 5.3 asking him to tread on the womb that bore him.

In the text

What’s the matter, you dissentious rogues, That, rubbing the poor itch of your opinion, Make yourselves scabs?

Caius Marcius, 1.1.149

His first words wreck the fable in three lines: where Menenius made the citizens the limbs of a body, he makes them a skin disease on it. ‘Opinion’ is treated as an itch, a physical irritation with no content, which is exactly his view of the vote he will later have to ask for.

Trust to’t, thou shalt not—on thy mother’s womb, That brought thee to this world.

Volumnia, 5.3.127

She makes her own body the gate of the city, which completes the play’s long argument that Rome is an anatomy. Virgilia and the boy each add a line straight afterwards, so the threat arrives as a whole family, one after another.

Mother and son

Volumnia states plainly that she made her son what he is, and the play offers no competing account of where his valour came from. The tragedy is that he obeys her twice: once in 3.2, when she teaches him to act, and once in 5.3, when the acting has to stop.

Through the play She counts his wounds in 2.1, coaches him in 3.2, and in 5.3 wins the argument and, by his own statement, kills him with it.

In the text

Why did you wish me milder? would you have me False to my nature? Rather say I play The man I am.

Coriolanus, 3.2.14

He means ‘play’ as an accusation and it turns into a confession: the verb concedes that even being oneself is a performance. From here on the play measures him by which part he is willing to take, and in 5.3 he will use the language of the theatre again, as a man who has forgotten his lines.

let the Volsces Plough Rome and harrow Italy: I’ll never Be such a gosling to obey instinct, but stand, As if a man were author of himself And knew no other kin.

Coriolanus, 5.3.34

‘As if’ gives him away before the sentence ends: he knows the self-authorship is a fiction he is choosing to act out. Four scenes after a mother’s teaching failed to make him bow to a crowd, he is using her own vocabulary of performance to try to resist her.

Names

He is given a new name for a city he took and has to be told what it is; he then discovers that the name is a possession which can be used against him. By Act V he will not answer to it, and in the last scene he is killed for insisting on it in front of the men he took it from.

Through the play He is hailed Coriolanus in 1.9, cannot recall the name of the man he wanted saved, refuses his own name in 5.1, and dies reciting the deed it commemorates in 5.6.

In the text

There is a world elsewhere.

Coriolanus, 3.3.151

Five words close the act, and they are the only thing in the speech that turns out to be true: there is a world elsewhere, and it is Antium, and it kills him. After twenty lines of curses the plainness lands: he stops performing for exactly one line.

Name not the god, thou boy of tears!

Aufidius, 5.6.112

Aufidius has watched him for five acts and knows which word will work. ‘Traitor’ is a political charge and Coriolanus can argue with it; ‘boy’ is a charge about his mother. The insult is accurate, which is why it destroys him: he yielded to a woman, and he knows Aufidius watched him do it.

Public performance and integrity

The gown of humility is a costume and the Forum is a stage, and everyone except Coriolanus can see that this is the ordinary condition of public life, with nothing scandalous in it. His refusal to act looks like the play’s only honesty, until his mother points out that he has been playing a part called ‘the man I am’ all along.

Through the play He cannot perform in 2.3, agrees to try in 3.2 and fails in 3.3 the moment he is called a traitor; in 5.3 he says he has forgotten his part like a dull actor, and that is when he becomes human.

In the text

What custom wills, in all things should we do’t, The dust on antique time would lie unswept, And mountainous error be too highly heapt For truth to o’er-peer.

Coriolanus, 2.3.106

It is a radical argument, that custom preserves error, and he uses it to avoid asking poor men for their votes. Shakespeare often gives him positions that would be admirable from someone else, and that is why he is tragic and more than a snob.

Action is eloquence, and the eyes of the ignorant More learned than the ears—waving thy head, Which often, thus, correcting thy stout heart,

Volumnia, 3.2.85

She is giving him a lesson in stagecraft, complete with a demonstration of the gesture, and the phrase ‘the eyes of the ignorant’ shows she shares his contempt entirely. The difference between mother and son is competence: she can do the thing they both despise.
