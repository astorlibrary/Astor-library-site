Coriolanus · William Shakespeare

Form and language

The political fable

A story told to a crowd in order to settle an argument by analogy.

Menenius’s belly and its rebellious members is lifted from the historical tradition, and Shakespeare stages the citizens interrupting it, so the audience sees the device being used and resisted at the same time.

There was a time when all the body's members Rebell'd against the belly, thus accused it: That only like a gulf it did remain I' the midst o' the body, idle and unactive, Still cupboarding the viand, never bearing Like labour with the rest,

Menenius, 1.1.77

Crowd scenes

Staging a crowd as a set of distinct voices.

The citizens are numbered, disagree with each other, and reason out loud before they act, which makes the patricians’ contempt for them a claim the play can test.

Before we proceed any further, hear me speak.

First Citizen, 1.1.1

Animal imagery

Comparisons that place people on a scale from vermin to predators.

Curs, scabs, geese, hares, a lamb that baes like a bear, a grub that becomes a butterfly and a man who becomes a dragon: the insults define the political sides before the arguments do.

What's the matter, you dissentious rogues, That, rubbing the poor itch of your opinion, Make yourselves scabs?

Caius Marcius, 1.1.149

Imagery of the body

The state described as anatomy, and anatomy treated as evidence.

Wounds are counted, displayed, refused and finally invoked in 5.3, so the same body that took Corioli becomes the disputed ground of the political plot.

There was a time when all the body's members Rebell'd against the belly, thus accused it: That only like a gulf it did remain I' the midst o' the body, idle and unactive, Still cupboarding the viand, never bearing Like labour with the rest,

Menenius, 1.1.77

Stage directions

A moment of action written into the text with no words attached to it.

‘He holds her by the hand, silent’ is among the most eloquent stage directions in Shakespeare, and it gives the turning point of the tragedy to a pause.

O mother, mother! What have you done? Behold, the heavens do ope, The gods look down, and this unnatural scene They laugh at.

Coriolanus, 5.3.186

Heroic hyperbole

Description inflated past the plausible in order to make a man into a monument.

Cominius reports a soldier who went into a city alone and came out painted with blood; the play uses the language of epic while showing us the ordinary politics that epic cannot survive.

O, me alone! make you a sword of me? If these shows be not outward, which of you But is four Volsces?

Caius Marcius, 1.6.83
