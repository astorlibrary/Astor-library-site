Coriolanus · William Shakespeare

Characters

Caius Marcius Coriolanus

A Roman patrician and general, named for the city he captured; first appears 1.1

He is superb at the one thing Rome no longer principally needs and incapable of the thing it now requires. His refusal to display his wounds or ask for votes is usually read as pride, but it is also a refusal to let his body be turned into an argument, which is the only consistent principle he has.

Volumnia

Coriolanus’s mother; first appears 1.3

She built him: she says she sent him to a cruel war when he was still a boy and would rather have eleven sons die nobly than one live idly. She is also the best politician in the play, and the one person who understands that the Forum requires acting, which is why her final speech works and destroys him.

Virgilia

Coriolanus’s wife; first appears 1.3

She is the play’s other kind of attachment: where Volumnia counts wounds, Virgilia refuses to leave the house until her husband is home and cannot bear the word blood. Her husband calls her his gracious silence, and in 5.3 she speaks eleven words that reach him before any of his mother’s rhetoric does.

Menenius Agrippa

A patrician, and Coriolanus’s oldest friend in Rome; first appears 1.1

He handles the crowd with jokes and a fable about the belly, which is the most sophisticated piece of political management in the play and also a way of telling hungry men that the distribution is natural. His confidence that the general will listen to him is the play’s cruellest small humiliation.

Tullus Aufidius

General of the Volscians; first appears 1.2

He is the only man in the play who values Coriolanus as Coriolanus values himself, which is why exile takes him to Antium. The embrace in 4.5 is in the language of a wedding night, and by 4.7 he is already calculating how to be rid of a guest who has taken his army’s affection.

Sicinius Velutus

A tribune of the people; first appears 1.1

He reads a crowd the way a general reads ground, and knows that the surest way to defeat a candidate is to let him speak. His question in 3.1, “What is the city but the people?”, is the clearest statement of republican principle anyone makes, and he makes it while managing a prosecution.

Junius Brutus

A tribune of the people; first appears 1.1

The quieter half of the pair, and the one who does the detailed work: rehearsing the citizens on what they should have said, and planning how to put Coriolanus in a rage in front of the people. He is right that a consul who wants to abolish the tribunate is a danger to the people who elected him.

Cominius

Consul and general of the Roman army; first appears 1.1

He gives the long report of Marcius’s deeds at the Capitol, which is the only place in the play where the hero’s worth is set out at length by someone who was there. In Act V he comes back from the Volscian camp with the detail that says everything: the man would not answer to his own name.

The Citizens of Rome

The plebeians, who riot, vote and banish; first appears 1.1

Shakespeare gives them numbered voices with different opinions, so they argue with each other before anyone argues with them. They are mocked for changing their minds, and the play is careful to show exactly who worked on them each time they did.
