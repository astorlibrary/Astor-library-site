The Duchess of Malfi · John Webster

Form and language

Prose against verse

Moving between prose and verse to mark a change of rank, mood or purpose.

Bosola rails in prose and turns to verse when he moves from satire to meditation or to business, sometimes within one speech: ‘Observe my meditation now’ in 2.1 is the hinge. His prison speeches to the Duchess are prose, and her answer, ‘I am Duchess of Malfi still’, drops into them as a plain sentence that the verse around it cannot improve.

He and his brother are like plum-trees that grow crooked over standing-pools; they are rich and o'erladen with fruit, but none but crows, pies, and caterpillars feed on them.

Bosola, 1.1

Sententiae

A moral maxim, often a rhyming couplet, that draws a general truth from a particular event.

Webster closes scenes and speeches with rhymed sayings in the old style: ‘She’s oft found witty, but is never wise’; ‘There’s no deep valley but near some great hill’; ‘Like diamonds, we are cut with our own dust’. They sound settled and rarely are. Many come from the people least entitled to moralise, and the gap between the maxim and the speaker’s conduct is part of the effect.

Though lust do mask in ne'er so strange disguise, She 's oft found witty, but is never wise.

Bosola, 2.3

Animal imagery

Comparisons of people with beasts, measuring how far they have fallen from being human.

The brothers see their sister as a hyena and a screech-owl and her children as cubs and young wolves; she sees herself as a caged bird and, in her fable, a salmon. The imagery ends by becoming literal, when Ferdinand is met at midnight in a lane behind a church with a man’s leg on his shoulder, claiming to be a wolf.

Methinks I see her laughing,-- Excellent hyena! Talk to me somewhat quickly, Or my imagination will carry me To see her in the shameful act of sin.

Ferdinand, 2.5

Stage property

An object handled on stage that carries far more weight than its use in the scene explains.

Webster’s properties are bodies and parts of bodies: apricots eaten greedily as proof of pregnancy, a poniard handed over as advice, a dead man’s hand wearing a wedding ring, wax figures of a family, and a coffin, cords and bell brought as a ‘present’. The Cardinal’s poisoned Bible is the last of them, a holy object turned into a weapon.

I have a trick may chance discover it, A pretty one; I have bought some apricocks, The first our spring yields.

Bosola, 2.1

Dramatic irony

The audience knows what a character on stage does not, so the words mean more than the speaker intends.

The Duchess speaks her most intimate lines to a husband who has left the room and a brother who has come into it. The Cardinal warns his courtiers that he may feign cries for help, and dies calling for help that his courtiers take for play-acting. Ferdinand’s quip that such weddings are more like executions than celebrations turns out to be a plan.

Such weddings may more properly be said To be executed than celebrated.

Ferdinand, 1.3

The political fable

A short beast-story or allegory told inside the play to make an argument about power and worth.

Twice a character stops the action to tell a story. Ferdinand’s fable of Reputation, Love and Death tells his sister she has lost the one companion who cannot be recovered; her fable of the salmon and the dog-fish answers Bosola’s sneer at Antonio’s birth by pointing out that no fish is valued until it is dead. Both are about worth and who gets to fix it.

'Stay,' quoth Reputation, 'Do not forsake me; for it is my nature, If once I part from any man I meet, I am never found again.' And so for you: You have shook hands with Reputation, And made him invisible.

Ferdinand, 3.2

The malcontent

A stock figure of Jacobean drama: a disappointed, usually poor and educated man who rails at the corruption of the court he is trying to rise in.

Bosola is the type with its contradictions left in. Antonio sees straight away that he rails at what he wants, and Ferdinand tells him to keep his ‘old garb of melancholy’ as cover. The railing is accurate, which makes him the play’s best satirist, and it changes nothing about what he will do for money until Act IV.

He and his brother are like plum-trees that grow crooked over standing-pools; they are rich and o'erladen with fruit, but none but crows, pies, and caterpillars feed on them.

Bosola, 1.1
