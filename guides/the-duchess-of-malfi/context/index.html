The Duchess of Malfi · John Webster

Context

Dates

1498
Giovanna d’Aragona widowed, c. 1498 Her husband, Alfonso Piccolomini, Duke of Amalfi, died, and she governed the duchy for their son. Her brother Luigi was a cardinal.
1504
The play’s own date: Bosola’s horoscope reads 1504 The nativity Bosola picks up in 2.3 is dated 1504, which places the action, loosely, in the first years of the sixteenth century.
1513
Antonio Bologna killed in Milan, c. 1513 The historical Antonio Beccadelli di Bologna was murdered after the Duchess and their children had disappeared. Bandello, who says he knew him, names the leader of the killers as Daniele da Bozolo, the source of Bosola’s name.
1554
Bandello’s novellas printed The Duchess’s story appears in the first part of Matteo Bandello’s collection of tales, published at Lucca.
1567
The Palace of Pleasure, second volume William Painter’s English version of the story, taken from a French adaptation of Bandello, is the one Webster followed most closely.
1580
John Webster born, c. 1580 The son of a London coachmaker. Almost nothing is known of his life, and the date is an inference.
1610
Galileo’s telescope Galileo published his observations of the moon this year. The Cardinal’s reference in 2.4 to ‘that fantastic glass Invented by Galileo the Florentine’ places the play after it.
1610
Arbella Stuart’s secret marriage A cousin of James I married William Seymour without the king’s leave and was imprisoned; she died in the Tower in 1615. Her case has often been set beside the Duchess’s.
1612
The White Devil printed Webster’s first surviving tragedy of his own, another Italian story centred on a defiant woman, came out after a winter performance that he blamed for its poor reception.
1614
Performed by December, when William Ostler, the first Antonio, dies William Ostler, named as Antonio in the 1623 cast list, died in December 1614, so the play had been performed by then.
1623
The first quarto Printed by Nicholas Okes for John Waterson, with commendatory verses and a list of actors that covers two different casts.
1945
Ashcroft and Gielgud at the Haymarket A London revival with Peggy Ashcroft as the Duchess and John Gielgud as Ferdinand, in the last months of the Second World War.
2014
Chosen to open the new Sam Wanamaker Playhouse The candlelit indoor theatre at Shakespeare’s Globe, modelled on playhouses of the Blackfriars kind, opened its first season with this play.

Places

Amalfi
Webster’s ‘Malfi’, on the coast south of Naples. Act I, the first three scenes of Act II, 3.1 and 3.2, and all of Act IV take place in the Duchess’s palace.
Rome
The Cardinal’s city: his scene with Julia in 2.4, Ferdinand’s rage in 2.5 and the council of war in 3.3.
Loreto
The shrine of Our Lady of Loretto, ‘scarce seven leagues From fair Ancona’, where the Duchess’s feigned pilgrimage ends in the dumb show of banishment.
Ancona
The free city where Antonio takes refuge and whose state banishes the family at the Cardinal’s request.
Milan
The setting of Act V, where the brothers and Antonio converge and the historical Antonio was killed.
Blackfriars Playhouse site, London
The King’s Men’s indoor theatre, lit by candles, named first on the 1623 title page. Candlelight could make the darkness of Act IV a real effect there.
The Globe, Bankside
The company’s open-air playhouse, where the play was also acted. The candlelit Sam Wanamaker Playhouse, a short walk away beside the modern Globe, opened with it in 2014.

Critics

Charles Lamb

Specimens of English Dramatic Poets (1808)

Lamb chose the Duchess’s death for his anthology and wrote that to move a horror skilfully, to touch a soul to the quick and lay upon fear as much as it can bear, only a Webster can do.

T. S. Eliot

Whispers of Immortality (1918)

Eliot’s poem begins “Webster was much possessed by death / And saw the skull beneath the skin”, and fixed the modern picture of Webster as the dramatist of death.
