The Duchess of Malfi · John Webster

Characters

The Duchess of Malfi

A young widow who holds the dukedom of Amalfi as dowager; first appears 1.2

She is never given a first name. She is quick, funny and used to command, and she courts Antonio the way she would conduct business, by dictating to him. What her brothers call lust she treats as ordinary life: a husband, children, hair beginning to grey. In prison she is stripped of everything rank gave her, and speaks with more authority than at any point before.

Antonio Bologna

Steward, or great-master, of the Duchess’s household; her second husband; first appears 1.1

The best judge of other people in the play and the least able to act on his judgement. He opens with a speech on how a court should be ordered and wins the tilting at the ring, but once married he is always a step behind: he drops the horoscope, flees when told to, and walks into the dark in the last act hoping to talk the Cardinal into kindness. His virtue is real and his birth is low, and the question of which counts runs through the play.

Daniel de Bosola

Provisor of the horse to the Duchess; her brothers’ intelligencer; first appears 1.1

A scholar who served seven years in the galleys for a murder the Cardinal was thought to have commissioned, and came out unrewarded. He sees through everyone at court and says so in the play’s sharpest prose, then takes Ferdinand’s gold anyway. His contempt for the world is the malcontent’s, but his conscience keeps working: he will not see the Duchess ‘in mine own shape’, weeps over her body, and spends the last act on a revenge in which he kills the man he meant to save.

Ferdinand, Duke of Calabria

The Duchess’s twin brother; first appears 1.2

He speaks of honour and the royal blood of Aragon, but his language about his sister comes back again and again to her body and what she does with it. He will not give a reason for forbidding her to marry, and when he does give one, over her corpse, it is money. He hires Bosola and devises the torments of Act IV, then denies he ordered anything. By Act V he believes he is a wolf with the hair on the inside.

The Cardinal

Brother to Ferdinand and the Duchess; a prince of the church; first appears 1.1

Colder than Ferdinand and more dangerous for it. Antonio says he bribed his way towards the papacy and lays plots worse than any imposed on Hercules; Delio says he was thought to have commissioned the murder that sent Bosola to the galleys. He tells Ferdinand to calm down because the rage is noisy; he has no objection to it. He keeps a mistress, exchanges his hat for a general’s sword in public ceremony, poisons Julia with a Bible, and dies asking to be laid by and never thought of.

Cariola

The Duchess’s waiting-woman; first appears 1.2

Witness to the marriage, keeper of the secret and the one person the Duchess can talk to plainly. She thinks the match ‘a fearful madness’ and the feigned pilgrimage a jest with religion, and says so, but she stays. Her death is the counterweight to her mistress’s: where the Duchess kneels, Cariola bites, scratches and claims to be with child to live a minute longer.

Julia

Castruccio’s wife and the Cardinal’s mistress; first appears 1.2

She chooses her lovers as openly as the Duchess chooses in secret, and the play treats her with more comedy and less sympathy. She lies to her husband to reach Rome, fences with Delio over his gold, and in Act V holds a pistol on Bosola to declare that she loves him. Bosola uses her to draw out the Cardinal’s secret, and the Cardinal kills her for knowing it.

Delio

Antonio’s friend; first appears 1.1

The man Antonio talks to, which makes him the audience’s guide to the court in Act I. He is loyal, practical and a little worldly: he suggests the cover story of poisoned apricots, asks Pescara for Antonio’s land to find out where it is going, and advises against the midnight visit. He is the only survivor with a speech, and he uses it to set the heir in his mother’s place.

The Marquis of Pescara

A soldier, one of the Emperor’s commanders in Italy; first appears 3.3

One of the few men of rank in the play who behaves as rank is supposed to. He refuses Delio a grant of Antonio’s confiscated land because it was taken without course of law, and gives it instead to Julia as fit ‘salary’ for the Cardinal’s lust. In the final scene he is the one courtier who goes down to answer the Cardinal’s cries. He shows that greatness and goodness can meet in one man, which makes the other great men harder to excuse.

The Madmen

Inmates of the common hospital, sent to the Duchess’s lodging; first appears 4.2

A mad lawyer, a secular priest, a doctor, an astrologer, an English tailor, a gentleman-usher, a farmer and a broker, let loose by Ferdinand to sing and dance at his sister’s door. Their talk satirises the professions, from heraldry to law that eats to the bone, and the Duchess says that their noise is what keeps her sane. They are Ferdinand’s cruelty turned into entertainment, and a forecast of his own madness in Act V.
