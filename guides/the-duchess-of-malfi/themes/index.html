The Duchess of Malfi · John Webster

Themes

Marriage and the widow's choice

The Duchess marries without a priest, a witness of rank or her family’s leave, by a spoken contract she has heard lawyers call absolute marriage. In the wooing every ceremony takes on a private meaning: a will becomes a proposal, a ring a cure for a sore eye, a kneeling steward a man to be raised. Her brothers call remarriage lust. The play shows a household, three children and a woman who wanted an ordinary life and had to hide it.

The brothers’ warning and the wooing in 1.3; the domestic teasing of 3.2 that Ferdinand overhears; the dead man’s hand wearing her wedding ring in 4.1.

Such weddings may more properly be said To be executed than celebrated.

Ferdinand, 1.3

For I am going into a wilderness, Where I shall find nor path nor friendly clue To be my guide.

Duchess, 1.3

Rank and greatness

‘The misery of us that are born great’ is the Duchess’s line, and the play looks at it from every side. Rank obliges her to propose, lets her brothers imprison her without trial, and decides who is paid and who hanged. Antonio’s low birth is the whole charge against him. Images of height and falling run through the verse, from Bosola’s crooked plum-trees to the pyramid that ends ‘in a little point, a kind of nothing’.

Greatness is a privilege in Act I, a prison in Act IV and, in Delio’s last speech, a footprint in snow that melts when the sun comes out.

He and his brother are like plum-trees that grow crooked over standing-pools; they are rich and o'erladen with fruit, but none but crows, pies, and caterpillars feed on them.

Bosola, 1.1

For I am going into a wilderness, Where I shall find nor path nor friendly clue To be my guide.

Duchess, 1.3

Watching and being watched

The court runs on information. Bosola is hired as an intelligencer, reads the Duchess’s body for signs of pregnancy, obtains a false key to her bedchamber and draws out her secret by praising her husband. Her defence is concealment of her own, a hidden witness, a pretended crime, a pretended pilgrimage, and the play is unsentimental about the cost: the woman who marries behind an arras has entered the same world of secrecy as her brothers.

The arras in 1.3, the apricots in 2.1, the key and the eavesdropping in 3.2, and the darkness in which Ferdinand visits her in 4.1.

I have a trick may chance discover it, A pretty one; I have bought some apricocks, The first our spring yields.

Bosola, 2.1

Though lust do mask in ne'er so strange disguise, She 's oft found witty, but is never wise.

Bosola, 2.3

Service, reward and the intelligencer

Nearly everyone below the three siblings is a servant looking for preferment. Bosola went unrewarded for his years in the galleys and is refused payment for the murders he commits; Antonio is dismissed as a bad steward and the officers who flattered him jeer as he goes; the Cardinal’s courtiers promise to ignore his cries and keep their word. Webster shows service as a market in which loyalty is bought, and the servant is left holding the guilt.

‘I am your creature’ in 1.2; ‘the inconstant And rotten ground of service’ in 3.2; ‘rather sought To appear a true servant than an honest man’ in 4.2.

He and his brother are like plum-trees that grow crooked over standing-pools; they are rich and o'erladen with fruit, but none but crows, pies, and caterpillars feed on them.

Bosola, 1.1

Say, then, my corruption Grew out of horse-dung: I am your creature.

Bosola, 1.2

Reputation, blood and fame

The brothers claim to act for the royal blood of Aragon and Castile, and Ferdinand’s fable makes Reputation the one companion who, once lost, is never found again. The Duchess tells Cariola she has trusted her with ‘More than my life,--my fame’. The play’s own verdict comes last: great names melt like prints in snow, and only integrity of life survives its owner.

From the Duchess’s trust in Cariola in 1.3, through Ferdinand’s fable in 3.2, to Delio’s closing couplet in 5.5.

Though lust do mask in ne'er so strange disguise, She 's oft found witty, but is never wise.

Bosola, 2.3

Methinks I see her laughing,-- Excellent hyena! Talk to me somewhat quickly, Or my imagination will carry me To see her in the shameful act of sin.

Ferdinand, 2.5

Death and the body

Bosola sees the body as a box of worm-seed and a rotten corpse dressed in silk, and much of the play’s imagery follows him into tombs, charnel-houses and graves. The Duchess faces the same facts differently. She calls death the best gift her brothers can give, kneels for it, and spends her last instructions on her son’s cold. Her lines about death’s ten thousand doors are the play’s reply to Bosola’s disgust.

The alabaster figure she refuses to be in 1.3; the wax figures, the tomb-maker and the cords in Act IV; the echo from her grave in 5.3.

Such weddings may more properly be said To be executed than celebrated.

Ferdinand, 1.3

This is flesh and blood, sir; 'Tis not the figure cut in alabaster Kneels at my husband's tomb.

Duchess, 1.3

Conscience

Conscience comes late in this play and mends nothing. Bosola argues with his before taking the job, obeys it only after the murder, and finds a guilty conscience ‘a perspective That shows us hell’. Ferdinand’s breaks out as madness at the sight of his sister’s face. The Cardinal, who never seems troubled, spends his last scene alone with a book about hell, seeing a figure with a rake in his fish-ponds.

Bosola’s asides in 1.2; his refusal to appear in his own shape in 4.1; his tears over the body in 4.2; the Cardinal’s ‘How tedious is a guilty conscience!’ in 5.5.

Say, then, my corruption Grew out of horse-dung: I am your creature.

Bosola, 1.2

A politician is the devil's quilted anvil; He fashions all sins on him, and the blows Are never heard

Bosola, 3.2
