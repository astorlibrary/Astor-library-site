Marriage and the widow’s choice

The Duchess marries without a priest, a witness of rank or her family’s leave, by a spoken contract she has heard lawyers call absolute marriage. In the wooing every ceremony takes on a private meaning: a will becomes a proposal, a ring a cure for a sore eye, a kneeling steward a man to be raised. Her brothers call remarriage lust. The play shows a household, three children and a woman who wanted an ordinary life and had to hide it.

Through the play The brothers’ warning and the wooing in 1.3; the domestic teasing of 3.2 that Ferdinand overhears; the dead man’s hand wearing her wedding ring in 4.1.

In the text

Such weddings may more properly be said To be executed than celebrated.

Ferdinand, 1.3

In the pun a wedding is a death sentence, and it is spoken before any wedding has taken place. It sounds like a courtier’s quip, but Ferdinand is the one person in a position to make it literally true, and he does. The brothers share their warning so smoothly between them that the Duchess remarks the speech must have been ‘studied’.

The birds that live i’ th’ field On the wild benefit of nature live Happier than we; for they may choose their mates, And carol their sweet pleasures to the spring.

Duchess, 3.5

The comparison removes rank altogether. Birds choose their mates without asking anyone and sing about it in the open, the two freedoms her greatness has denied her. The image looks forward to her prison, where she will tell Cariola that the robin and the nightingale ‘Never live long in cages’.

Rank and greatness

‘The misery of us that are born great’ is the Duchess’s line, and the play looks at it from every side. Rank obliges her to propose, lets her brothers imprison her without trial, and decides who is paid and who hanged. Antonio’s low birth is the whole charge against him. Images of height and falling run through the verse, from Bosola’s crooked plum-trees to the pyramid that ends ‘in a little point, a kind of nothing’.

Through the play Greatness is a privilege in Act I, a prison in Act IV and, in Delio’s last speech, a footprint in snow that melts when the sun comes out.

In the text

He and his brother are like plum-trees that grow crooked over standing-pools; they are rich and o’erladen with fruit, but none but crows, pies, and caterpillars feed on them.

Bosola, 1.1

The image condemns the brothers and the man who wants to feed on them in the same sentence. The trees are rich but crooked, rooted in stagnant water, and only scavengers profit, among whom Bosola immediately counts himself: he would hang on their ears like a horseleech ‘till I were full’. The malcontent’s contempt and his appetite are one impulse.

For know, whether I am doom’d to live or die, I can do both like a prince.

Duchess, 3.2

The boast is made to an empty room and answered by the wrong man, with a dagger and ‘Die, then, quickly!’ Before it she has joked about her brothers, her greying hair and more children; she ends on her rank, which is where Ferdinand strikes. That she can in fact do both like a prince is what Act IV sets out to prove.

Watching and being watched

The court runs on information. Bosola is hired as an intelligencer, reads the Duchess’s body for signs of pregnancy, obtains a false key to her bedchamber and draws out her secret by praising her husband. Her defence is concealment of her own, a hidden witness, a pretended crime, a pretended pilgrimage, and the play is unsentimental about the cost: the woman who marries behind an arras has entered the same world of secrecy as her brothers.

Through the play The arras in 1.3, the apricots in 2.1, the key and the eavesdropping in 3.2, and the darkness in which Ferdinand visits her in 4.1.

In the text

I have a trick may chance discover it, A pretty one; I have bought some apricocks, The first our spring yields.

Bosola, 2.1

He tests a theory with bait. The first apricots of the year, which he later tells her were ripened in horse-dung, become a pregnancy test, and her greed for them is taken as proof. The scene is comic in performance and invasive in substance: a woman’s body is read without her consent, and the fruit brings on the labour it was meant to detect.

A politician is the devil’s quilted anvil; He fashions all sins on him, and the blows Are never heard

Bosola, 3.2

The ‘politician’, the stage schemer, is an anvil padded so that no hammer-blow is heard: every sin is shaped on him in silence. Bosola is describing himself as he goes to betray a woman who has just trusted him, and the next lines call his trade a ‘base quality’. He sees his corruption exactly and continues with it.

Service, reward and the intelligencer

Nearly everyone below the three siblings is a servant looking for preferment. Bosola went unrewarded for his years in the galleys and is refused payment for the murders he commits; Antonio is dismissed as a bad steward and the officers who flattered him jeer as he goes; the Cardinal’s courtiers promise to ignore his cries and keep their word. Webster shows service as a market in which loyalty is bought, and the servant is left holding the guilt.

Through the play ‘I am your creature’ in 1.2; ‘the inconstant And rotten ground of service’ in 3.2; ‘rather sought To appear a true servant than an honest man’ in 4.2.

In the text

Say, then, my corruption Grew out of horse-dung: I am your creature.

Bosola, 1.2

Bosola has just refused the gold as devils’ wages, and he takes the job anyway. The bitter joke about horse-dung looks forward to the apricots ripened in the same stuff in Act II. A ‘creature’ was a dependant made by a patron’s favour, and also a thing created: the word hands Ferdinand ownership of whatever Bosola is about to become.

I serv’d your tyranny, and rather strove To satisfy yourself than all the world: And though I loath’d the evil, yet I lov’d You that did counsel it; and rather sought To appear a true servant than an honest man.

Bosola, 4.2

The last line is the most exact self-judgement in the play. Bosola kept the act and the man who ordered it apart, loathing one and loving the other, and chose the look of loyalty over the fact of honesty. He says it to claim his pay; within a few lines, over the Duchess’s body, he discovers that the distinction does not survive what it has done.

Reputation, blood and fame

The brothers claim to act for the royal blood of Aragon and Castile, and Ferdinand’s fable makes Reputation the one companion who, once lost, is never found again. The Duchess tells Cariola she has trusted her with ‘More than my life,—my fame’. The play’s own verdict comes last: great names melt like prints in snow, and only integrity of life survives its owner.

Through the play From the Duchess’s trust in Cariola in 1.3, through Ferdinand’s fable in 3.2, to Delio’s closing couplet in 5.5.

In the text

‘Stay,’ quoth Reputation, ‘Do not forsake me; for it is my nature, If once I part from any man I meet, I am never found again.’ And so for you: You have shook hands with Reputation, And made him invisible.

Ferdinand, 3.2

Love can be found among shepherds and Death in battles, but Reputation, once parted from, is gone for good. The formal fable, in the middle of a violent scene, shows what Ferdinand believes he is defending, and it is a thing that exists only in other people’s opinion. The Duchess answers by asking why she alone, of all the princes of the world, should be ‘cas’d up, like a holy relic’.

My sister, O my sister! there ’s the cause on ’t. Whether we fall by ambition, blood, or lust, Like diamonds, we are cut with our own dust.

Ferdinand, 5.5

His last words go back to the beginning. His sister is the cause, and the couplet generalises it: ambition, blood and lust, the Cardinal’s, the family’s and his own, destroy the great as diamonds are cut with their own powder. The Duchess compared a widow who remarries to a diamond in Act I; the image returns to judge the men who objected.

Death and the body

Bosola sees the body as a box of worm-seed and a rotten corpse dressed in silk, and much of the play’s imagery follows him into tombs, charnel-houses and graves. The Duchess faces the same facts differently. She calls death the best gift her brothers can give, kneels for it, and spends her last instructions on her son’s cold. Her lines about death’s ten thousand doors are the play’s reply to Bosola’s disgust.

Through the play The alabaster figure she refuses to be in 1.3; the wax figures, the tomb-maker and the cords in Act IV; the echo from her grave in 5.3.

In the text

This is flesh and blood, sir; ’Tis not the figure cut in alabaster Kneels at my husband’s tomb.

Duchess, 1.3

The widow of convention is a stone figure kneeling on her husband’s tomb for ever, and the Duchess refuses the part in so many words. The line sets living flesh against carved stone, and Act IV reverses it with deliberate cruelty: she is shown wax figures of her family, Cariola tells her she looks like ‘some reverend monument’, and Bosola comes to make her tomb.

Didst thou ever see a lark in a cage? Such is the soul in the body: this world is like her little turf of grass, and the heaven o’er our heads like her looking-glass, only gives us a miserable knowledge of the small compass of our prison.

Bosola, 4.2

The soul is a caged lark and the sky a looking-glass that shows only how small the cage is. The image is traditional and the application is exact, since he is speaking to a woman imprisoned in her own palace. His purpose, he says later, is to bring her ‘By degrees to mortification’, and what he offers her is the argument that nothing she is losing was worth having; there is no comfort in it.

Conscience

Conscience comes late in this play and mends nothing. Bosola argues with his before taking the job, obeys it only after the murder, and finds a guilty conscience ‘a perspective That shows us hell’. Ferdinand’s breaks out as madness at the sight of his sister’s face. The Cardinal, who never seems troubled, spends his last scene alone with a book about hell, seeing a figure with a rake in his fish-ponds.

Through the play Bosola’s asides in 1.2; his refusal to appear in his own shape in 4.1; his tears over the body in 4.2; the Cardinal’s ‘How tedious is a guilty conscience!’ in 5.5.

In the text

Cover her face; mine eyes dazzle: she died young.

Ferdinand, 4.2

Nine words in three clauses, none of them about what he feels. ‘Dazzle’ can mean blurred by tears or by too much light, and either way he cannot look. Then, for the first time, he gives reasons: they were twins, and he had hoped to gain ‘An infinite mass of treasure by her death’. The line holds grief and guilt at the edge of speech.

We are merely the stars’ tennis-balls, struck and banded Which way please them.

Bosola, 5.4

The man who spent the play choosing his masters now blames the stars. ‘Banded’ is a tennis term, struck to and fro, and the image makes human life a game played by someone else. It is an evasion, and also, in an act made of accidents, a fair description of what keeps happening. The audience saw who was coming; Bosola did not.
