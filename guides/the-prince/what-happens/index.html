The Prince · Niccolò Machiavelli

What happens

Dedication; Chapters 1–5 The dedication, and the kinds of principality

Machiavelli offers Lorenzo the only valuable thing he owns, which is what he knows, and then divides the subject like a clerk opening a file: republics or principalities, hereditary or new, won by one’s own arms or another’s, by fortune or by ability. The long third chapter takes Louis XII through Lombardy error by error, and the fifth concludes that a conquered republic must be lived in or destroyed.

Dedication
The little volume is offered in place of horses and cloth of gold, with a closing reminder that its author is out of work.
Chapter 1
Four sentences that lay out every branch of the argument to come.
Chapter 2
Hereditary states are easy to keep; a prince of average powers will manage.
Chapter 3
Annexed territories, colonies against garrisons, and the six mistakes by which Louis XII lost Milan.
Chapter 4
Why Alexander’s successors kept Asia: the Turk’s kind of kingdom against the French kind.
Chapter 5
Cities used to freedom never forget it, so the safe courses are ruin or residence.

Chapters 6–11 Founders, Cesare Borgia, and the men who rose by crime

The centre of the book’s case material. Moses, Cyrus, Romulus and Theseus show what ability does with an opportunity; Francesco Sforza and Cesare Borgia set ability beside fortune; Agathocles and Oliverotto da Fermo raise the question of whether success by murder counts. Then come the prince raised by his fellow citizens, the test of a state’s strength, and the papacy, handled with careful mock reverence.

Chapter 6
New orders are the hardest thing to introduce; armed prophets win and Savonarola did not.
Chapter 7
Borgia’s rise on his father’s power, the execution at Cesena, and the one mistake Machiavelli will charge him with.
Chapter 8
Agathocles and Oliverotto: cruelty well used and badly used, and the difference between empire and glory.
Chapter 9
The civil principality; a prince needs the people more than he needs the nobles.
Chapter 10
A state is strong if it can take the field, or at least hold a fortified town with a loyal people inside it.
Chapter 11
Ecclesiastical states, and how Alexander VI and Julius II made the Church a power in Italy.

Chapters 12–14 Armies, mercenaries and Italy’s ruin

Three chapters that the author plainly thought the most urgent in the book. Hired captains are useless if they are bad and dangerous if they are good; borrowed armies are worse, because they win for somebody else. Italy’s ruin is traced to a century of mercenary war fought so as to hurt nobody, and the prince is told to make war his one profession, in peacetime most of all.

Chapter 12
Mercenaries, the condottieri and their bloodless battles, and Charles VIII taking Italy with chalk.
Chapter 13
Auxiliaries; Borgia’s progress from French troops to hired troops to his own; David refusing Saul’s armour.
Chapter 14
The prince’s study is war: hunting to learn the ground, history to learn the models.

Chapters 15–19 How a prince should behave

The chapters that made the name an adjective. Machiavelli announces that he is leaving imagined republics for the truth of the matter, and then works through the virtues in turn: generosity ruins a treasury, mercy can cost more lives than severity, a promise binds only while the reasons for it last. Each licence comes with a limit, and the limit is always the same: do not become hated or despised.

Chapter 15
The break with earlier writers: how one lives against how one ought to live.
Chapter 16
Liberality consumes itself; a reputation for meanness is one of the vices that let a prince govern.
Chapter 17
Cruelty and clemency, feared or loved, Hannibal’s discipline against Scipio’s mutiny.
Chapter 18
The fox and the lion, Alexander VI’s oaths, and the need to seem to have the virtues.
Chapter 19
Conspiracies fail against a prince the people esteem; ten Roman emperors are sorted by who hated them.

Chapters 20–23 Fortresses, reputation, secretaries and flatterers

Practical questions put to the rules already established. Fortresses help a prince who fears his people and cannot save one whom they hate; Ferdinand of Aragon shows how a run of great enterprises keeps subjects too astonished to plot; a prince is judged by the men around him. The chapter on secretaries is written by a man who had been one and would like to be one again.

Chapter 20
Arming subjects, fostering factions, building castles: the best fortress is not to be hated.
Chapter 21
Ferdinand’s pious cruelty, the folly of neutrality, and the advice always to choose the lesser evil.
Chapter 22
Three kinds of intellect, and the one test of a servant that never fails.
Chapter 23
How to be told the truth without losing respect; good counsel comes from the prince’s own wisdom.

Chapters 24–26 Fortune, and the call to free Italy

The argument closes on its largest question. The Italian princes blame fortune for losses their own idleness caused; fortune is given half of human affairs and no more, first as a river that can be banked and then as a woman who favours the bold. The last chapter drops analysis altogether and appeals to the Medici, in the language of Exodus, to raise a banner and free Italy.

Chapter 24
Naples and Milan were lost through their princes’ idleness in quiet times, and Machiavelli says they should not blame fortune.
Chapter 25
The river in flood, men who cannot change their nature, Julius II’s lucky haste, and fortune as a woman.
Chapter 26
Italy beaten, despoiled and waiting; the Medici urged to be her redeemer; four lines of Petrarch to end.
