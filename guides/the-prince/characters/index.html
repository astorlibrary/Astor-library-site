The Prince · Niccolò Machiavelli

Characters

Niccolò Machiavelli

The author: former secretary to the Florentine republic, writing unemployed; first appears Dedication

He is present in his own treatise more than its reputation for coldness suggests. He tells a French cardinal at Nantes that the French do not understand statecraft, talks with Cesare Borgia on the day Julius II is elected, and ends the dedication with a plain statement that he is suffering undeservedly. The chapter on choosing secretaries is written by a man who knows exactly how good a secretary he was.

Lorenzo de’ Medici

The dedicatee: Lorenzo di Piero, the young Medici then ruling Florence; first appears Dedication

He never acts in the book; he is the person it is trying to move. The dedication addresses him as your Magnificence and the final chapter addresses his illustrious house, and between the two he becomes the unnamed you to whom the advice on armies and cruelty is given. The treatise works as an application for employment made to the family that had its author tortured.

Cesare Borgia, Duke Valentino

Son of Pope Alexander VI; conqueror of the Romagna; the book’s chief modern example; first appears Chapter 3

Given a state by his father’s power and the French king’s soldiers, he set about making it his own: he broke the Roman factions, strangled his hired captains at Sinigalia, pacified the Romagna through a cruel deputy and then had the deputy cut in two. Machiavelli can find nothing to blame except the election of Julius II, and offers him for imitation although he lost everything, which makes him the test case for how much ability can do against fortune.

Pope Alexander VI

Rodrigo Borgia, pope from 1492 to 1503; Cesare’s father; first appears Chapter 3

He appears twice, and the two appearances are the book’s view of the papacy. In chapter 11 he is the pope who showed what money and arms could do for the Church, though all he meant to do was enrich his son. In chapter 18 he is the supreme example of the successful liar, a man who swore more solemnly and kept his word less than anyone living, and was always believed.

Pope Julius II

Giuliano della Rovere, pope from 1503 to 1513; first appears Chapter 2

An old enemy of the Borgias whom Cesare allowed to become pope, which the book calls the cause of Cesare’s ruin. He then serves three separate arguments: he reached the papacy with a name for generosity and dropped it to pay for his wars, he put himself in the hands of Spanish auxiliaries and was rescued by luck, and his headlong temper happened to suit his times so exactly that he never learned it could fail.

Francesco Sforza

Mercenary captain who made himself Duke of Milan; first appears Chapter 1

He is the first example the book names, and the cleanest case of a private man who became a prince by his own ability and then kept what he had won with little trouble. He is also a warning aimed the other way: hired by the Milanese to fight Venice, he joined Venice and took Milan, which is what a good mercenary does to his employer. His sons lost the duchy by avoiding the hardships of arms, and the castle he built made the family more trouble than any enemy.

Moses, Cyrus, Romulus and Theseus

The four founders named together as the highest examples; first appears Chapter 6

They are always cited as a group, and the grouping is itself the argument: a prophet who spoke with God stands in a list with a Persian conqueror and two heroes of legend, and is said to have done nothing the others did not. Each owed fortune only the opportunity, which in every case was a people enslaved, scattered or discontented. They return in the last chapter, where Italy’s misery is offered to the Medici as the same opportunity.

Fra Girolamo Savonarola

Dominican preacher who dominated Florence in the 1490s, executed in 1498; first appears Chapter 6

The one Florentine of Machiavelli’s own time to be given a place among the makers of new orders, and he is there as the failure. His order lasted exactly as long as the crowd’s belief did, because he had no means of compelling anyone once it lapsed. He is probably also the unnamed preacher of chapter 12 who said that Italy’s sins had brought the French, and who was right about the sins and wrong about which ones.

Agathocles of Syracuse

A potter’s son who became king of Syracuse by massacre; first appears Chapter 8

He rose through the army, called the senate and the richest citizens together one morning and had them all killed, then ruled securely for years and beat off Carthage. He is the book’s hardest case, because by its own tests he succeeded: nothing was owed to fortune, and the cruelty was done at one stroke. Machiavelli nonetheless refuses him a place among the most excellent men, and the refusal is the clearest sign that success is not the only measure in the book.

Oliverotto da Fermo

Mercenary soldier who seized Fermo by murdering his guardian; first appears Chapter 8

An orphan brought up by his uncle Giovanni Fogliani, he came home with a hundred horsemen, gave a banquet, moved the talk to a private room and had his uncle and the leading citizens cut down there. He held Fermo for a year. The story is told in more detail than any other in the book, and it ends with him strangled at Sinigalia by a better deceiver than himself.

Louis XII of France

King of France, twice conqueror of Milan; first appears Chapter 3

He is the pupil who gets every answer wrong. Invited in by Venice, he had two-thirds of Italy at his feet within months, and then strengthened the Church, brought Spain into Naples, crushed his small allies, never came to live in his conquest and planted no colonies. Machiavelli counts the errors on his fingers, and the count ends in the rule that whoever makes another powerful ruins himself.

Ferdinand of Aragon

King of Spain; the living model of a prince who has made himself great; first appears Chapter 1

He is praised in chapter 21 as almost a new prince, risen from a minor king to the first in Christendom by keeping his subjects permanently amazed: Granada, then Africa, then Italy, then France, each enterprise growing out of the last. The praise is double-edged, since he did it all under the cloak of religion, and he is generally taken to be the unnamed ruler of chapter 18 who preaches peace and good faith and is the enemy of both.

Septimius Severus

Roman emperor; the fox and the lion in one man; first appears Chapter 19

In the survey of ten emperors he is the only cruel one who died in his bed. He marched on Rome under pretence of avenging Pertinax, bought off one rival with the title of Caesar while he destroyed the other, and then turned on the first. Machiavelli draws a careful conclusion: a new prince should take from Severus what is needed to found a state, and from Marcus Aurelius what is needed to keep one that is already settled.
