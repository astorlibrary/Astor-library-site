Winning a state and keeping it

The book is less interested in how power is seized than in why it so often fails to last. Its recurring image is the foundation: a prince who inherits has his laid for him, a prince lifted by luck or by someone else’s army must lay them afterwards, at risk to the architect and the building. Almost every rule in the book is a rule about making a new state behave like an old one as quickly as possible.

Through the book Stated as a classification in chapter 1, tested on Louis XII and Cesare Borgia, and cashed in chapter 24, where a new prince who follows the advice is promised more security than an old family has.

In the text

just as those who draw landscapes place themselves below in the plain to contemplate the nature of the mountains and of lofty places, and in order to contemplate the plains place themselves upon high mountains, even so to understand the nature of the people it needs to be a prince, and to understand that of princes it needs to be of the people.

Dedication

In the comparison his lack of rank becomes his qualification: distance makes a subject visible. It is politely symmetrical, and the symmetry hides a cost to Lorenzo, since it implies that a prince cannot see what princes are like and needs somebody on the plain to tell him. The job application is folded inside the theory of knowledge.

there is nothing more difficult to take in hand, more perilous to conduct, or more uncertain in its success, than to take the lead in the introduction of a new order of things

Chapter 6

The explanation that follows is about interests: those who did well under the old arrangement fight hard, and those who may do well under the new one help lukewarmly because they have not seen it work yet. It is one of the few passages in the book about reform, as distinct from rule, and it explains why reformers so often turn to force.

Fortune and ability

Fortune in this book means something narrower than chance: the world’s habit of changing faster than men do. Ability, which is the translation’s usual word for virtù, is the capacity to be ready for the change or to change with it. Machiavelli gives fortune half of human affairs and then spends a chapter showing that her half falls hardest on those who built nothing in calm weather, which shifts a good deal of what looks like bad luck back on to the unlucky.

Through the book The founders owe fortune only an opening; Borgia is broken by an extreme malignity of it; chapter 25 tries two images, a river and a woman, and they do not give quite the same advice.

In the text

in the beginning of the malady it is easy to cure but difficult to detect, but in the course of time, not having been either detected or treated in the beginning, it becomes easy to detect but difficult to cure.

Chapter 3

The sentence is a chiasmus, with cure and detect changing places, so its form acts out the reversal it describes. The medical likeness makes statecraft a matter of diagnosis, which suits a writer whose trade was reading situations early. It also prepares the book’s case against fortune: most disasters called sudden were visible once to someone who was looking.

he told me that he had thought of everything that might occur at the death of his father, and had provided a remedy for all, except that he had never anticipated that, when the death did happen, he himself would be on the point to die.

Chapter 7

He told me is the only moment in the chapter where the author steps into the scene, and he does it to certify the one thing no planning covered. The sentence runs through a long list of provisions and then trips on except, which is how the career went. The book’s whole dispute between ability and fortune is here as an anecdote: a man can close every gap but the one he is standing in.

Armies

No subject gets more pages. A prince who depends on hired or borrowed soldiers does not possess his state; he is its tenant. The argument is military, but it is also the book’s theory of dependence in its plainest form: anything that rests on another’s goodwill, whether an army, an ally or a patron’s favour, will be withdrawn at the moment it is needed. Machiavelli had organised a Florentine militia himself, and these are the chapters where he sounds least detached.

Through the book Announced in the last clause of chapter 1, argued through chapters 12 to 14, repeated in chapter 24, and turned into the practical programme of the exhortation in chapter 26.

In the text

Hence it is that all armed prophets have conquered, and the unarmed ones have been destroyed.

Chapter 6

Prophet is the startling word. By choosing it Machiavelli puts Moses, whom he has just said one may not discuss, under the same law as Romulus: the people believed because they could be made to go on believing. Florentine readers had watched the unarmed case burn in their own piazza fifteen years earlier, so the generalisation rests on something the first audience had seen.

The chief foundations of all states, new as well as old or composite, are good laws and good arms

Chapter 12

Laws are named first and then dropped within two sentences, on the ground that good laws cannot exist where there are not good arms. The move tells a reader what kind of book this is: law, the usual subject of political writing, is treated as a consequence of force. Foundations is the book’s commonest figure, and here the building is every state there has ever been.

Cruelty, fear and hatred

The scandal is smaller than its reputation and more exact. Cruelty is well used when it is done once, for security, and then stopped; it is badly used when it grows. Fear is safer than love because the prince controls it, but fear is not hatred, and hatred is the one thing the book treats as fatal. The practical rule that follows is almost mild: leave your subjects’ property and their women alone.

Through the book First weighed over Agathocles in chapter 8, made a policy in chapter 17, and tested in chapter 19 against ten emperors, where the ones who die are those who were hated or despised.

In the text

men ought either to be well treated or crushed, because they can avenge themselves of lighter injuries, of more serious ones they cannot

Chapter 3

This is the first sentence in the book likely to shock, and it arrives as an aside in a discussion of costs. The reasoning is actuarial: the question is which injuries come back, whatever men deserve. The either and or leave no room for the ordinary middle course of governing, mild unfairness, which the sentence identifies as the dangerous one.

The barbarity of this spectacle caused the people to be at once satisfied and dismayed.

Chapter 7

Spectacle is exact: the killing is theatre, staged for an audience that hated the deputy and needed to fear the master. The two participles name the two effects a prince wants from a single act, gratitude and awe, and Machiavelli reports them as a critic reports a well-judged scene. The cruelty was Borgia’s policy throughout; the execution lets him disown it and profit from it together.

Seeming and being

A prince need not have the virtues, but he must seem to have them, and religion above all. The reason given concerns audiences more than ethics: everyone can see a ruler and very few can touch him, so his reputation is made by people judging at a distance and by results. Machiavelli adds that it is best actually to be merciful and faithful, provided the mind is ready to turn when it must, which requires a harder discipline than hypocrisy.

Through the book Prepared by the staged execution in chapter 7, stated outright in chapter 18, and extended in chapter 21 to Ferdinand, whose piety is an instrument of foreign policy.

In the text

it is necessary to be a fox to discover the snares and a lion to terrify the wolves.

Chapter 18

Older advice to rulers had used the lion for courage and the fox as a warning. Machiavelli keeps both animals and makes them a division of labour, each covering the other’s weakness. The detail worth noticing is that the fox only discovers snares; cunning is presented first as a defence against a world of wolves and trappers, and the licence to break faith is drawn from that.

always using religion as a plea, so as to undertake greater schemes, he devoted himself with pious cruelty to driving out and clearing his kingdom of the Moors

Chapter 21

Pious cruelty is the most compressed phrase in the book, an adjective and a noun that ought to cancel each other and are made to cooperate. The chapter calls the example admirable and rare, and the praise is technical, as one might admire a well-made engine. A reader is left to decide whether the coolness is approval or the driest kind of exposure.

Politics and morality

Chapter 15 sets aside the long tradition of books describing the perfect ruler and proposes to describe rulers as they are. Machiavelli does not say goodness is worthless; he says that a man who insists on it among so many who are not good will be destroyed, and his state with him. Necessity is the word that carries the weight: the prince must know how to do wrong and use the knowledge or not as the case requires.

Through the book Implicit from the first cold sentence about crushing men, declared as a method in chapter 15, and given its most uncomfortable form in chapter 19: good works can make a prince hated as surely as bad ones.

In the text

Yet it cannot be called talent to slay fellow-citizens, to deceive friends, to be without faith, without mercy, without religion; such methods may gain empire, but not glory.

Chapter 8

Anyone who thinks the book measures everything by success has to get past this sentence. Empire and glory are separated, and glory is withheld on grounds that are frankly moral. The awkwardness is that a few chapters later a prince will be told to break faith when he must; the difference seems to lie in the triple without, a life with no restraint at all as against a ruler who does wrong only under necessity.

how one lives is so far distant from how one ought to live, that he who neglects what is done for what ought to be done, sooner effects his ruin than his preservation

Chapter 15

The distance between is and ought is not denied, and it is not closed by lowering the ought; it is declared too wide to govern across. The argument is about survival, and ruin is its measure. What the sentence leaves unsaid is that a prince’s ruin is rarely his alone, which is the ground on which the chapters that follow will defend doing wrong.

The Church

Religion is discussed only by what it does. Moses is a founder with an army, the papacy is a principality that needs no defending because ancient custom defends it, and Ferdinand’s holy wars are a way of raising taxes and keeping barons busy. The tone towards the Church is elaborately respectful and the content is not: the popes appear as the power that kept Italy divided and let the foreigners in.

Through the book Louis XII’s first error is to enrich the Church in chapter 3; chapter 11 explains how Alexander and Julius made it formidable; chapter 26 borrows the miracles of Exodus to flatter a Medici pope’s family.

In the text

he who told us that our sins were the cause of it told the truth, but they were not the sins he imagined, but those which I have related.

Chapter 12

A sermon is accepted and rewritten in one sentence. The word ‘sin’ stays and its content changes: the offence was hiring other men to do the fighting, and luxury and impiety have nothing to do with it. The following sentence sharpens it by saying that these were the sins of princes, so the moral vocabulary of the pulpit is kept and pointed at a political class.

Alexander the Sixth did nothing else but deceive men, nor ever thought of doing otherwise, and he always found victims

Chapter 18

The example chosen for successful lying is a pope, named without comment, in a book addressed to a family that then held the papacy. With that exaggeration, nothing else and always, he becomes a controlled experiment: a man who broke every oath and was believed at the next one. The lesson is about the audience’s appetite for being persuaded.
