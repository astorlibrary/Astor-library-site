The Prince · Niccolò Machiavelli

Themes

Winning a state and keeping it

The book is less interested in how power is seized than in why it so often fails to last. Its recurring image is the foundation: a prince who inherits has his laid for him, a prince lifted by luck or by someone else’s army must lay them afterwards, at risk to the architect and the building. Almost every rule in the book is a rule about making a new state behave like an old one as quickly as possible.

Stated as a classification in chapter 1, tested on Louis XII and Cesare Borgia, and cashed in chapter 24, where a new prince who follows the advice is promised more security than an old family has.

just as those who draw landscapes place themselves below in the plain to contemplate the nature of the mountains and of lofty places, and in order to contemplate the plains place themselves upon high mountains, even so to understand the nature of the people it needs to be a prince, and to understand that of princes it needs to be of the people.

Dedication

men ought either to be well treated or crushed, because they can avenge themselves of lighter injuries, of more serious ones they cannot

Chapter 3

Fortune and ability

Fortune in this book means something narrower than chance: the world’s habit of changing faster than men do. Ability, which is the translation’s usual word for virtù, is the capacity to be ready for the change or to change with it. Machiavelli gives fortune half of human affairs and then spends a chapter showing that her half falls hardest on those who built nothing in calm weather, which shifts a good deal of what looks like bad luck back on to the unlucky.

The founders owe fortune only an opening; Borgia is broken by an extreme malignity of it; chapter 25 tries two images, a river and a woman, and they do not give quite the same advice.

in the beginning of the malady it is easy to cure but difficult to detect, but in the course of time, not having been either detected or treated in the beginning, it becomes easy to detect but difficult to cure.

Chapter 3

there is nothing more difficult to take in hand, more perilous to conduct, or more uncertain in its success, than to take the lead in the introduction of a new order of things

Chapter 6

Armies

No subject gets more pages. A prince who depends on hired or borrowed soldiers does not possess his state; he is its tenant. The argument is military, but it is also the book’s theory of dependence in its plainest form: anything that rests on another’s goodwill, whether an army, an ally or a patron’s favour, will be withdrawn at the moment it is needed. Machiavelli had organised a Florentine militia himself, and these are the chapters where he sounds least detached.

Announced in the last clause of chapter 1, argued through chapters 12 to 14, repeated in chapter 24, and turned into the practical programme of the exhortation in chapter 26.

Hence it is that all armed prophets have conquered, and the unarmed ones have been destroyed.

Chapter 6

The chief foundations of all states, new as well as old or composite, are good laws and good arms

Chapter 12

Cruelty, fear and hatred

The scandal is smaller than its reputation and more exact. Cruelty is well used when it is done once, for security, and then stopped; it is badly used when it grows. Fear is safer than love because the prince controls it, but fear is not hatred, and hatred is the one thing the book treats as fatal. The practical rule that follows is almost mild: leave your subjects’ property and their women alone.

First weighed over Agathocles in chapter 8, made a policy in chapter 17, and tested in chapter 19 against ten emperors, where the ones who die are those who were hated or despised.

men ought either to be well treated or crushed, because they can avenge themselves of lighter injuries, of more serious ones they cannot

Chapter 7

Seeming and being

A prince need not have the virtues, but he must seem to have them, and religion above all. The reason given concerns audiences more than ethics: everyone can see a ruler and very few can touch him, so his reputation is made by people judging at a distance and by results. Machiavelli adds that it is best actually to be merciful and faithful, provided the mind is ready to turn when it must, which requires a harder discipline than hypocrisy.

Prepared by the staged execution in chapter 7, stated outright in chapter 18, and extended in chapter 21 to Ferdinand, whose piety is an instrument of foreign policy.

The barbarity of this spectacle caused the people to be at once satisfied and dismayed.

Chapter 7

it is necessary to be a fox to discover the snares and a lion to terrify the wolves.

Chapter 18

Politics and morality

Chapter 15 sets aside the long tradition of books describing the perfect ruler and proposes to describe rulers as they are. Machiavelli does not say goodness is worthless; he says that a man who insists on it among so many who are not good will be destroyed, and his state with him. Necessity is the word that carries the weight: the prince must know how to do wrong and use the knowledge or not as the case requires.

Implicit from the first cold sentence about crushing men, declared as a method in chapter 15, and given its most uncomfortable form in chapter 19: good works can make a prince hated as surely as bad ones.

Yet it cannot be called talent to slay fellow-citizens, to deceive friends, to be without faith, without mercy, without religion; such methods may gain empire, but not glory.

Chapter 8

how one lives is so far distant from how one ought to live, that he who neglects what is done for what ought to be done, sooner effects his ruin than his preservation

Chapter 15

The Church

Religion is discussed only by what it does. Moses is a founder with an army, the papacy is a principality that needs no defending because ancient custom defends it, and Ferdinand’s holy wars are a way of raising taxes and keeping barons busy. The tone towards the Church is elaborately respectful and the content is not: the popes appear as the power that kept Italy divided and let the foreigners in.

Louis XII’s first error is to enrich the Church in chapter 3; chapter 11 explains how Alexander and Julius made it formidable; chapter 26 borrows the miracles of Exodus to flatter a Medici pope’s family.

Hence it is that all armed prophets have conquered, and the unarmed ones have been destroyed.
