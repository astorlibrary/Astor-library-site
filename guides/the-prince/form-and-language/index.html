The Prince · Niccolò Machiavelli

Form and language

Argument by example

Proving a general rule by setting out particular cases, usually one ancient and one modern.

Nearly every chapter states a rule and then produces a ruler who kept it or broke it. The cases do real work: Agathocles and Borgia strain the rules they are brought in to prove, and the book is most interesting where an example will not fit.

he who is the cause of another becoming powerful is ruined

Chapter 3

Antithesis

Setting two opposed terms against each other so that the choice between them seems to exhaust the subject.

The first chapter is nothing but forks: republics or principalities, hereditary or new, fortune or ability. The habit persists at every scale, down to sentences such as well treated or crushed, and it makes each conclusion sound like the only road left.

men ought either to be well treated or crushed, because they can avenge themselves of lighter injuries, of more serious ones they cannot

Chapter 3

The maxim

A short balanced sentence that sounds like settled wisdom and usually turns a received idea inside out.

Chapters tend to end on a sentence built to be carried away: men forget a father’s death sooner than a lost inheritance; whoever makes another powerful is ruined. These are the lines that travelled through Europe without the qualifications that surround them on the page.

men ought either to be well treated or crushed, because they can avenge themselves of lighter injuries, of more serious ones they cannot

Chapter 3

Analogy

Explaining an abstract matter through a concrete likeness that carries part of the argument.

A plain book saves its images for the places where reasoning runs out: the landscape painter, the archer aiming high, the physician’s hectic fever, the fox and the lion, the river in flood. Each makes a disputable claim feel like common sense.

just as those who draw landscapes place themselves below in the plain to contemplate the nature of the mountains and of lofty places, and in order to contemplate the plains place themselves upon high mountains, even so to understand the nature of the people it needs to be a prince, and to understand that of princes it needs to be of the people.

Dedication

Direct address

A narrator or speaker turning outward to address the reader or the audience by name.

The grammar keeps sliding from a prince ought to you must. The shift usually comes at the hardest advice, so that the reader, and first of all Lorenzo, finds himself already standing in the position being described.

Every one sees what you appear to be, few really know what you are

Chapter 18

Objection and reply

Raising the reader’s likely objection in the reader’s own voice and answering it on the spot.

And if any one should say, And if it be urged, Some may wonder: the manner is that of a man used to defending a policy in committee. It lets him admit the awkward case, Caesar’s generosity or the good emperors who were murdered, before an opponent can bring it.

he who told us that our sins were the cause of it told the truth, but they were not the sins he imagined, but those which I have related.

Chapter 12

Plain style

Prose that declines ornament and claims the authority of the matter alone.

The dedication promises no swelling words or rounded periods, and mostly Machiavelli keeps the promise, which makes its departures audible: the mock piety about the popes in chapter 11, and the prophetic rhetoric of chapter 26.

just as those who draw landscapes place themselves below in the plain to contemplate the nature of the mountains and of lofty places, and in order to contemplate the plains place themselves upon high mountains, even so to understand the nature of the people it needs to be a prince, and to understand that of princes it needs to be of the people.
