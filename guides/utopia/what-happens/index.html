Utopia · Thomas More

What happens

Book I More, Giles and Hythloday

More describes his embassy to Flanders and his friendship with Peter Giles, who introduces him outside a church in Antwerp to Raphael Hythloday. In More’s garden the three talk about the New World. When Giles and More urge Hythloday to put his learning at a king’s service, he refuses: courts want flattery, and they meet every new idea by appealing to what pleased their ancestors.

Book I
Henry VIII sends More to Flanders; the talks stall at Bruges and he goes to Antwerp, where Giles’s company eases his homesickness.
Book I
Giles describes Hythloday’s voyages with Vespucci and his return by way of Ceylon and Calicut.
Book I
Hythloday says he has given his estate away and will not enslave himself to any prince; More replies that a good counsellor can do a whole nation good.

Book I Dinner with Cardinal Morton

To show how counsel is received, Hythloday recalls a dinner at the table of John Morton, Archbishop of Canterbury, Cardinal and Chancellor, in about 1497. A lawyer praises the hanging of thieves; Hythloday argues that England makes thieves through idle retainers, enclosure and sheep, and proposes instead the penal labour of the Polylerits. The company scorns the idea until the Cardinal approves it. A fool’s joke about beggars and friars turns into a quarrel with an angry friar, and Morton ends the dinner.

Book I
The lawyer praises severe punishment for theft; Hythloday answers that no penalty deters men who have no other way to live.
Book I
Enclosure, rising prices and idle households are blamed for making the thieves the law then hangs.
Book I
A people tributary to Persia set thieves to public work instead of killing them; Morton proposes a trial of the method.
Book I
A jester’s plan for beggars and a friar’s scriptural rage show the table taking its cue from whatever the Cardinal smiles at.

Book I Philosophy at court, and the case against property

Hythloday imagines himself in the French king’s council advising against the conquest of Italy, with the example of the Achorians, and among ministers raising money by debasing the coinage and reviving old laws, with the Macarians’ limit on a king’s treasure. More grants that such advice would be ignored but argues for a pliable philosophy that plays its part in the drama in hand. Hythloday answers that the only cure is to abolish property; More objects that nobody would work, and Hythloday promises to describe Utopia after dinner.

Book I
A king’s advisers plot leagues and invasions; counsel to stay at home and govern well would not be heard.
Book I
Ministers propose debasing the coin and selling dispensations; Hythloday would cite the Macarians’ oath never to hold more than a thousand pounds of gold.
Book I
More compares counsel to acting in a play already under way; Hythloday says the preachers have bent Christ’s teaching like a leaden rule.
Book I
Hythloday names private property as the root of the trouble; More and Giles object, and the three go in to dine.

Book II, opening to ‘Of their Trades’ The shape of the island

After dinner Hythloday describes a crescent-shaped island, cut off from the mainland by King Utopus’s channel, with fifty-four cities built to one plan and farms worked by townspeople in two-year shifts. Amaurot, the capital, stands on the tidal river Anider, with gardens behind every house and doors that anyone may open. Magistrates are elected by households in thirties and three hundreds, and every citizen works six hours a day at farming and a trade.

Book II, opening
Utopus digs the channel; the cities stand a day’s walk apart; townspeople take turns on the land.
Book II, Of their Towns, particularly of Amaurot
Walls, a stone bridge, uniform streets, houses exchanged by lot every ten years, and gardens kept in rivalry.
Book II, Of their Magistrates
Elected officers, a prince chosen for life, three days’ debate on any public question, and death for plotting outside the council.
Book II, Of their Trades, and Manner of Life
Everyone farms and learns a trade; idleness is abolished, lectures fill the early mornings, and luxuries are not made.

Book II, ‘Of their Traffic’ to ‘Of their Slaves’ Living without money

Households of fixed size take what they need from common stores and eat together in halls. Surplus is exported, and the gold it brings is kept for war and made into chamber-pots and chains so that nobody will prize it. Hythloday tells of the Anemolian ambassadors who arrive dressed in gold and are taken for slaves, then sets out the Utopians’ philosophy of pleasure, their appetite for Greek learning, and their laws on slavery, the incurably sick, marriage, divorce and treaties.

Book II, Of their Traffic
Children moved between households, colonies planted abroad, common stores and common meals, hospitals outside the walls.
Book II, Of the Travelling of the Utopians
Travel by passport, gold for chamber-pots, the Anemolian embassy, true and false pleasures, and the Greek books Hythloday left behind.
Book II, Of their Slaves, and of their Marriages
Slavery as punishment, counselled death for the incurably suffering, betrothed couples shown to each other naked, few laws, no lawyers and no treaties.

Book II, ‘Of their Military Discipline’ to the end War, worship and the last word

The Utopians detest war and wage it with bribes, bounties on enemy princes and mercenaries whose deaths they count a service to mankind. Their religions are many and protected by a law of Utopus, provided nobody denies Providence or the soul’s survival. Hythloday closes by calling every other commonwealth a conspiracy of the rich, and pride the serpent that stops the world from following Utopia. More, privately unconvinced, praises the account, leads him in to supper and says that there are many things he would ‘rather wish, than hope’ to see followed.

Book II, Of their Military Discipline
Just causes, rewards for killing an enemy prince, Zapolet mercenaries, wives fighting beside their husbands.
Book II, Of the Religions of the Utopians
Many faiths under one Supreme Being, Utopus’s law against compulsion, few and holy priests, temples kept dim.
Book II, Of the Religions of the Utopians
Hythloday’s attack on money and pride; the narrator’s unspoken objections and his closing wish.
