Utopia · Thomas More

Characters

Raphael Hythloday

A Portuguese traveller and philosopher; the book’s main speaker; first appears Book I

He gave his estate to his brothers while young, sailed on three of Vespucci’s four voyages, stayed behind in the New World and lived five years in Utopia. He is an eloquent talker, argues from examples, and will not compromise: he refuses royal service because he could neither lie nor be heard, and he names private property as the disease that every other reform only soothes. More gives him a surname that means something like “dealer in nonsense”, and the angriest, most persuasive pages in the book.

More, the narrator

An English envoy in Flanders and the teller of the book; not quite the same person as its author; first appears Book I

He shares the author’s name, office and family, and the book makes him a little less than the author: courteous, practical, a lawyer who believes a good man should serve the state and work within its bad habits. He argues against Hythloday twice, for a pliable philosophy that plays its part in the drama in hand and against holding all things in common, and at the end he keeps his objections to himself because his guest looks tired. His last sentence is the book’s most quoted hedge.

Peter Giles

A scholar and city secretary of Antwerp; More’s friend; first appears Book I

A real person and a friend of Erasmus, whom More praises at length for his learning, his modesty and his ‘prudent simplicity’. In the fiction he is the introducer: he brings Hythloday to More, urges the traveller to serve a king for his friends’ sake, and doubts that any nation in the New World can be better governed than old Europe. His presence gives the fantasy a real address.

Cardinal John Morton

Archbishop of Canterbury and Lord Chancellor under Henry VII; first appears Book I

The one great man in the book who listens. Hythloday describes him as grave, easy in conversation and fond of testing suitors by speaking sharply; he stops the lawyer’s set speech, asks a real question about how thieves should be punished, and proposes a trial of the Polylerits’ method. Yet his table is where the case against courts is proved. The guests despise an idea until he approves it, and applaud in earnest what he only liked in jest.

The lawyer

An English lawyer at Morton’s table, later called ‘the Counsellor’; first appears Book I

He opens the argument by praising a justice that hangs twenty thieves on one gibbet, and wonders aloud why theft continues. Answered, he prepares a formal rebuttal that will repeat everything in order and answer nothing, and the Cardinal stops him before he starts. His last contribution is a shake of the head and a prediction that the scheme would endanger the whole nation. Burnet’s English calls him “the Counsellor”, which makes him the working model of the royal adviser Hythloday refuses to become.

The Friar

A divine at Morton’s table; first appears Book I

A grave, morose man who enjoys the Fool’s joke at the monks’ expense until it turns on the friars. He then rails at the jester as knave, backbiter and son of perdition, quotes scripture to prove that his anger is no sin, and threatens the fate of the children who mocked Elisha and a papal bull. He is the book’s portrait of learning used for self-defence.

The Fool

A jester at Morton’s table; first appears Book I

He plays the fool so naturally that he seems to be one, and most of his jokes fall flat, but he is the only guest who says something rude and true: that the answer to beggars is to put them in monasteries, and that no vagabonds are like the friars. The company likes the jest in earnest because the Cardinal smiles. Erasmus had lately punned More’s name into folly in The Praise of Folly, and the jester is a small version of the licensed truth-teller the whole book tries to be.

King Utopus

Conqueror and lawgiver of Utopia; first appears Book II, opening

He conquered a peninsula called Abraxa, gave it his own name, and had it cut from the mainland by a fifteen-mile channel dug by his soldiers as well as the natives. He drew the plan of Amaurot and left its ornament to later generations, and he made the law that every man may follow what religion he pleases and argue for it without violence. The ideal commonwealth, it is worth noticing, begins with a conquest.

The Utopians

The citizens of the island, taken as a people; first appears Book II, opening

They are industrious, cheerful, fond of lectures and gardens, contemptuous of gold, jewels, hunting and dice, and quick to learn Greek. They also live in full view of one another, need a passport to leave their city, keep slaves, marry after inspecting each other naked, bar from office anyone who denies Providence, and win wars with bribes. Hythloday presents them almost as a single character with one mind, which is the attraction of the place and also its strangeness.

Syphogrants, Tranibors and the Prince

Utopia’s elected officers; first appears Book II, Of their Magistrates

Every thirty households elect a Syphogrant each year, and every ten Syphogrants answer to a Tranibor; the Syphogrants choose a Prince for life, by secret vote, from four candidates named by the people, and he can be removed if he is suspected of meaning to enslave them. The Syphogrants’ chief business is to see that nobody is idle. The system is built against conspiracy, down to the rule that no question is debated on the day it is raised and the law that makes it death to discuss public affairs outside council.
