Utopia · Thomas More

Themes

Property, money and the common store

Hythloday’s central claim is that while there is property, and money is the measure of everything, no nation can be governed either justly or happily. Book II tests the claim: households take what they need from public stores, cities supply one another as though the island were one family, and gold is made into chamber-pots. The book lets the narrator raise the obvious objection, that without hope of gain nobody will work, and answers it with the six-hour day.

Named as the root cause at the end of Book I; put into practice in ‘Of their Traffic’ and ‘Of the Travelling of the Utopians’; restated in the closing speech, where every other commonwealth is ‘a conspiracy of the rich’.

your sheep, which are naturally mild, and easily kept in order, may be said now to devour men and unpeople, not only villages, but towns

Hythloday, Book I

for if you suffer your people to be ill-educated, and their manners to be corrupted from their infancy, and then punish them for those crimes to which their first education disposed them, what else is to be concluded from this but that you first make thieves and then punish them?

Hythloday, Book I

Crime, punishment and justice

The argument of the book begins at a gallows. Hythloday tells an English lawyer that hanging thieves is neither just nor effective, since a country that drives men off the land and then hangs them for stealing has made the thieves it punishes. The Polylerits’ penal labour, Utopian slavery and the maxim that ‘extreme justice is an extreme injury’ all turn on one question: what punishment is for. The answers are humane by the standards of 1516 and hard by later ones.

Morton’s dinner in Book I; the Utopians’ use of slavery in place of death and their few, plain laws in ‘Of their Slaves’; the ‘two sorts of justice’, one for the poor and one for princes, in the same section.

were then hanged so fast that there were sometimes twenty on one gibbet!

The lawyer, Book I

your sheep, which are naturally mild, and easily kept in order, may be said now to devour men and unpeople, not only villages, but towns

Hythloday, Book I

Counsel

Book I is an argument between two men about whether a philosopher can do any good at court. Hythloday says he would be ignored, or corrupted, or blamed for other men’s crimes; More says a man should play his part in the drama in hand and make bad things as little bad as he can. The question was not abstract. Within a few years of writing it the author was in Henry VIII’s service; in 1535 the same king had him beheaded.

Raised by Peter Giles’s suggestion that Hythloday serve a king; illustrated at Morton’s table; argued to a standstill in the imagined French council; left unresolved when the talk turns to Utopia and again in the narrator’s last sentence.

I think my friends ought to rest contented with this, and not to expect that for their sakes I should enslave myself to any king whatsoever.

Hythloday, Book I

for the springs both of good and evil flow from the prince over a whole nation, as from a lasting fountain.

More, Book I

Pleasure, true and false

The Utopians hold that happiness lies in pleasure, and spend pages sorting the true kind from the false: health, learning, a good conscience and music on one side; fine clothes, titles, jewels, hoarded gold, hunting and dice on the other. The philosophy has a religious floor under it, since they think virtue would be madness if it brought no reward after death. It is also the book’s sharpest satire, because it makes the pleasures Europe prizes look like a child’s.

Prepared by the gold and the Anemolian ambassadors, argued at length in ‘Of the Travelling of the Utopians’, and carried over into their treatment of the sick and dying in ‘Of their Slaves’.

it is the fear of want that makes any of the whole race of animals either greedy or ravenous; but, besides fear, there is in man a pride that makes him fancy it a particular glory to excel others in pomp and excess

Hythloday, Book II, Of their Traffic

They eat and drink out of vessels of earth or glass, which make an agreeable appearance, though formed of brittle materials; while they make their chamber-pots and close-stools of gold and silver

Hythloday, Book II, Of the Travelling of the Utopians

Religion and toleration

Utopus found the island’s people easy to conquer because they were fighting over religion, and made a law that everyone could believe as he pleased and argue for it peacefully. The Utopians worship in different ways but agree on one Supreme Being, whom they all call Mithras; their temples hold no images of God, so that each may picture Him in his own way; and they are drawn to Christianity partly because it seemed to favour holding goods in common. The toleration has a floor: nobody who denies Providence or the immortality of the soul may hold office.

Glimpsed in the Fool’s quarrel with the friar in Book I and in the pleasure philosophy, set out fully in ‘Of the Religions of the Utopians’, and tried against a hard case: the zealous convert who is banished for stirring up the people, though his faith itself is tolerated.

for the Cardinal has provided for you by what he proposed for restraining vagabonds and setting them to work, for I know no vagabonds like you.

The Fool, Book I

We know how religiously they are observed in Europe, more particularly where the Christian doctrine is received, among whom they are sacred and inviolable!

Hythloday, Book II, Of their Slaves, and of their Marriages

Order and liberty

Utopia is free of want, and not of supervision. Citizens need a passport to travel and permission to walk in their own district; ‘all men live in full view’; it is death to discuss public affairs outside council; slaves in gold chains do the killing and the dirty work. The book hides none of this. Whether the Utopians have traded one freedom for another, or simply given it up, is a question Hythloday never asks and a reader cannot avoid.

The Polylerits’ marked and cropped penal workers in Book I; the magistrates and passports of Book II; the slaves, the counselled deaths and the marriage laws in ‘Of their Slaves, and of their Marriages’.

I think my friends ought to rest contented with this, and not to expect that for their sakes I should enslave myself to any king whatsoever.

Hythloday, Book I

Utopus, that conquered it (whose name it still carries, for Abraxa was its first name), brought the rude and uncivilised inhabitants into such a good government, and to that measure of politeness, that they now far excel all the rest of mankind.

Hythloday, Book II, opening

The New World, colonies and war

The book was written within a decade of the printing of Vespucci’s voyages, and its traveller claims to have sailed on them. Utopia borrows the explorer’s report and some of its assumptions: the Utopians plant colonies on land its owners leave unfarmed, and count resistance a just cause of war. Their wars are fought with money, bounties and mercenaries, and the ideal state turns out to know every trick of the powers it despises.

Hythloday’s voyages and the compass he teaches the sailors to use in Book I; the colonies in ‘Of their Traffic’; the bribery and the Zapolets in ‘Of their Military Discipline’.

his face was tanned, he had a long beard, and his cloak was hanging carelessly about him, so that, by his looks and habit, I concluded he was a seaman.

More, Book I

Utopus, that conquered it (whose name it still carries, for Abraxa was its first name), brought the rude and uncivilised inhabitants into such a good government, and to that measure of politeness, that they now far excel all the rest of mankind.
