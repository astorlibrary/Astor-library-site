Utopia · Thomas More

Form and language

Frame narrative

A story told inside another story, so that every account reaches the reader through somebody with an interest in it.

Nothing in Utopia reaches the reader directly. More reports a conversation in Antwerp, in which Hythloday reports a dinner in England and then an island he alone has seen, while the narrator holds back his own judgement. The frame lets the author say dangerous things about England in another man’s mouth, and leaves the reader to decide how far to trust a witness whose name means nonsense.

his face was tanned, he had a long beard, and his cloak was hanging carelessly about him, so that, by his looks and habit, I concluded he was a seaman.

More, Book I

Irony

Saying one thing in a form that invites the opposite judgement.

More’s irony is usually a straight face held a beat too long. Europe’s princes keep their treaties so religiously, Hythloday says, especially where Christianity is received; the Utopians have no lawyers, because lawyers disguise matters and wrest the laws, in a book by a lawyer. The narrator’s courteous silence at the end works the same way, by saying much less than he thinks.

were then hanged so fast that there were sometimes twenty on one gibbet!

The lawyer, Book I

Symbolic names

Invented names that carry a judgement on the thing named.

The island is No-place. Its capital, Amaurot, is the dim or shadowy city, on the Anider, the waterless river; its prince was called the Ademus, the ruler without a people; the traveller’s surname suggests an expert in nonsense. Hythloday’s model nations are the Achorians, people without a country, the Macarians, the blessed, and the Polylerits, the people of much nonsense. A reader with Greek catches the joke on every page while the institutions are described in earnest.

Utopus, that conquered it (whose name it still carries, for Abraxa was its first name), brought the rude and uncivilised inhabitants into such a good government, and to that measure of politeness, that they now far excel all the rest of mankind.

Hythloday, Book II, opening

Objection and reply

Raising the opponent’s strongest point in order to answer it.

The book is organised around two objections, both given to the narrator: that a wise man should serve princes, and that people will not work where all things are common. Hythloday answers the first with the Morton story and the imagined councils, and the second with the whole of Book II. The final objection, reserved and never spoken, is left for the reader to supply.

I think my friends ought to rest contented with this, and not to expect that for their sakes I should enslave myself to any king whatsoever.

Hythloday, Book I

Defamiliarisation

Presenting ordinary customs through outsiders’ eyes so that they look absurd.

Utopian children laugh at an ambassador for wearing pearls like a toddler; Utopian philosophers cannot see the pleasure in watching dogs tear a hare or in another man’s taking off his hat. By describing European habits as the Utopians see them, More makes gold, rank and fashion look like what they materially are: metal, ancestry, and wool that was once on a sheep.

They eat and drink out of vessels of earth or glass, which make an agreeable appearance, though formed of brittle materials; while they make their chamber-pots and close-stools of gold and silver

Hythloday, Book II, Of the Travelling of the Utopians

Analogy

Arguing from a comparison with something better understood.

Hythloday thinks in comparisons: sheep that devour men, schoolmasters who would sooner beat than teach, a physician who cures one disease by causing another, a king who is a gaoler, a doctrine bent like a leaden rule. More answers in kind, with a play already in performance. The analogies do the persuading, because each carries a judgement that is obvious in ordinary life into politics, where it is not obvious at all.

for the springs both of good and evil flow from the prince over a whole nation, as from a lasting fountain.

More, Book I

Argument by example

Proving a political claim by a case, real or supposed, that shows it working.

Machiavelli argued from Cesare Borgia and the Romans; Hythloday argues from peoples nobody can check, the Polylerits, the Achorians and the Macarians, whose names announce that they do not exist. It is the humanist method of learning from history applied to invented history, and the joke is that the invented cases are more persuasive than most real ones.

That it was not easy to form a judgment of its success, since it was a method that never yet had been tried

Cardinal Morton, Book I
