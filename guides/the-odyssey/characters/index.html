The Odyssey · Homer

Characters

Odysseus (Butler's Ulysses)

King of Ithaca, ten years at Troy and ten more getting back; first appears Book 5

The Iliad’s heroes are strong; this one is clever, and the poem treats cleverness as a kind of endurance. He can build a raft, a bed and a lie with equal care, and he tells five separate false life-stories once he is home. He is also the only survivor of his expedition, a fact he narrates himself and for which he never fully takes the blame.

Penelope

Wife of Odysseus, besieged in her own house; first appears Book 1

She has no army, no husband and a son too young to help, and she holds a hundred-odd men off for three years with a loom. The poem never says exactly when she recognises the beggar, and her decision to set the bow contest on the very day he arrives can be read as despair, luck or collusion. She out-tricks Odysseus, which no other mortal in the poem manages.

Telemachus

Son of Odysseus, a baby when his father sailed; first appears Book 1

The first four books are his, and they show a young man who has been told all his life whose son he is and has no way to check. His journey finds out very little about Odysseus and a good deal about himself. By Book 21 he is giving orders to his mother and could, the poem hints, have strung the bow himself.

Athene (Butler's Minerva)

Goddess of craft and war, daughter of Zeus; first appears Book 1

She opens the plot by lobbying her father while Poseidon is out of the room, and she stays in it to the last line. She appears as Mentes, as Mentor, as a little girl with a pitcher and as a shepherd boy, and she likes Odysseus for the quality a moralist would disapprove of: he lies as well as she does.

Poseidon (Butler's Neptune)

God of the sea, father of the Cyclops; first appears Book 5

His anger is the reason a voyage of a few weeks takes ten years, and it has a specific cause: Odysseus blinded his son and then supplied a name for the curse. He cannot kill Odysseus, because fate has settled that the man gets home, so he settles for making it slow, solitary and expensive, and takes out the remainder on the Phaeacians.

Polyphemus

A Cyclops, shepherd and cannibal; first appears Book 9

He is the poem’s clearest picture of a host who breaks every rule: he asks his guests’ business before feeding them, eats them, and offers as his guest-gift the favour of being eaten last. Yet he is tender with his ram, and Odysseus was in his cave uninvited, helping himself to cheese. The episode is less one-sided than its teller makes it sound.

Calypso

A nymph, daughter of Atlas, on the island of Ogygia; first appears Book 5

Her name comes from the Greek for hiding, and she has hidden Odysseus from the world for seven years. She offers the one thing no Homeric hero is otherwise offered (never to die), and he declines it for a woman he concedes is less beautiful. Told to release him, she protests that gods are allowed mortal lovers and goddesses are not.

Circe

A goddess and enchantress on the island of Aeaea; first appears Book 10

She turns half the crew into pigs, fails to turn Odysseus, and then becomes the most useful ally of the whole voyage. It is Circe who sends him to the dead and who briefs him, point by point, on the Sirens, Scylla and the cattle of the Sun. He stays a year, and it is the crew who have to remind him about home.

Nausicaa

Daughter of Alcinous, king of the Phaeacians; first appears Book 6

She is the only person on the beach who does not run away from the salt-caked stranger, and she manages the encounter better than he could have hoped, down to the instruction to walk behind her wagon so that nobody gossips. The poem lets her think of him as a husband and then sends him home to someone else.

Eumaeus

Swineherd to Odysseus, born a king’s son and sold as a child; first appears Book 14

He is the poem’s model host, and he is a slave with a hut and a herd of pigs. He gives a beggar the best cut of pork and his own cloak, and refuses to believe the beggar’s good news because he has been lied to before. The narrator addresses him directly as “you”, a courtesy the poem extends to nobody else in Ithaca.

Eurycleia

The old nurse of Odysseus and of Telemachus; first appears Book 1

She knows the house better than anyone in it, and she knows Odysseus by touch: the scar on his leg tells her what twenty years and a goddess’s disguise were hiding from everybody else. Her reward is a hand at her throat. After the killing it is she who names the twelve disloyal maids.

Antinous and the suitors

The ringleader of the hundred and eight men courting Penelope; first appears Book 1

The suitors are guests who will not leave, and their offence is described again and again as eating. Antinous is the worst of them: he proposes Telemachus’s murder and throws a stool at a beggar in another man’s hall. Yet his account of Penelope’s web in Book 2 is half admiring, and the suitors’ kin have a grievance the last book takes seriously.
