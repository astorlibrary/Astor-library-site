The Odyssey · Homer

Context

Dates

1184 BC
The traditional date for the fall of Troy Worked out by the Greek scholar Eratosthenes centuries later. It is a calculation from legend, but it falls close to a destruction that archaeologists date to around 1180 BCE at the site of Hisarlik.
700 BC
The Odyssey takes something like its present form Probably in the late eighth or early seventh century BCE, a little after the Iliad, in the Greek-speaking eastern Aegean. It draws on generations of oral song about the returns of the heroes from Troy.
150 BC
Alexandrian scholars edit the text Over the third and second centuries BCE the librarians at Alexandria, Aristarchus the most influential among them, compared manuscripts and marked suspect lines. The division into twenty-four books is usually credited to this period.
19 BC
Virgil dies, leaving the Aeneid Rome’s national epic rewrites Homer: its first six books answer the Odyssey with a hero who sails west from Troy and visits the dead.
1488
Homer printed for the first time The first printed edition of the Greek text of both epics appeared in Florence, edited by Demetrius Chalcondyles.
1616
George Chapman’s complete Homer Chapman’s English Odyssey, in rhymed pentameter, had appeared about two years earlier; the collected Whole Works of Homer made both poems available to English readers without Greek.
1726
Pope’s Odyssey completed Alexander Pope’s version in heroic couplets, produced with two collaborators, followed his Iliad and was the standard English Homer for a century.
1835
Samuel Butler, the translator, born The translator was born at Langar in Nottinghamshire, a clergyman’s son. He would be a sheep farmer in New Zealand, a painter, a satirist and a critic of Darwin before he turned to Homer.
1842
Tennyson publishes ‘Ulysses’ A dramatic monologue for the hero in old age, restless in Ithaca and ready to sail again. It takes its cue from Tiresias’s prophecy and from Dante’s Ulysses; Homer’s ending leaves him at home.
1897
Butler publishes The Authoress of the Odyssey Butler argued that the poem was written by a young woman in Sicily, at Trapani, who put herself into it as Nausicaa. Scholars have not accepted the theory, but it made him read the poem’s domestic detail more closely than most.
1900
Butler’s prose Odyssey published He wanted, he said, a version for readers who knew no Greek, in the plain English of his own day. His Iliad had appeared two years before. Butler died in 1902.
1922
James Joyce’s Ulysses Joyce maps the Odyssey’s episodes on to one day in Dublin, with an advertising canvasser as Odysseus. The Roman form of the name, as in Butler, was still the ordinary English one.
1928
Milman Parry’s study of the Homeric epithet Parry showed that the repeated phrases form a system fitted to the metre, of the kind a poet composing aloud would need. Fieldwork among singers in Yugoslavia in the 1930s supported the case that Homeric verse grew out of oral performance.
1939
A Mycenaean palace found near Pylos Carl Blegen’s excavation at Ano Englianos uncovered a Bronze Age palace and an archive of clay tablets. When their script, Linear B, was deciphered in 1952, it proved to be an early form of Greek.

Places

Troy (Hisarlik)
The mound at Hisarlik in north-west Turkey, generally accepted as the site of Troy. Everything in the poem is measured from here: Odysseus sails from it in the first line and has still not got home ten years later.
Ithaca
The modern island of Ithaki in the Ionian Sea, which has carried the name since antiquity. Homer’s description does not fit it in every detail, and other islands have been proposed, but this is the traditional identification.
Pylos (Palace of Nestor)
The Bronze Age palace at Ano Englianos in Messenia, excavated from 1939 and known as the Palace of Nestor. Telemachus lands at ‘sandy Pylos’ in Book 3 and finds the old king sacrificing on the beach.
Sparta
Lacedaemon, where Telemachus visits Menelaus and Helen in Book 4 and sees a marriage that survived the war, after a fashion.
Cape Malea
The south-eastern tip of the Peloponnese, a notorious place for ships. Here, in Book 9, the north wind drives Odysseus off his course for home and off the map of the known world.
Corfu
Traditionally identified with Scheria, the island of the Phaeacians, since at least the fifth century BCE. Homer’s Scheria is a place at the edge of the real, and the identification rests on ancient local pride; Homer’s geography does not settle it.
Strait of Messina
The narrow water between Sicily and mainland Italy, traditionally identified with the passage between Scylla and Charybdis in Book 12. Ancient writers already placed the wanderings around Sicily and southern Italy; the poem itself gives no bearings.
Mount Parnassus
Where the young Odysseus, visiting his grandfather Autolycus, was gored by a boar. The scar is how Eurycleia knows him in Book 19.

Critics

Erich Auerbach

“Odysseus’ Scar”, in Mimesis (1946)

Auerbach set the scene in which Eurycleia recognises Odysseus by his scar beside the story of Abraham and Isaac. Homer, he argued, brings everything into the foreground in full daylight, while Genesis leaves most things unsaid and is fraught with background.

Max Horkheimer and Theodor W. Adorno

Dialectic of Enlightenment (1947)

Horkheimer and Adorno read Odysseus as the prototype of the modern individual, who survives by cunning and self-denial. Bound to the mast while his oarsmen row with wax in their ears, he hears the Sirens’ song as an art he may enjoy only because he cannot act on it.
