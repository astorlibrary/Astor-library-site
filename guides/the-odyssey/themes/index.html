Homecoming

The Greek word is nostos, and the poem measures its hero against other men’s returns: Agamemnon got home quickly and was murdered at dinner; Menelaus got home rich and sits beside a wife he cannot quite trust. Arriving is the easy part. Home has to be re-entered carefully, in disguise, and won back room by room.

Through the poem A longing on Calypso’s beach in Book 5, a coastline glimpsed and lost in Book 10, an unrecognised shore in Book 13, and finally an olive-tree bedpost in Book 23, after which Tiresias’s prophecy says he must leave again.

In the text

I am quite aware that my wife Penelope is nothing like so tall or so beautiful as yourself. She is only a woman, whereas you are an immortal. Nevertheless, I want to get home, and can think of nothing else.

Odysseus, Book 5

This is diplomacy, since he is speaking to a goddess who could still drown him, but it is also the poem’s central choice, made in three sentences. He concedes every point of comparison and then says ‘Nevertheless’. He is choosing the mortal life more than the better woman: ageing, a particular island, a death. After the Iliad’s heroes, who trade life for fame, here is one who turns down eternity for home.

There was a young olive growing within the precincts of the house, in full vigour, and about as thick as a bearing-post. I built my room round this with strong walls of stone and a roof to cover them

Odysseus, Book 23

It is a trap, and the master of traps walks into it: only the man who built the bed knows that one post is a rooted tree. His anger is the proof, because it is unplanned. The secret is apt as well as secure. He did not bring a bed into a house; he built the house around a living thing and made the marriage out of that. His fear that someone has ‘cut down the olive tree at its roots’ is the fear of the whole twenty years.

Hosts, guests and strangers

Nearly every episode is a test of how a household treats someone who turns up at the door. The rule (feed the stranger first, ask his name afterwards, send him on with a gift) is under the protection of Zeus. Nestor, Menelaus, the Phaeacians and a swineherd keep it; the Cyclops inverts it; the suitors break it from the guest’s side by never leaving.

Through the poem Shown working in Books 3, 4 and 7, parodied in the Cyclops’s cave, kept by the poorest household in Book 14, and enforced in Book 22 with a bow.

In the text

there is nothing better in this world than that man and wife should be of one mind in a house. It discomfits their enemies, makes the hearts of their friends glad, and they themselves know more about it than any one.

Odysseus, Book 6

As rhetoric it is perfectly judged: a frightening stranger reassures a girl of marriageable age by praising marriage. But the Greek word behind ‘of one mind’, homophrosyne, describes what the poem will show in Book 23, where husband and wife turn out to think alike because each is testing the other. The last clause is the shrewdest: a good marriage is partly a secret, which is what the bed will be.

Stranger, though a still poorer man should come here, it would not be right for me to insult him, for all strangers and beggars are from Jove.

Eumaeus, Book 14

The law of hospitality gets its plainest statement from a slave in a pig farm, with no audience and nothing to gain. The suitors, noblemen in a palace, will throw furniture at the same beggar. The narrator marks the moment by turning to address the swineherd directly (‘you answered, O swineherd Eumaeus’), a warmth the poem saves for him. Rank in the Odyssey is tested by what you do with a stranger.

Names, fame and recognition

Odysseus survives by being nobody and cannot bear it. A hero exists in what is said about him, so the man who calls himself Noman has to shout his name and address from the ship. In Ithaca the process runs the other way: he withholds his name and is recognised piece by piece, by a dog, a scar, a bow and a bed.

Through the poem Withheld for a whole book at the Phaeacian court, declared with pride in Book 9, hidden again from Book 13, and then surrendered in stages to son, nurse, servants, wife and father.

In the text

Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled far and wide after he had sacked the famous town of Troy. Many cities did he visit, and many were the nations with whose manners and customs he was acquainted

Book 1

The Iliad’s first noun is anger; this poem’s is a man, and an unnamed one. ‘Ingenious’ is Butler’s word for the Greek polytropos, ‘of many turns’, which can mean much-travelled or much-twisting, and the sentence allows both. Note too what he is credited with: visiting cities and learning customs, with no mention of killing. The hero is introduced as a kind of tourist with a sack of Troy behind him.

I am Ulysses son of Laertes, renowned among mankind for all manner of subtlety, so that my fame ascends to heaven.

Odysseus, Book 9

He has just heard a bard sing about him, so he knows the claim about fame is true, and he makes it without embarrassment: in this world reputation is a fact like height. What he is famous for is ‘subtlety’, which means tricks. He is about to narrate four books in which that subtlety loses him twelve ships, and the boast in the first sentence frames how he wants the losses understood.

Cunning, lies and disguise

The poem’s word for its hero is polytropos, “of many turns”, and it admires the quality without apology. Athene loves him because he lies well; Penelope matches him with the web and the bed. But deceit corrodes as well as protects: by Book 24 he cannot greet his own grieving father without first trying a false story on him.

Through the poem A tactic against the Cyclops, a habit by Book 13, a household policy in Books 17 to 21, and something close to a compulsion in the orchard scene of Book 24.

In the text

we could see her working on her great web all day long, but at night she would unpick the stitches again by torchlight. She fooled us in this way for three years and we never found her out

Antinous, Book 2

The story is told by her enemy, as an accusation, and it comes out as a tribute: three years, a hundred men, and they ‘never found her out’ until a maid told them. The web is a shroud, so she defers one man’s funeral to defer her own remarriage. It is also the female version of her husband’s craft. He makes things that hold, like a raft or a bed; she makes something that must never be finished.

that is why I cannot desert you in your afflictions; you are so plausible, shrewd and shifty.

Athene (Butler’s Minerva), Book 13

A goddess explains her loyalty, and the reasons are three words a moralist would use as charges. Butler’s ‘shifty’ is sharper than most translators allow themselves and catches the tone: affectionate, amused, professional. She has just listened to him lie to her face and enjoyed it. The line tells us what kind of virtue this poem rewards: wariness, more than honesty, and an unwillingness to take even a homecoming on trust.

Loyalty

Twenty years is long enough for everyone to have made a choice, and the second half of the poem goes through the household finding out what each one chose. Penelope, Eumaeus, Eurycleia, the cowherd Philoetius and the dog pass. Melanthius the goatherd and twelve of the fifty maids do not, and the poem’s punishment of them is savage: the maids are hanged and Melanthius is mutilated.

Through the poem Tested gently in the swineherd’s hut, brutally in the hall, intimately at the footbath, and paid out in Book 22.

In the text

As soon as he saw Ulysses standing there, he dropped his ears and wagged his tail, but he could not get close up to his master.

Book 17

Athene’s disguise works on every human being in Ithaca and fails on a dog, instantly. Argos is also a picture of the household: once swift, now lying in filth because ‘the women take no care of him’. Odysseus has to wipe away a tear without Eumaeus seeing, and walk on; the dog dies as he goes in. The only recognition that costs him nothing in risk is the one he is not allowed to answer.

as thrushes or doves beat against a net that has been set for them in a thicket just as they were getting to their nest, and a terrible fate awaits them, even so did the women have to put their heads in nooses one after the other and die most miserably.

Book 22

The narrative says they deserve it; the simile does not sound convinced. Small birds heading home to their nest are caught in a net, and home is the poem’s own highest value. The next sentence, about their feet moving ‘but not for very long’, is as cold as anything in Homer. The women were slaves, and what choice they had is not discussed. The poem’s justice and the poem’s sympathy part company here, and both are left on the page.

Justice, revenge and the gods

Zeus opens the poem by saying that mortals bring suffering on themselves beyond what is fated, and offers Aegisthus as the example. The crew who eat the Sun’s cattle and the suitors who eat Odysseus’s are both warned and both destroyed. Whether what happens in the hall is justice or a massacre is a question the final book raises and then closes by decree.

Through the poem Stated as a principle in Book 1, illustrated on Thrinacia in Book 12, carried out in Book 22, and challenged by the dead men’s families in Book 24.

In the text

See now, how men lay blame upon us gods for what is after all nothing but their own folly.

Zeus (Butler’s Jove), Book 1

The first speech in the poem is a complaint about being misjudged, and it sets the moral rules for everything after. People are warned; people ignore the warning; people pay. The crew and the suitors will both fit the pattern exactly. What the speech leaves out is Poseidon, whose persecution of Odysseus is about to be discussed and looks more like a god’s temper than anybody’s folly.

rejoice in silence; restrain yourself, and do not make any noise about it; it is an unholy thing to vaunt over dead men.

Odysseus, Book 22

Iliadic heroes vaunt over the fallen as a matter of course, and Odysseus vaunted over the Cyclops to his cost. Here he has learnt, or the poem has: the suitors died by ‘heaven’s doom and their own evil deeds’, and the killer presents himself as an instrument. It is a scruple of piety, though, and has nothing to do with mercy. His very next sentence asks the nurse which of the women have been disloyal.

Storytelling

This is a poem full of poets. Phemius sings in Ithaca, Demodocus at the Phaeacian court, and Odysseus himself is compared to a bard by his host. The famous adventures reach us only in his words, to an audience he needs to impress, and the same man tells elaborate false autobiographies in Ithaca with exactly the same fluency.

Through the poem A bard’s song makes Penelope weep in Book 1 and Odysseus weep in Book 8; he takes over as narrator for Books 9 to 12, and strings the bow in Book 21 like a singer tuning a lyre.

In the text

He wept as a woman weeps when she throws herself on the body of her husband who has fallen before his own city and people, fighting bravely in defence of his home and children.

Book 8

The song is about his greatest success, and the simile puts him in the place of one of its victims: a Trojan widow, who in the following lines is beaten on the back with spears and led off to slavery. The poem does not say Odysseus feels guilt. It simply makes the sacker of cities cry in the shape of the sacked, and leaves the comparison there for the listener.

strung it as easily as a skilled bard strings a new peg of his lyre and makes the twisted gut fast at both ends.

Book 21

At the moment he becomes a killer, he is compared to a poet. The string then ‘sang sweetly under his touch’, and his next words invite the suitors to ‘song and dance’. The simile closes a circle drawn since Book 8: the man who wept at a bard’s song and took over as narrator now plays an instrument of his own, and the performance that follows is the one the whole poem has been tuning for.
