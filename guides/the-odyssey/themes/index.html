The Odyssey · Homer

Themes

Homecoming

The Greek word is nostos, and the poem measures its hero against other men’s returns: Agamemnon got home quickly and was murdered at dinner; Menelaus got home rich and sits beside a wife he cannot quite trust. Arriving is the easy part. Home has to be re-entered carefully, in disguise, and won back room by room.

A longing on Calypso’s beach in Book 5, a coastline glimpsed and lost in Book 10, an unrecognised shore in Book 13, and finally an olive-tree bedpost in Book 23, after which Tiresias’s prophecy says he must leave again.

I am quite aware that my wife Penelope is nothing like so tall or so beautiful as yourself. She is only a woman, whereas you are an immortal. Nevertheless, I want to get home, and can think of nothing else.

Odysseus, Book 5

there is nothing better in this world than that man and wife should be of one mind in a house. It discomfits their enemies, makes the hearts of their friends glad, and they themselves know more about it than any one.

Odysseus, Book 6

Hosts, guests and strangers

Nearly every episode is a test of how a household treats someone who turns up at the door. The rule (feed the stranger first, ask his name afterwards, send him on with a gift) is under the protection of Zeus. Nestor, Menelaus, the Phaeacians and a swineherd keep it; the Cyclops inverts it; the suitors break it from the guest’s side by never leaving.

Shown working in Books 3, 4 and 7, parodied in the Cyclops’s cave, kept by the poorest household in Book 14, and enforced in Book 22 with a bow.

there is nothing better in this world than that man and wife should be of one mind in a house. It discomfits their enemies, makes the hearts of their friends glad, and they themselves know more about it than any one.

Odysseus, Book 6

Cyclops, you ask my name and I will tell it you; give me, therefore, the present you promised me; my name is Noman; this is what my father and mother and my friends have always called me.

Odysseus, Book 9

Names, fame and recognition

Odysseus survives by being nobody and cannot bear it. A hero exists in what is said about him, so the man who calls himself Noman has to shout his name and address from the ship. In Ithaca the process runs the other way: he withholds his name and is recognised piece by piece, by a dog, a scar, a bow and a bed.

Withheld for a whole book at the Phaeacian court, declared with pride in Book 9, hidden again from Book 13, and then surrendered in stages to son, nurse, servants, wife and father.

Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled far and wide after he had sacked the famous town of Troy. Many cities did he visit, and many were the nations with whose manners and customs he was acquainted

Book 1

Go, then, within the house and busy yourself with your daily duties, your loom, your distaff, and the ordering of your servants; for speech is man’s matter, and mine above all others

Telemachus, Book 1

Cunning, lies and disguise

The poem’s word for its hero is polytropos, “of many turns”, and it admires the quality without apology. Athene loves him because he lies well; Penelope matches him with the web and the bed. But deceit corrodes as well as protects: by Book 24 he cannot greet his own grieving father without first trying a false story on him.

A tactic against the Cyclops, a habit by Book 13, a household policy in Books 17 to 21, and something close to a compulsion in the orchard scene of Book 24.

Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled far and wide after he had sacked the famous town of Troy. Many cities did he visit, and many were the nations with whose manners and customs he was acquainted

Book 1

we could see her working on her great web all day long, but at night she would unpick the stitches again by torchlight. She fooled us in this way for three years and we never found her out

Antinous, Book 2

Loyalty

Twenty years is long enough for everyone to have made a choice, and the second half of the poem goes through the household finding out what each one chose. Penelope, Eumaeus, Eurycleia, the cowherd Philoetius and the dog pass. Melanthius the goatherd and twelve of the fifty maids do not, and the poem’s punishment of them is harsher than modern readers find comfortable.

Tested gently in the swineherd’s hut, brutally in the hall, intimately at the footbath, and paid out in Book 22.

we could see her working on her great web all day long, but at night she would unpick the stitches again by torchlight. She fooled us in this way for three years and we never found her out

Justice, revenge and the gods

Zeus opens the poem by saying that mortals bring suffering on themselves beyond what is fated, and offers Aegisthus as the example. The crew who eat the Sun’s cattle and the suitors who eat Odysseus’s are both warned and both destroyed. Whether what happens in the hall is justice or a massacre is a question the final book raises and then closes by decree.

Stated as a principle in Book 1, illustrated on Thrinacia in Book 12, carried out in Book 22, and challenged by the dead men’s families in Book 24.

See now, how men lay blame upon us gods for what is after all nothing but their own folly.

Zeus (Butler's Jove), Book 1

Cyclops, if any one asks you who it was that put your eye out and spoiled your beauty, say it was the valiant warrior Ulysses, son of Laertes, who lives in Ithaca.

Odysseus, Book 9

Storytelling

This is a poem full of poets. Phemius sings in Ithaca, Demodocus at the Phaeacian court, and Odysseus himself is compared to a bard by his host. The famous adventures reach us only in his words, to an audience he needs to impress, and the same man tells elaborate false autobiographies in Ithaca with exactly the same fluency.

A bard’s song makes Penelope weep in Book 1 and Odysseus weep in Book 8; he takes over as narrator for Books 9 to 12, and strings the bow in Book 21 like a singer tuning a lyre.
