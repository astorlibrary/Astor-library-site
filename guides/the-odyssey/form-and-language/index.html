The Odyssey · Homer

Form and language

Beginning in the middle

Opening an epic late in its story and supplying the earlier events afterwards, usually through a character’s own narration.

The poem starts in the twentieth year, and with the wrong person: four books about Telemachus before Odysseus appears. The ten years of wandering are then folded into one evening’s talk, so that the poem’s present is only about forty days long.

Invocation

An opening address to a muse or god, asking for the song and announcing its subject.

The proem asks the Muse for ‘that ingenious hero’ and does not name him until the second paragraph. It also gets its excuses in early, blaming the crew’s deaths on ‘their own sheer folly’ before we have heard a word of the evidence.

Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who travelled far and wide after he had sacked the famous town of Troy. Many cities did he visit, and many were the nations with whose manners and customs he was acquainted

Book 1

Epithets and formulae

Fixed descriptive phrases attached to a person or thing and repeated whenever they recur, a working tool of poetry composed in performance.

Dawn is “rosy-fingered” every time she appears, and whole blocks of lines (arming, washing, serving a meal) recur word for word. Butler keeps many of these in his prose. The repetitions are there for a reason: every dawn and every dinner follows the same ritual, so any breach of it shows immediately.

Go, then, within the house and busy yourself with your daily duties, your loom, your distaff, and the ordering of your servants; for speech is man’s matter, and mine above all others

Telemachus, Book 1

Extended simile

A comparison developed over several lines until it becomes a small scene of its own, typically closed with ‘even so’.

The Odyssey’s similes have a habit of crossing sides. The victor of Troy weeps like a woman being dragged into slavery from a sacked town; Penelope greets her husband like a shipwrecked sailor reaching land, which is his experience more than hers. The comparisons make characters change places with those their stories have harmed or left behind.

He wept as a woman weeps when she throws herself on the body of her husband who has fallen before his own city and people, fighting bravely in defence of his home and children.

Book 8

Embedded narration

A story told after the event by someone who was in it, with the advantages and the unreliability that implies.

Books 9 to 12 are in the first person, and the poem has taken care to show us beforehand that the speaker is a gifted liar who wants a ship and presents from his audience. Nothing confirms or denies his account except Poseidon’s anger, which is real enough.

I am Ulysses son of Laertes, renowned among mankind for all manner of subtlety, so that my fame ascends to heaven.

Odysseus, Book 9

Dramatic irony

The audience knows something that a character speaking or acting does not.

From Book 13 onwards almost every conversation in Ithaca is conducted with a man whose identity we know and the speakers do not. Eumaeus mourns his master to his master’s face; a suitor hopes the beggar will be no more successful ‘in other things’ than at stringing the bow that is about to kill them.

See now, how men lay blame upon us gods for what is after all nothing but their own folly.

Zeus (Butler's Jove), Book 1

Recognition scene

A scene in which one character discovers who another really is, often by means of a token or sign.

The second half is built as a ladder of these, each with a different token and a different cost: Telemachus by a goddess’s help, Argos by scent, Eurycleia by a scar, the herdsmen by being shown it, the suitors by an arrow, Penelope by the bed, Laertes by the orchard trees counted out to a child.

There is no other Ulysses who will come hereafter. Such as I am, it is I, who after long wandering and much hardship have got home in the twentieth year to my own country.

Odysseus, Book 16

Symbolic objects

Physical things that the narrative returns to until they stand for a relationship or an idea.

The web that is never finished is Penelope’s marriage kept open. The bow that only one man can string is the kingship. The bed built round a living olive tree is a marriage that cannot be moved without being killed, and the poem makes the point by having someone threaten to move it.

we could see her working on her great web all day long, but at night she would unpick the stitches again by torchlight. She fooled us in this way for three years and we never found her out

Antinous, Book 2
