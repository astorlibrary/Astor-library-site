Macbeth · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Equivocation

Saying something literally true in a form designed to be understood wrongly.

The witches’ apparitions and the Porter’s joke about the equivocator who could not equivocate to heaven belong to the same 1606 argument about oaths and mental reservation.

Macbeth shall never vanquish'd be until
Great Birnam wood to high Dunsinane hill
Shall come against him.

Third Apparition, 4.1.97

Blood imagery

A repeated image used to bind separate scenes into one idea.

Blood arrives as heroism in 1.2, becomes a stain in 2.2, a debt in 3.4 and a hallucination in 5.1 without ever leaving the stage.

Will all great Neptune’s ocean wash this blood
Clean from my hand? No, this my hand will rather
The multitudinous seas incarnadine,
Making the green one red.

Macbeth, 2.2.60

Soliloquy

A character alone on stage, thinking aloud to the audience.

Macbeth’s soliloquies are where the murders actually happen; by Act V they have thinned to a report on how little he feels.

Come, you spirits
That tend on mortal thoughts, unsex me here,
And fill me from the crown to the toe top-full
Of direst cruelty!

Lady Macbeth, 1.5.39

Antithesis

Balancing opposed terms against each other inside one line.

‘Fair is foul, and foul is fair’ sets the pattern, and the play goes on pairing terms that ought to exclude each other: lost and won, innocent flower and serpent.

Fair is foul, and foul is fair:
Hover through the fog and filthy air.

The Witches, 1.1.12

Dramatic irony

The audience knows something a character on stage does not.

Duncan praising the sweetness of the air at Inverness is the clearest case: the audience has just heard what is waiting inside.

Disordered nature

Weather and animal behaviour made to answer human action.

Horses eat each other, an owl kills a falcon and darkness strangles the day after Duncan’s murder: the killing of a king is reported as a fault in the world.
