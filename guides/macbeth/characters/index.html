Macbeth · William Shakespeare

Characters

Macbeth

Thane of Glamis, then Cawdor, then king of Scotland; first appears 1.3

A soldier praised for a savagery the state approves of, who discovers that the same nerve is unbearable when he uses it for himself. He imagines every murder in full before he commits it and feels every consequence afterwards.

Lady Macbeth

Macbeth’s wife, later queen; first appears 1.5

She reads her husband accurately in a single speech and organises the murder he cannot organise himself. Her strength is a performance she has to summon, and once the crown is won she has no further part to play, which is where the sleepwalking begins.

Banquo

A general, father of Fleance; first appears 1.3

He hears the same prophecy and does nothing with it, which sets him against Macbeth from the start. His silence after Duncan’s death is not innocence either: he suspects Macbeth of foul play and keeps quiet because the prophecy also promised something to him.

Duncan

King of Scotland; first appears 1.2

Generous, trusting and a poor judge of faces: he says as much about the last thane of Cawdor moments before promoting the next traitor. Shakespeare makes him gentler than the historical king so that the murder costs the audience something.

Macduff

Thane of Fife; first appears 2.3

He discovers the body, stays away from the coronation, and leaves for England without warning his household, a decision the play does not let him off. His grief in 4.3 is the one moment where a man is allowed to feel a loss fully before acting on it.

Malcolm

Duncan’s elder son, Prince of Cumberland; first appears 1.2

Named heir in 1.4, which is the political fact that makes the prophecy inconvenient. He flees, learns caution, and sounds out Macduff by describing himself as a worse tyrant than Macbeth, a scene that shows a king being made.

The Weird Sisters

Three witches; first appears 1.1

They tell the truth in a form designed to be misheard. Nothing they say forces anybody to do anything, which is the difficulty: the play never settles whether they cause the future or only describe it accurately enough to be dangerous.

Lady Macduff

Macduff’s wife; first appears 4.2

She gets one scene, and uses it to say the thing nobody else will: that a man who leaves his wife and children unguarded has behaved like a traitor to them, whatever his politics. Her son is killed in front of her, and she is chased off the stage crying ‘Murder!’.

Fleance

Banquo’s son; first appears 2.1

He speaks only a few lines and then escapes into the dark, and the second half of the play runs on that escape. James VI and I was held to be descended from Banquo through Fleance.
