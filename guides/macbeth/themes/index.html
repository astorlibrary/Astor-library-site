Macbeth · William Shakespeare

Themes

Ambition

The play is careful to show that ambition is not the same as wanting power. Macbeth has power already and is praised for it; what the witches give him is a way of picturing more, and the picture will not leave him alone. Shakespeare gives the desire a body (a spur, a leap, a fall) and no destination.

Suggested in 1.3, argued over in 1.7, and by 3.4 it has become momentum: he is so far in blood that going back would be as tedious as going on.

It is too full o' the milk of human kindness
To catch the nearest way.

Lady Macbeth, 1.5.14

Come, you spirits
That tend on mortal thoughts, unsex me here,
And fill me from the crown to the toe top-full
Of direst cruelty!

Lady Macbeth, 1.5.39

Guilt and conscience

Guilt in this play is physical. It stops a man saying ‘Amen’, it will not wash off, it takes a seat at dinner, and it eventually walks the corridors at night with a candle. Nobody is punished for the murders by the state; they are punished by their own nervous systems.

Macbeth’s imagination runs ahead of his conscience in Acts I and II, and his wife’s runs behind hers: she is unshaken at the murder and destroyed by it in Act V.

If it were done when 'tis done, then 'twere well
It were done quickly.

Macbeth, 1.7.1

Is this a dagger which I see before me,
The handle toward my hand?

Macbeth, 2.1.32

Kingship and the state

Scotland is described as a body: bleeding, sick, needing a physician. Malcolm’s list of the king-becoming graces in 4.3 sets out what Macbeth is not, and the play’s interest is less in who deserves the crown than in what happens to a country when its head is afraid.

Duncan’s Scotland rewards loyalty in public; Macbeth’s rewards silence; Malcolm’s begins with a promise of order that the audience has been given reason to doubt.

My way of life
Is fall'n into the sear, the yellow leaf;
And that which should accompany old age,
As honour, love, obedience, troops of friends,
I must not look to have.

Macbeth, 5.3.22

Our fears do make us traitors.

Lady Macduff, 4.2.4

Fate, prophecy and equivocation

Every prophecy in the play comes true, and the ones Macbeth relies on mislead him. The apparitions promise safety in terms that are technically exact and practically worthless, which is the same trick the Jesuit doctrine of equivocation was accused of in the Gunpowder Plot trials of 1606.

First a prediction, then a riddle, then a trapdoor: Birnam wood does move, and the man not of woman born does exist.

All hail, Macbeth! that shalt be king hereafter!

Third Witch, 1.3.50

It will have blood; they say, blood will have blood.

Macbeth, 3.4.128

Appearance and reality

The play opens by declaring that fair is foul, and then spends five acts proving it operationally: a welcoming castle, a loyal host, an innocent flower, a safe prophecy. Reading the play is largely a matter of watching characters fail to read each other.

Duncan cannot find the mind’s construction in the face; by Act IV Malcolm will not trust a face at all, and tests Macduff with a false one of his own.

Fair is foul, and foul is fair:
Hover through the fog and filthy air.

The Witches, 1.1.12

Look like the innocent flower,
But be the serpent under't.

Lady Macbeth, 1.5.65

Manhood and gender

“When you durst do it, then you were a man” is an argument as much as an insult, and it works. The play returns again and again to what manhood is supposed to allow: Macduff’s answer in 4.3, that he must also feel the loss as a man, is offered as a correction to everything Macbeth has been told.

Lady Macbeth asks to be unsexed in 1.5; Macbeth tells his wife to bring forth men-children only in 1.7; Macduff rejects the equation in 4.3.

To catch the nearest way.
