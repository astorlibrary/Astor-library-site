Ambition

The play is careful to show that ambition is not the same as wanting power. Macbeth has power already and is praised for it; what the witches give him is a way of picturing more, and the picture will not leave him alone. Shakespeare gives the desire a body (a spur, a leap, a fall) and no destination.

Through the play Suggested in 1.3, argued over in 1.7, and by 3.4 it has become momentum: he is so far in blood that going back would be as tedious as going on.

In the text

Stars, hide your fires;
Let not light see my black and deep desires.

Macbeth, 1.4.52

The wish is already there before Lady Macbeth has said a word. Duncan names Malcolm his heir, and Macbeth asks for darkness to cover what he wants.

I have no spur
To prick the sides of my intent, but only
Vaulting ambition, which o’erleaps itself
And falls on the other.

Macbeth, 1.7.25

Here are the spur, the leap and the fall. Macbeth can name nothing that drives him except the wanting, and he pictures it as a rider who jumps too far and comes down on the other side.

My way of life
Is fall’n into the sear, the yellow leaf;
And that which should accompany old age,
As honour, love, obedience, troops of friends,
I must not look to have.

Macbeth, 5.3.22

What the leap leads to. Macbeth counts what a king should have in old age (honour, love, obedience, friends) and knows he will have none of it.

Guilt and conscience

Guilt in this play is physical. It stops a man saying ‘Amen’, it will not wash off, it takes a seat at dinner, and it eventually walks the corridors at night with a candle. Nobody is punished for the murders by the state; they are punished by their own minds and bodies.

Through the play Macbeth’s imagination runs ahead of his conscience in Acts I and II, and his wife’s runs behind hers: she is unshaken at the murder and destroyed by it in Act V.

In the text

Will all great Neptune’s ocean wash this blood
Clean from my hand? No, this my hand will rather
The multitudinous seas incarnadine,
Making the green one red.

Macbeth, 2.2.60

The blood that will not wash off. Straight after the murder Macbeth is sure that the sea would turn red before his hand came clean.

Out, damned spot! out, I say!

Lady Macbeth, 5.1.30

In that same scene Lady Macbeth told him that a little water would clear them of the deed. Three acts later she is the one who cannot get her hands clean.

Kingship and the state

Scotland is described as a body: bleeding, sick, needing a physician. Malcolm’s list of the king-becoming graces in 4.3 sets out what Macbeth is not, and the play’s interest is less in who deserves the crown than in what happens to a country when its head is afraid.

Through the play Duncan’s Scotland rewards loyalty in public; Macbeth’s rewards silence; Malcolm’s begins with a promise of order that the audience has been given reason to doubt.

In the text

Our fears do make us traitors.

Lady Macduff, 4.2.4

Under Macbeth a man can be made a traitor by running away. Lady Macduff says so in the scene in which the murderers come for her and her son.

More needs she the divine than the physician.

Doctor, 5.1.64

The Doctor cannot treat the queen. Two scenes later Macbeth asks him to find the disease in Scotland itself and purge it.

Fate, prophecy and equivocation

Every prophecy in the play comes true, and the ones Macbeth relies on mislead him. The apparitions promise safety in terms that are technically exact and practically worthless, which is the same trick the Jesuit doctrine of equivocation was accused of in the Gunpowder Plot trials of 1606.

Through the play The prophecies move from prediction to riddle: Birnam wood does move, and the man not of woman born does exist.

In the text

All hail, Macbeth! that shalt be king hereafter!

Third Witch, 1.3.50

The first prophecy is a plain statement about the future. It says nothing of how the crown will come, and Macbeth supplies the rest.

Macbeth shall never vanquish’d be until
Great Birnam wood to high Dunsinane hill
Shall come against him.

Third Apparition, 4.1.97

By Act IV the promises come as riddles. This one is exact, and Macbeth hears it as a guarantee that he cannot lose.

Macduff was from his mother’s womb
Untimely ripp’d.

Macduff, 5.8.17

The last promise fails on its own wording. Macduff was cut from the womb, so “of woman born” does not cover him.

Appearance and reality

The play opens by declaring that fair is foul, and then shows it again and again: a welcoming castle, a loyal host, an innocent flower, a safe prophecy. Characters repeatedly fail to read each other.

Through the play Duncan cannot find the mind’s construction in the face; by Act IV Malcolm will not trust a face at all, and tests Macduff with a false one of his own.

In the text

Fair is foul, and foul is fair:
Hover through the fog and filthy air.

The Witches, 1.1.12

The witches state the rule in the first scene, before any person in the play has told a lie.

Look like the innocent flower,
But be the serpent under’t.

Lady Macbeth, 1.5.65

Lady Macbeth makes the rule an instruction: when Duncan arrives, Macbeth is to look like one thing and be another.

Is this a dagger which I see before me,
The handle toward my hand?

Macbeth, 2.1.32

Now Macbeth cannot trust his own eyes. He sees a dagger, cannot take hold of it, and goes the way it points.

Manhood and gender

“When you durst do it, then you were a man” is an argument as much as an insult, and it works. The play returns again and again to what manhood is supposed to allow: Macduff’s answer in 4.3, that he must also feel the loss as a man, is offered as a correction to everything Macbeth has been told.

Through the play Lady Macbeth asks to be unsexed in 1.5; Macbeth tells his wife to bring forth men-children only in 1.7; Macduff rejects the equation in 4.3.

In the text

Come, you spirits
That tend on mortal thoughts, unsex me here,
And fill me from the crown to the toe top-full
Of direst cruelty!

Lady Macbeth, 1.5.39

Lady Macbeth treats pity as something that belongs to her sex, and asks for it to be taken out of her so that the murder can be done.

Give sorrow words: the grief that does not speak
Whispers the o’er-fraught heart and bids it break.

Malcolm, 4.3.210

Malcolm’s advice to Macduff, who has just learned that his family is dead. A few lines later Malcolm tells him to “dispute it like a man”, and Macduff answers that he must also feel it as a man.
