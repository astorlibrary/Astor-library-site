Macbeth · William Shakespeare

Context

Dates

1040
The historical Macbeth becomes king of Alba He ruled for seventeen years, a reign Shakespeare compresses to a few violent months.
1057
The historical Macbeth is killed at Lumphanan Not at Dunsinane, and not by Macduff.
1587
Holinshed’s Chronicles reissued Shakespeare’s chief source for the Scottish material, including the meeting with the three weird sisters.
1597
James VI publishes Daemonologie The future king of England writes a dialogue on witchcraft six years before inheriting the English throne.
1603
James VI of Scotland becomes James I of England A Scottish king in London, claiming descent from Banquo, changes what a Scottish play on a London stage can mean.
1605
The Gunpowder Plot The conspiracy and its trials put the word equivocation into everyday London use.
1606
Macbeth most likely written and first performed The Porter’s equivocator is the strongest internal evidence for the date.
1611
Simon Forman records seeing Macbeth at the Globe The earliest surviving eyewitness account of the play in performance.
1623
First Folio The only early printed text of the play, and a short one.
1847
Verdi’s Macbeth premieres in Florence Revised for Paris in 1865, it becomes a performance tradition of its own.
1936
Orson Welles directs the Federal Theatre Project’s Black-cast Macbeth Staged at the Lafayette Theatre in Harlem and relocated to a Caribbean island.

Places

Inverness
Macbeth’s castle, where Duncan is murdered while a guest.
Forres
Duncan’s court, where Macbeth is thanked and Malcolm is named heir.
Dunsinane Hill
Macbeth’s fortress in the last act, and the hill Birnam wood must reach.
Birnam
The wood the soldiers cut for cover, about twelve miles from Dunsinane.
Fife
Macduff’s household, murdered in 4.2.
Iona
Colmekill, where Duncan’s body is carried for burial among his ancestors.

Critics

Samuel Johnson

The Plays of William Shakespeare (1765)

In his note on the play Johnson praised the “solemnity, grandeur, and variety of its action” but found “no nice discriminations of character”: the events are so large that they decide what the characters do. He thought Lady Macbeth “merely detested”, and that Macbeth’s courage keeps some respect for him without making the reader sorry at his fall.

Thomas De Quincey

“On the Knocking at the Gate in Macbeth”, in The London Magazine (1823)

De Quincey asks why the knocking after Duncan’s murder is so disturbing. His answer is that while the murderers are alone the ordinary world is suspended. The knocking announces that it has come back, and the audience then feels how far outside it the murder took place.

A. C. Bradley

Shakespearean Tragedy (1904)

Bradley stresses the darkness, storm and blood that fill the play. He reads Macbeth as a man of strong imagination whose conscience reaches him as terrifying images rather than as moral argument, and Lady Macbeth as someone whose will is stronger than her nature, so that it holds until the deed is done and then gives way.

L. C. Knights

How Many Children Had Lady Macbeth? (1933)

Knights attacked criticism, Bradley’s above all, that treats Shakespeare’s characters as people with lives beyond the play. He read Macbeth as a dramatic poem organised by its imagery and its themes, such as the confusion of values in “Fair is foul” and the disorder that follows the murder of a king. The essay was published as a pamphlet and reprinted in his Explorations (1946).

Cleanth Brooks

“The Naked Babe and the Cloak of Manliness”, in The Well Wrought Urn (1947)

Taking up Caroline Spurgeon’s observation that Macbeth’s titles hang on him like borrowed clothes, Brooks follows a second set of images: babies, from the “naked new-born babe” of Macbeth’s soliloquy to the crowned child the witches show him. He argues that the babe stands for the future, which Macbeth tries and fails to control.

Janet Adelman

““Born of Woman”: Fantasies of Maternal Power in Macbeth”, in Cannibals, Witches, and Divorce (1987)

Adelman argues that the play is shaped by a fear of women’s power over men, seen in the witches and in Lady Macbeth’s call to the spirits to “unsex me here”. She reads the ending, where Macbeth is killed by a man “from his mother’s womb / Untimely ripp’d” and the women have vanished from the play, as a fantasy of escape from that power. A revised version is in her book Suffocating Mothers (1992).

Alan Sinfield

“Macbeth: History, Ideology and Intellectuals”, in Critical Quarterly (1986)

Sinfield notes that the play condemns Macbeth’s violence while praising the violence done on behalf of Duncan and Malcolm, Macbeth’s own in the first act included. He connects this with the claim of James I’s state to be the only lawful user of violence, and asks whether the play supports that claim or shows how it works.
