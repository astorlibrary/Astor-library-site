Desire and the imagined lover

Orsino courts Olivia only by proxy, Olivia falls for a messenger she sees for ten minutes, and Malvolio builds a marriage out of a letter with no name on it. The play’s joke is that every one of them is in love with something they have made up, and that the feeling is real whether or not they are right about the object.

Through the play Orsino’s abstract appetite in 1.1 becomes a proposal to a servant in 5.1, and the play does not stop to explain the transfer.

In the text

If music be the food of love, play on; Give me excess of it, that, surfeiting, The appetite may sicken, and so die.

Orsino, 1.1.1

He is not asking for music because he enjoys it; he wants to overeat until he is sick of the feeling. The metaphor treats love as an appetite that can be killed by indulgence, and eight lines later he stops the music because it is not as sweet as it was: the attention span the whole play measures him by.

Love sought is good, but given unsought is better.

Olivia, 3.1.147

She makes a proverb out of her own position (unasked love is worth more) at the moment she is doing the asking. The line is a rhymed couplet in a scene that slips back and forth between prose and verse, and the shift into sententious rhyme is exactly how a formal woman gives herself permission.

Disguise and identity

Viola’s costume is a practical solution that turns into a trap: it earns her the intimacy she wants and makes the declaration impossible. On a stage where the women were played by boys, a boy playing a girl playing a boy is a machine for saying true things under cover, more than a complication.

Through the play She calls disguise a wickedness in 2.2, tells Olivia in 3.1 that she is not what she is, and never removes the clothes, and the play ends with Orsino still calling her Cesario.

In the text

Disguise, I see, thou art a wickedness, Wherein the pregnant enemy does much.

Viola, 2.2.24

She blames the costume, and the ‘pregnant enemy’ is the devil, ready and resourceful. It is the only moral judgement anybody passes on the disguise in the whole play, and she passes it on herself before anyone else has noticed there is a problem.

Then think you right: I am not what I am.

Viola, 3.1.132

It is a confession that cannot be heard as one, and it is deliberately a step short of a lie. The formula inverts the name God gives himself in Exodus, and Shakespeare gives the same construction to Iago; here it costs its speaker something instead of arming her.

Mourning and its uses

Two households are organised around a dead brother. Olivia’s grief is public and vowed to last seven years; Viola’s is private and she goes to work. The play is interested in the difference between grief you can display and grief you have to earn a living through.

Through the play Neither mourning lasts: Sebastian is not dead, and Olivia’s mourning ends the moment something more interesting arrives.

In the text

What country, friends, is this?

Viola, 1.2.1

Five words that establish everything: she has no idea where she is, no standing, and no information. The Captain’s answer, ‘This is Illyria, lady’, is the last time anybody in the play addresses her as a woman until the final scene.

A blank, my lord. She never told her love, But let concealment, like a worm i’ the bud, Feed on her damask cheek: she pined in thought, And with a green and yellow melancholy She sat like patience on a monument, Smiling at grief.

Viola, 2.4.106

She describes her own situation to the man responsible for it, in the third person and the past tense, and he asks whether the girl died. ‘A blank’ is the exact word for a history nobody was told, and the statue image is chosen for stillness: she is describing the one thing she is not allowed to do, which is act.

Festivity and cruelty

Twelfth Night was the end of the Christmas misrule, and the play shows what happens when the licence runs on too long. The gulling of Malvolio is funny, then uncomfortable, then a man shut in the dark being told by a fake priest that the room is light.

Through the play A catch sung at midnight in 2.3 leads by 4.2 to an imprisonment, and in 5.1 Olivia calls it notorious abuse while Fabian and Feste explain why he had it coming.

In the text

Art any more than a steward? Dost thou think, because thou art virtuous, there shall be no more cakes and ale?

Sir Toby, 2.3.99

Two questions, and the first one is the real argument: Sir Toby thinks rank settles the matter, and morality does not come into it. The line is often quoted as a defence of pleasure against puritanism, but in context it is a gentleman telling a servant to remember his place, and the joke depends on that as much as on the ale.

Madman, thou errest: I say, there is no darkness but ignorance; in which thou art more puzzled than the Egyptians in their fog.

Feste, 4.2.37

The proposition is a scholastic commonplace and it is being used to gaslight a man who has correctly observed that he is shut in the dark. Feste has put on a false beard nobody can see, which makes this the one disguise in the play worn purely for the pleasure of the wearer.

Service, rank and ambition

Almost everybody in Illyria works for somebody. Malvolio’s offence is wanting to cross the line upward; Maria crosses it successfully by marrying Sir Toby, and nobody objects. The play punishes the ambition it finds humourless and rewards the one that makes people laugh.

Through the play The letter’s promise that some have greatness thrust upon them is quoted back at Malvolio by the fool in the last scene, after it has landed him in a dark room.

In the text

Oh, you are sick of self-love, Malvolio, and taste with a distempered appetite.

Olivia, 1.5.75

The diagnosis is delivered in the same appetite imagery Orsino used in the first scene, so the play’s two kinds of self-absorption are made to rhyme. It is also the reason the forged letter will work: his mistress has told him publicly what his weakness is, and Maria is listening.

be not afraid of greatness: some are born great, some achieve greatness, and some have greatness thrust upon ’em.

Malvolio, 2.5.126

Maria’s cleverest stroke is that the sentence says nothing at all: it covers every possible route upward and so confirms whichever one the reader already believes in. Feste quotes it back at him in the last scene, and a flattering commonplace becomes the epitaph of the joke.

Time, patience and endings

Viola hands the knot to time because she can see no other way through, and time duly unties it by producing her brother. Nobody’s cleverness earns the comedy’s resolutions, which is part of why the last act feels magical and also slightly arbitrary.

Through the play From ‘O time! thou must untangle this’ in 2.2 to the whirligig of time bringing in its revenges in 5.1, the play hands responsibility to the calendar.

In the text

O time! thou must untangle this, not I; It is too hard a knot for me to untie!

Viola, 2.2.37

The cleverest person in the play admits she has no plan, and the comedy takes her at her word: nothing she does resolves anything, and the knot is cut by her brother walking on stage in Act V. It is an unusual thing for a Shakespearean protagonist to say, and the play never contradicts it.

An apple, cleft in two, is not more twin Than these two creatures. Which is Sebastian?

Antonio, 5.1.213

The recognition is given to Antonio, ahead of the twins and the lovers, and he has most to lose by it: he has just been calling Viola a traitor. The homely image of a cut apple lands in the middle of the play’s most formal verse, and his question is the one the whole comedy has been withholding.
