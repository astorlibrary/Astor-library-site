Twelfth Night · William Shakespeare

Themes

Desire and the imagined lover

Orsino courts Olivia only by proxy, Olivia falls for a messenger she sees for ten minutes, and Malvolio builds a marriage out of a letter with no name on it. The play’s joke is that every one of them is in love with something they have made up, and that the feeling is real whether or not they are right about the object.

Orsino’s abstract appetite in 1.1 becomes a proposal to a servant in 5.1, and the play does not stop to explain the transfer.

If music be the food of love, play on; Give me excess of it, that, surfeiting, The appetite may sicken, and so die.

Orsino, 1.1.1

Whoe'er I woo, myself would be his wife.

Viola, 1.4.40

Disguise and identity

Viola’s costume is a practical solution that turns into a trap: it earns her the intimacy she wants and makes the declaration impossible. On a stage where the women were played by boys, a boy playing a girl playing a boy is a machine for saying true things under cover, more than a complication.

She calls disguise a wickedness in 2.2, tells Olivia in 3.1 that she is not what she is, and never removes the clothes, and the play ends with Orsino still calling her Cesario.

What country, friends, is this?

Viola, 1.2.1

Whoe'er I woo, myself would be his wife.

Viola, 1.4.40

Mourning and its uses

Two households are organised around a dead brother. Olivia’s grief is public and vowed to last seven years; Viola’s is private and she goes to work. The play is interested in the difference between grief you can display and grief you have to earn a living through.

Neither mourning lasts: Sebastian is not dead, and Olivia’s mourning ends the moment something more interesting arrives.

What country, friends, is this?

Viola, 1.2.1

A blank, my lord. She never told her love, But let concealment, like a worm i' the bud, Feed on her damask cheek: she pined in thought, And with a green and yellow melancholy She sat like patience on a monument, Smiling at grief.

Viola, 2.4.106

Festivity and cruelty

Twelfth Night was the end of the Christmas misrule, and the play shows what happens when the licence runs on too long. The gulling of Malvolio is funny, then uncomfortable, then a man shut in the dark being told by a fake priest that the room is light.

A catch sung at midnight in 2.3 leads by 4.2 to an imprisonment, and in 5.1 Olivia calls it notorious abuse while Fabian and Feste explain why he had it coming.

Trip no further, pretty sweeting; Journeys end in lovers meeting, Every wise man's son doth know.

Feste, 2.3.36

Art any more than a steward? Dost thou think, because thou art virtuous, there shall be no more cakes and ale?

Sir Toby, 2.3.99

Service, rank and ambition

Almost everybody in Illyria works for somebody. Malvolio’s offence is wanting to cross the line upward; Maria crosses it successfully by marrying Sir Toby, and nobody objects. The play punishes the ambition it finds humourless and rewards the one that makes people laugh.

The letter’s promise that some have greatness thrust upon them is quoted back at Malvolio by the fool in the last scene, after it has landed him in a dark room.

Oh, you are sick of self-love, Malvolio, and taste with a distempered appetite.

Olivia, 1.5.75

Art any more than a steward? Dost thou think, because thou art virtuous, there shall be no more cakes and ale?

Sir Toby, 2.3.99

Time, patience and endings

Viola hands the knot to time because she can see no other way through, and time duly unties it by producing her brother. Nobody’s cleverness earns the comedy’s resolutions, which is part of why the last act feels magical and also slightly arbitrary.

From ‘O time! thou must untangle this’ in 2.2 to the whirligig of time bringing in its revenges in 5.1, the play hands responsibility to the calendar.

O time! thou must untangle this, not I; It is too hard a knot for me to untie!

Viola, 2.2.37

Trip no further, pretty sweeting; Journeys end in lovers meeting, Every wise man's son doth know.

Feste, 2.3.36
