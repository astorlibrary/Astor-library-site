The Tempest · William Shakespeare

Themes

Rule and service

Every relationship on the island is a version of master and servant: Prospero and Ariel, Prospero and Caliban, Prospero and Ferdinand at the log-pile, Stephano and his new subject. The play sets them side by side so that the differences between labour, slavery, apprenticeship and worship become visible.

The first act establishes three servants with three grievances; the last frees one, claims another and says nothing at all about the third.

What cares these roarers for the name of king? To cabin: silence! trouble us not.

Boatswain, 1.1.15

If by your art, my dearest father, you have Put the wild waters in this roar, allay them.

Miranda, 1.2.1

Ownership of the island

Caliban’s claim is stated plainly in the first act and never refuted, only overruled. Shakespeare gives the dispossessed party the historical facts and the finest description of the place, and gives the occupier the power, which is why writers of the decolonising world answered it, Aimé Césaire with his own Une tempête in 1969.

‘This island’s mine’ in 1.2 sets the terms; Gonzalo’s commonwealth in 2.1 imagines an island belonging to nobody; the play ends without saying who is left on it.

This island's mine, by Sycorax my mother, Which thou takest from me.

Caliban, 1.2.392

You taught me language; and my profit on't Is, I know how to curse. The red plague rid you For learning me your language!

Caliban, 1.2.426

Magic and theatre

Prospero’s art is indistinguishable from stagecraft: costumes, music, entrances, illusions, an interrupted masque. When he describes his own show dissolving he uses the words ‘the great globe itself’, spoken in a theatre of that name, and the metaphor stops being a metaphor.

The storm is staged in 1.1, exposed as a production in 1.2, perfected in the masque of 4.1, renounced in 5.1, and handed to the audience in the epilogue.

If by your art, my dearest father, you have Put the wild waters in this roar, allay them.

Miranda, 1.2.1

Hell is empty And all the devils are here.

Ariel, 1.2.248

Freedom

Three characters want to be free and each means something different by it: Ariel wants release from service, Caliban wants his island back, and Stephano’s crew want a bottle and a crown. The play is careful to make the drunkards’ freedom song ridiculous without making Caliban’s demand ridiculous.

Ariel asks in 1.2 and is refused; Caliban sings of a new master in 2.2; Ariel is released in the last twenty lines, and Prospero then asks to be freed himself.

Is there more toil? Since thou dost give me pains, Let me remember thee what thou hast promised, Which is not yet perform'd me.

Ariel, 1.2.283

I am your wife, if you will marry me; If not, I'll die your maid: to be your fellow You may deny me; but I'll be your servant, Whether you will or no.

Miranda, 3.1.98

Forgiveness

Prospero has the power to do anything he likes to the men who ruined him, and the decisive scene is the one in which he decides not to. The timing is strange: he chooses mercy after a spirit has described their suffering to him, and before any of them has asked for it.

Twelve years of grievance are recited in 1.2; the harpy indicts the guilty in 3.3; 5.1 turns on Ariel’s remark that he would feel pity if he were human.

Full fathom five thy father lies; Of his bones are coral made; Those are pearls that were his eyes: Nothing of him that doth fade But doth suffer a sea-change Into something rich and strange.

Ariel, 1.2.463

Though with their high wrongs I am struck to th’ quick, Yet with my nobler reason ’gainst my fury Do I take part. The rarer action is In virtue than in vengeance.

Prospero, 5.1.29

Nature and nurture

Prospero insists that Caliban is a devil on whom nurture will never stick, having spent years teaching him. Miranda, raised on the same island by the same man with the same books, is his counter-example, and the play leaves the argument standing unresolved between them.

Caliban is taught language in 1.2 and uses it to curse; Prospero’s verdict comes in 4.1; the masque of Ceres and Juno makes fertility itself a thing to be blessed and supervised.

You taught me language; and my profit on't Is, I know how to curse. The red plague rid you For learning me your language!

Caliban, 1.2.426

A devil, a born devil, on whose nature Nurture can never stick; on whom my pains, Humanely taken, all, all lost, quite lost;

Prospero, 4.1.206
