Rule and service

Every relationship on the island is a version of master and servant: Prospero and Ariel, Prospero and Caliban, Prospero and Ferdinand at the log-pile, Stephano and his new subject. The play sets them side by side so that the differences between labour, slavery, apprenticeship and worship become visible.

Through the play The first act establishes three servants with three grievances; the last frees one, claims another and says nothing at all about the third.

In the text

What cares these roarers for the name of king? To cabin: silence! trouble us not.

Boatswain, 1.1.15

A sailor tells a king that titles mean nothing to a storm. Within a hundred lines the storm turns out to be Prospero’s.

What seest thou else In the dark backward and abysm of time?

Prospero, 1.2.59

Prospero checks how little Miranda knows before deciding how much to tell her, and the grandeur of the image hides the control.

Remember First to possess his books; for without them He’s but a sot, as I am, nor hath not One spirit to command

Caliban, 3.2.86

The servant sees how the island is ruled: the power is in the library, and without it Prospero is a man like any other.

Ownership of the island

Caliban’s claim is stated plainly in the first act and never refuted, only overruled. Shakespeare gives the dispossessed party the historical facts and the finest description of the place, and gives the occupier the power, which is why writers of the decolonising world answered it, Aimé Césaire with his own Une tempête in 1969.

Through the play ‘This island’s mine’ in 1.2 sets the terms; Gonzalo’s commonwealth in 2.1 imagines an island belonging to nobody; the play ends without saying who is left on it.

In the text

This island’s mine, by Sycorax my mother, Which thou takest from me.

Caliban, 1.2.392

Caliban argues by inheritance, as Prospero would argue for Milan. Nobody disputes the facts; they change the subject to his conduct.

Be not afeard; the isle is full of noises, Sounds and sweet airs, that give delight and hurt not.

Caliban, 3.2.131

The play gives its most delicate verse about the island to the man everyone on stage calls a beast.

Two of these fellows you Must know and own. This thing of darkness I Acknowledge mine.

Prospero, 5.1.309

“Acknowledge” can claim a possession or admit a kinship, and the actor playing Prospero has to choose.

Magic and theatre

Prospero’s art is indistinguishable from stagecraft: costumes, music, entrances, illusions, an interrupted masque. When he describes his own show dissolving he uses the words ‘the great globe itself’, spoken in a theatre of that name, and the metaphor stops being a metaphor.

Through the play The storm is staged in 1.1, exposed as a production in 1.2, perfected in the masque of 4.1, renounced in 5.1, and handed to the audience in the epilogue.

In the text

If by your art, my dearest father, you have Put the wild waters in this roar, allay them.

Miranda, 1.2.1

Miranda knows at once that the storm is her father’s work, which says more about the household than any description could.

Our revels now are ended. These our actors, As I foretold you, were all spirits and Are melted into air, into thin air:

Prospero, 4.1.162

An apology for a cancelled entertainment grows, within eight lines, into the play’s largest statement about existence.

But this rough magic I here abjure, and, when I have required Some heavenly music, which even now I do, To work mine end upon their senses that This airy charm is for, I’ll break my staff, Bury it certain fathoms in the earth, And deeper than did ever plummet sound I’ll drown my book.

Prospero, 5.1.55

Prospero renounces his art while he uses it: the same sentence orders up the music that will disarm his enemies.

Freedom

Three characters want to be free and each means something different by it: Ariel wants release from service, Caliban wants his island back, and Stephano’s crew want a bottle and a crown. The play is careful to make the drunkards’ freedom song ridiculous without making Caliban’s demand ridiculous.

Through the play Ariel asks in 1.2 and is refused; Caliban sings of a new master in 2.2; Ariel is released in the last twenty lines, and Prospero then asks to be freed himself.

In the text

Is there more toil? Since thou dost give me pains, Let me remember thee what thou hast promised, Which is not yet perform’d me.

Ariel, 1.2.283

Ariel’s complaint is a contract: work done, a promise made, payment outstanding. Prospero answers with gratitude, not terms.

I am your wife, if you will marry me; If not, I’ll die your maid: to be your fellow You may deny me; but I’ll be your servant, Whether you will or no.

Miranda, 3.1.98

Kept ignorant of her own life for twelve years, Miranda proposes, sets out the terms and closes off a refusal.

As you from crimes would pardon’d be, Let your indulgence set me free.

Prospero, Epilogue.19

The man who has spent the play releasing others, or declining to, ends by asking the audience to release him.

Forgiveness

Prospero has the power to do anything he likes to the men who ruined him, and the decisive scene is the one in which he decides not to. The timing is strange: he chooses mercy after a spirit has described their suffering to him, and before any of them has asked for it.

Through the play Twelve years of grievance are recited in 1.2; the harpy indicts the guilty in 3.3; 5.1 turns on Ariel’s remark that he would feel pity if he were human.

In the text

Full fathom five thy father lies; Of his bones are coral made; Those are pearls that were his eyes: Nothing of him that doth fade But doth suffer a sea-change Into something rich and strange.

Ariel, 1.2.463

The song tells a gentle lie: the drowned father becomes coral and pearl, so grief arrives already consoled, as the last act will claim.

Though with their high wrongs I am struck to th’ quick, Yet with my nobler reason ’gainst my fury Do I take part. The rarer action is In virtue than in vengeance.

Prospero, 5.1.29

Twelve years of planning end in four lines, prompted by a spirit. “Rarer” concedes that mercy is the less natural choice.

How beauteous mankind is! O brave new world, That has such people in’t!

Miranda, 5.1.205

Miranda admires men who set her adrift to die, and Prospero’s four-word reply is all the irony the scene needs.

Nature and nurture

Prospero insists that Caliban is a devil on whom nurture will never stick, having spent years teaching him. Miranda, raised on the same island by the same man with the same books, is his counter-example, and the play leaves the argument standing unresolved between them.

Through the play Caliban is taught language in 1.2 and uses it to curse; Prospero’s verdict comes in 4.1; the masque of Ceres and Juno makes fertility itself a thing to be blessed and supervised.

In the text

You taught me language; and my profit on’t Is, I know how to curse. The red plague rid you For learning me your language!

Caliban, 1.2.426

Caliban complains in fluent blank verse that he learned only to curse, so the complaint disproves itself.

A devil, a born devil, on whose nature Nurture can never stick; on whom my pains, Humanely taken, all, all lost, quite lost;

Prospero, 4.1.206

Prospero says nurture will never stick on Caliban, after years spent teaching him. The repetitions are a man arguing with himself.
