The divided self

Jekyll’s theory is that a man contains two natures and would be happier if they were housed separately. Stevenson shows the theory failing in an interesting way: separating the two frees the other half and leaves the good one with the bill. Hyde is a licence; he did not break in.

Through the book Proposed as a beloved daydream in the final statement, achieved with the first draught, and abandoned when the transformations begin to happen without the drug.

In the text

man is not truly one, but truly two. I say two, because the state of my own knowledge does not pass beyond that point.

Dr Henry Jekyll, Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case

Jekyll’s own statement of the theory, and he limits it himself: two is as far as his knowledge reaches, not a finding about every man.

Though so profound a double-dealer, I was in no sense a hypocrite; both sides of me were in dead earnest

Dr Henry Jekyll, Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case

The division was there long before the drug. Jekyll lived both lives in earnest for years; the experiment gave the second one a body of its own.

And yet when I looked upon that ugly idol in the glass, I was conscious of no repugnance, rather of a leap of welcome. This, too, was myself.

Dr Henry Jekyll, Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case

Every other witness feels disgust at Hyde’s face. Jekyll feels welcome, which undoes his later claim that Hyde was a stranger to him.

Respectability

Almost everybody in this book protects somebody’s name. Enfield stops telling his story, Utterson pockets evidence in a murder case, Jekyll’s whole experiment exists so that his standing can survive his appetites. The Gothic pressure comes less from the laboratory than from the number of educated men who decline to ask a question.

Through the book Enfield’s discretion in chapter one, Utterson’s suppression of the letter, and Jekyll’s confession that he was never a hypocrite because both halves of him were sincere.

In the text

I let my brother go to the devil in his own way.

Gabriel John Utterson, Story of the Door

Utterson’s rule before the story starts: he will not interfere in a friend’s private life. The plot then tests the rule on Jekyll.

the person that drew the cheque is the very pink of the proprieties, celebrated too, and (what makes it worse) one of your fellows who do what they call good

Richard Enfield, Story of the Door

The scandal is the name on the cheque. Enfield’s “what makes it worse” takes it for granted that a good name and private violence can go together.

Edward Hyde would pass away like the stain of breath upon a mirror

Dr Henry Jekyll, Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case

What Jekyll wanted from the drug was safety: Hyde would vanish, and the respectable man at home could laugh at suspicion.

Documents and testimony

A will, a cheque, a letter, a comparison of handwriting, a sealed envelope, a statement: the story reaches the reader almost entirely as paper handled by a lawyer. What looks like a supernatural tale is organised like a case file, and the last two chapters are exhibits that arrive when neither witness can be questioned.

Through the book Utterson’s safe holds the will from the second chapter; by the last, it holds Lanyon’s envelope and Jekyll’s statement, and the story is over before anyone reads them.

In the text

disappearance or unexplained absence for any period exceeding three calendar months

Search for Mr. Hyde

A legal paper drawn up for a man who might disappear without dying. Utterson reads it as a sign of blackmail, which is a sound inference and wrong.

It is one thing to mortify curiosity, another to conquer it

Remarkable Incident of Dr. Lanyon

The lawyer chooses not to open a packet he is entitled to hold. The case could be over here if one respectable man read what he had been given.

Here then, as I lay down the pen and proceed to seal up my confession, I bring the life of that unhappy Henry Jekyll to an end.

Dr Henry Jekyll, Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case

Henry Jekyll ends with the sealing of a document. The book reaches its reader as papers in a lawyer’s hands, and the last of them is his.

London

Stevenson gives Jekyll’s house two addresses. The front is a comfortable square; the back is the neglected door on a by-street that Enfield knows. Soho appears through fog as a district in a nightmare, and the geography does the argument’s work: one building, two entrances, and a respectable man who uses both.

Through the book The door in chapter one, the Soho rooms after the murder, the court below the window, and finally the dissecting rooms between the two halves of the house.

In the text

It was two storeys high; showed no window, nothing but a door on the lower storey and a blind forehead of discoloured wall on the upper; and bore in every feature, the marks of prolonged and sordid negligence.

Story of the Door

The back of Jekyll’s house, before anyone knows it is his: no window, no bell or knocker, and a wall with a blind forehead that cannot look back.

The indescribable

Every witness says Hyde is deformed and no witness can name the deformity. Stevenson makes the failure repeat until it becomes the subject: the response to Hyde is social and instinctive before it is visual, which leaves a reader to wonder what these respectable observers think they are recognising.

Through the book Enfield cannot specify it, Utterson cannot, Poole cannot, and Jekyll finally explains it as evil written plainly on a face, which is an explanation from the least reliable source available.

In the text

There is something wrong with his appearance; something displeasing, something downright detestable. I never saw a man I so disliked, and yet I scarce know why.

Richard Enfield, Story of the Door

Enfield tries three times and gives no physical detail at all. He describes his own dislike and offers it as a description of Hyde.

It sounds nothing to hear, but it was hellish to see. It wasn’t like a man; it was like some damned Juggernaut.

Richard Enfield, Story of the Door

Ordinary English will not serve, so Enfield borrows a word brought home by imperial travellers, and admits that it “sounds nothing to hear”.

if ever I read Satan’s signature upon a face, it is on that of your new friend.

Gabriel John Utterson, Search for Mr. Hyde

Utterson reaches for a signature, the one thing that settles a question in his profession, and can only say he has read it on a face.

Appetite and habit

Stevenson leaves the pleasures vague and describes the mechanics of dependence precisely. Jekyll starts taking the draught for enjoyment, then to be himself again, then in double doses, then at any hour; the last crisis is a failed supply of a chemical. Read this way, it is a careful account of a habit.

Through the book An occasional escape becomes an involuntary change in Regent’s Park, and the final chapter turns on a drawer of salt that cannot be replaced.

In the text

the moment I choose, I can be rid of Mr. Hyde.

Dr Henry Jekyll, Dr. Jekyll Was Quite at Ease

“The moment I choose” is true when Jekyll says it. His statement is the story of the point at which choosing stopped.

My devil had been long caged, he came out roaring.

Dr Henry Jekyll, Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case

By Jekyll’s own account, two months of virtuous living made the next outbreak worse. The Carew murder follows his attempt to reform.

I was slowly losing hold of my original and better self, and becoming slowly incorporated with my second and worse.

Dr Henry Jekyll, Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case

Now it takes the drug to stay Jekyll, not to become Hyde. “Slowly”, twice in one sentence, gives the change the shape of a habit.
