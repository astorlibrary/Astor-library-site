The divided self

Jekyll’s theory is that a man contains two natures and would be happier if they were housed separately. Stevenson shows the theory failing in an interesting way: separating the two frees the other half and leaves the good one with the bill. Hyde is a licence; he did not break in.

Through the book Proposed as a beloved daydream in the final statement, achieved with the first draught, and abandoned when the transformations begin to happen without the drug.

In the text

He began to go wrong, wrong in mind; and though of course I continue to take an interest in him for old sake’s sake as they say, I see and I have seen devilish little of the man.

Dr Hastie Lanyon, Search for Mr. Hyde

The repetition of ‘wrong’, first moral and then mental, is Lanyon trying to be charitable and failing. He has diagnosed his colleague as mad, the most generous verdict orthodox science can offer, and one night’s demonstration, which he asked for, will overturn it.

there before my eyes⁠—pale and shaken, and half fainting, and groping before him with his hands, like a man restored from death⁠—there stood Henry Jekyll!

Dr Hastie Lanyon, Dr. Lanyon’s Narrative

The transformation the whole book has been holding back is given in a single clause, and it is the restoration: Hyde becoming Jekyll. Lanyon’s narrative then stops. He tells Utterson what he saw and will not write down what he was told afterwards, which is the last act of professional discretion in the story.

Respectability

Almost everybody in this book protects somebody’s name. Enfield stops telling his story, Utterson pockets evidence in a murder case, Jekyll’s whole experiment exists so that his standing can survive his appetites. The Gothic pressure comes less from the laboratory than from the number of educated men who decline to ask a question.

Through the book Enfield’s discretion in chapter one, Utterson’s suppression of the letter, and Jekyll’s confession that he was never a hypocrite because both halves of him were sincere.

In the text

I let my brother go to the devil in his own way.

Gabriel John Utterson, Story of the Door

It is offered as tolerance and it is also a policy of non-intervention, stated before any of the events. Cain’s question was whether he was his brother’s keeper; Utterson has decided in advance that he is not, and the plot then gives him a friend going to the devil and eight chapters in which he never really steps in.

It is one thing to mortify curiosity, another to conquer it

Remarkable Incident of Dr. Lanyon

Mortify is the word used earlier for Utterson’s habit of drinking gin instead of the wine he loves, and here it covers a lawyer choosing not to open a packet he is legally entitled to hold. The novella’s plot could end at this sentence if one respectable man broke one seal, and the point is that he does not.

Documents and testimony

A will, a cheque, a letter, a comparison of handwriting, a sealed envelope, a statement: the story reaches the reader almost entirely as paper handled by a lawyer. What looks like a supernatural tale is organised like a case file, and the last two chapters are exhibits that arrive when neither witness can be questioned.

Through the book Utterson’s safe holds the will from the second chapter; by the last, it holds Lanyon’s envelope and Jekyll’s statement, and the story is over before anyone reads them.

In the text

disappearance or unexplained absence for any period exceeding three calendar months

Search for Mr. Hyde

A will normally provides for death; this one provides for a man ceasing to be present without dying. Utterson reads it as evidence that Hyde has a hold over his friend, which is a reasonable professional inference and completely wrong. The clause is in fact the most honest sentence Jekyll writes before his confession.

The stick with which the deed had been done, although it was of some rare and very tough and heavy wood, had broken in the middle under the stress of this insensate cruelty

The Carew Murder Case

The cane was a present from Utterson, and half of it will be found in Jekyll’s house: the murder weapon travels from the lawyer to the victim’s body to the respectable friend’s rooms. Stevenson measures the violence indirectly, by what it did to a piece of hardwood, which is more frightening than a description of the body would have been.

London

Stevenson gives Jekyll’s house two addresses. The front is a comfortable square; the back is the neglected door on a by-street that Enfield knows. Soho appears through fog as a district in a nightmare, and the geography does the argument’s work: one building, two entrances, and a respectable man who uses both.

Through the book The door in chapter one, the Soho rooms after the murder, the court below the window, and finally the dissecting rooms between the two halves of the house.

In the text

It was two storeys high; showed no window, nothing but a door on the lower storey and a blind forehead of discoloured wall on the upper; and bore in every feature, the marks of prolonged and sordid negligence.

Story of the Door

‘Blind forehead’ gives the building a face that cannot look back, which is the problem every witness will have with Hyde. The whole description is of absences (no window, nothing but a door, neither bell nor knocker), so the reader meets the concealed half of Jekyll’s house as a subtraction from an ordinary street.

The indescribable

Every witness says Hyde is deformed and no witness can name the deformity. Stevenson makes the failure repeat until it becomes the subject: the response to Hyde is social and instinctive before it is visual, which leaves a reader to wonder what these respectable observers think they are recognising.

Through the book Enfield cannot specify it, Utterson cannot, Poole cannot, and Jekyll finally explains it as evil written plainly on a face, which is an explanation from the least reliable source available.

In the text

It sounds nothing to hear, but it was hellish to see. It wasn’t like a man; it was like some damned Juggernaut.

Richard Enfield, Story of the Door

Enfield reaches for a Hindu temple car brought back by imperial travellers because ordinary English will not do the job, and he flags the failure himself: it sounds nothing to hear. The comparison makes Hyde mechanical more than cruel: something that keeps going without deciding anything.

And next moment, with apelike fury, he was trampling his victim under foot and hailing down a storm of blows, under which the bones were audibly shattered and the body jumped upon the roadway.

The Carew Murder Case

The violence is graded by sound and motion more than by injury: audibly shattered, and a body that jumps. Apelike is the closest the book comes to the Victorian idea that the lower nature is an earlier one, and the observation belongs to a witness who faints.

Appetite and habit

Stevenson leaves the pleasures vague and describes the mechanics of dependence precisely. Jekyll starts taking the draught for enjoyment, then to be himself again, then in double doses, then at any hour; the last crisis is a failed supply of a chemical. Read this way, it is a careful account of a habit.

Through the book An occasional escape becomes an involuntary change in Regent’s Park, and the final chapter turns on a drawer of salt that cannot be replaced.

In the text

the moment I choose, I can be rid of Mr. Hyde.

Dr Henry Jekyll, Dr. Jekyll Was Quite at Ease

Every word of it is true when he says it and false within the year: a man is describing an escape he has not yet failed to make. ‘Choose’ is the load-bearing word, and the final statement is largely an account of the point at which choosing stopped being available.

Edward Hyde would pass away like the stain of breath upon a mirror

Dr Henry Jekyll, Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case

The image is of something that was never a substance in the first place, which is how Jekyll would like the arrangement understood. Look at what follows it in the sentence: a man safe at home who could afford to laugh at suspicion. The comfort being described is immunity from consequences, which is a different thing from moral relief.
