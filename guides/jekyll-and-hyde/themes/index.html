Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde · Robert Louis Stevenson

Themes

The divided self

Jekyll’s theory is that a man contains two natures and would be happier if they were housed separately. Stevenson shows the theory failing in an interesting way: separating the two frees the other half and leaves the good one with the bill. Hyde is a licence; he did not break in.

Proposed as a beloved daydream in the final statement, achieved with the first draught, and abandoned when the transformations begin to happen without the drug.

disappearance or unexplained absence for any period exceeding three calendar months

Search for Mr. Hyde

He began to go wrong, wrong in mind; and though of course I continue to take an interest in him for old sake’s sake as they say, I see and I have seen devilish little of the man.

Dr Hastie Lanyon, Search for Mr. Hyde

Respectability

Almost everybody in this book protects somebody’s name. Enfield stops telling his story, Utterson pockets evidence in a murder case, Jekyll’s whole experiment exists so that his standing can survive his appetites. The Gothic pressure comes less from the laboratory than from the number of educated men who decline to ask a question.

Enfield’s discretion in chapter one, Utterson’s suppression of the letter, and Jekyll’s confession that he was never a hypocrite because both halves of him were sincere.

I let my brother go to the devil in his own way.

Gabriel John Utterson, Story of the Door

I never saw a circle of such hateful faces; and there was the man in the middle, with a kind of black, sneering coolness

Richard Enfield, Story of the Door

Documents and testimony

A will, a cheque, a letter, a comparison of handwriting, a sealed envelope, a statement: the story reaches the reader almost entirely as paper handled by a lawyer. What looks like a supernatural tale is organised like a case file, and the last two chapters are exhibits that arrive when neither witness can be questioned.

Utterson’s safe holds the will from the second chapter; by the last, it holds Lanyon’s envelope and Jekyll’s statement, and the story is over before anyone reads them.

disappearance or unexplained absence for any period exceeding three calendar months

Search for Mr. Hyde

If he be Mr. Hyde,” he had thought, “I shall be Mr. Seek.

Gabriel John Utterson, Search for Mr. Hyde

London

Stevenson gives Jekyll’s house two addresses. The front is a comfortable square; the back is the neglected door on a by-street that Enfield knows. Soho appears through fog as a district in a nightmare, and the geography does the argument’s work: one building, two entrances, and a respectable man who uses both.

The door in chapter one, the Soho rooms after the murder, the court below the window, and finally the dissecting rooms between the two halves of the house.

It was two storeys high; showed no window, nothing but a door on the lower storey and a blind forehead of discoloured wall on the upper; and bore in every feature, the marks of prolonged and sordid negligence.

Story of the Door

The indescribable

Every witness says Hyde is deformed and no witness can name the deformity. Stevenson makes the failure repeat until it becomes the subject: the response to Hyde is social and instinctive before it is visual, which leaves a reader to wonder what these respectable observers think they are recognising.

Enfield cannot specify it, Utterson cannot, Poole cannot, and Jekyll finally explains it as evil written plainly on a face, which is an explanation from the least reliable source available.

It was two storeys high; showed no window, nothing but a door on the lower storey and a blind forehead of discoloured wall on the upper; and bore in every feature, the marks of prolonged and sordid negligence.

Story of the Door

It sounds nothing to hear, but it was hellish to see. It wasn’t like a man; it was like some damned Juggernaut.

Richard Enfield, Story of the Door

Appetite and habit

Stevenson leaves the pleasures vague and describes the mechanics of dependence precisely. Jekyll starts taking the draught for enjoyment, then to be himself again, then in double doses, then at any hour; the last crisis is a failed supply of a chemical. Read this way, it is a careful account of a habit.

An occasional escape becomes an involuntary change in Regent’s Park, and the final chapter turns on a drawer of salt that cannot be replaced.

the moment I choose, I can be rid of Mr. Hyde.

Dr Henry Jekyll, Dr. Jekyll Was Quite at Ease

the unjust might go his way, delivered from the aspirations and remorse of his more upright twin; and the just could walk steadfastly and securely on his upward path

Dr Henry Jekyll, Henry Jekyll’s Full Statement of the Case
