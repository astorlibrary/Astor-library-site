Pride and Prejudice · Jane Austen

Characters

Elizabeth Bennet

Second of the five Bennet daughters; first appears Chapter 1

Quick, funny and justly confident in her own judgement, which is exactly why the novel arranges for her to be wrong about the two men she is most certain of. Her wit is a form of courage in a household where nobody else will say anything true.

Fitzwilliam Darcy

Owner of Pemberley in Derbyshire; first appears Chapter 3

Ten thousand a year and no talent for a room full of strangers. Austen gives him the rudest remark at the first ball and then spends fifty chapters showing that his conduct has always been better than his manner, which is not quite the same as saying he was right.

Jane Bennet

The eldest Bennet daughter; first appears Chapter 1

Her determination to think well of everybody is presented as a moral achievement and a practical disaster: it makes her unreadable to Bingley, unreadable to Darcy, and defenceless against Caroline Bingley. The novel likes her and does not recommend her method.

Charles Bingley

Tenant of Netherfield Park; first appears Chapter 3

Amiable, rich and so willing to be guided that his friend can detach him from the woman he loves with a few words about her family. He is the novel’s evidence that good nature without judgement is a kind of weakness.

George Wickham

Lieutenant in the militia; first appears Chapter 15

He tells Elizabeth a story about Darcy within an hour of meeting her, and she believes it because it is well told and because she wants to. His charm is a working method, and the militia’s stay in Meryton gives it a market.

Mr Collins

Clergyman of Hunsford, heir to Longbourn; first appears Chapter 13

He proposes to two women in three days and is not embarrassed by either outcome, because the proposal was never about a person. Everything he says is addressed upwards to his patroness, even when he is standing in somebody else’s parlour insulting their daughter.

Charlotte Lucas

Elizabeth’s friend, later Mrs Collins; first appears Chapter 5

Twenty-seven, without fortune, and the only character who states the terms of the marriage market out loud without either joking or complaining. Her parsonage is arranged so that she sees as little of her husband as the house allows, which is the closest the novel comes to a verdict on the bargain.

Lydia Bennet

The youngest Bennet daughter; first appears Chapter 1

Fifteen, unsupervised and encouraged, she does exactly what her upbringing has trained her to do, and the novel is careful to spread the blame across her mother’s indulgence and her father’s amused neglect. Her elopement is the one plot event that could not be undone by an explanation.

Mr Bennet

Master of Longbourn; first appears Chapter 1

He retreats to his library and treats his family as a source of entertainment, and the novel charges him for it: the daughter he never bothered to check runs away, and his best line, about making sport for our neighbours, comes after he has found out what that costs.

Lady Catherine de Bourgh

Of Rosings Park, Darcy’s aunt; first appears Chapter 29

She gives orders about other people’s carriages, shelves and marriages with the same confidence, and puts the class objection to the match to Elizabeth’s face. Her one decisive action in the plot is the one that brings the marriage about.

Mrs Bennet

Mistress of Longbourn; first appears Chapter 1

Ridiculous and not wrong. Her one subject is getting five daughters married before her husband dies, and the entail means that the alternative is real poverty, so the novel laughs at her method and not her motive.
