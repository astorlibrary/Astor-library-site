Pride and Prejudice · Jane Austen

What happens

Chapters 1–12 Bingley at Netherfield, and the Meryton assembly

A single man with four or five thousand a year moves into the neighbourhood and the Bennets begin calculating. The Meryton assembly fixes Darcy’s reputation in one overheard sentence, and Jane’s convenient illness keeps Elizabeth at Netherfield long enough for the two of them to start sparring in earnest.

Chapters 13–23 Mr Collins, Wickham and Charlotte’s marriage

Mr Collins arrives to inspect the estate he will inherit and the cousin he intends to marry; Wickham arrives with a grievance that flatters Elizabeth’s judgement. Within three days Collins is refused, Charlotte Lucas accepts him, and the Bingleys leave for London taking Jane’s prospects with them.

Chapters 24–34 Hunsford, Rosings and Darcy’s proposal

Elizabeth visits Charlotte’s parsonage and sees what a comfortable home actually costs. Lady Catherine presides, Colonel Fitzwilliam lets slip that Darcy separated Bingley from Jane, and Darcy proposes in terms that make him easy to refuse, though she cries for half an hour once he has gone.

Chapters 35–42 Darcy’s letter

Darcy hands over a letter and walks away, and the novel changes direction without a single event taking place. Elizabeth reads it twice, reconstructs Wickham from the beginning, and arrives at the only self-accusation in the book that is not comic.

Chapters 43–50 Pemberley, and the news from Brighton

A house, a housekeeper and a set of grounds supply the evidence about Darcy that conversation could not. Then Lydia runs off with Wickham, and the family’s standing, which has been a joke for forty chapters, becomes something that can actually be destroyed.

Chapters 51–61 Lydia’s marriage, Lady Catherine and the engagements

Lydia returns married and unembarrassed, and a slip of Lydia’s tongue leads Elizabeth to find out who paid for the marriage. Lady Catherine’s attempt to extract a promise produces the opposite of the one she wants, and the last chapters distribute incomes as carefully as affections.
