First impressions and judgement

The discarded title is still the working one. Almost every character forms a verdict in a single evening and then spends the book defending it, and Austen makes the reader do the same, so the correction lands on both.

Through the book Fixed at the Meryton assembly, hardened by Wickham’s story, dismantled by a letter in Chapter 35, and formally re-examined at Pemberley.

In the text

She is tolerable; but not handsome enough to tempt me; and I am in no humour at present to give consequence to young ladies who are slighted by other men.

Mr Darcy, Chapter 3

Darcy’s verdict on Elizabeth, given in one evening and within her hearing. Hers on him follows from it.

I had not known you a month before I felt that you were the last man in the world whom I could ever be prevailed on to marry.

Elizabeth Bennet, Chapter 34

Elizabeth tells Darcy how quickly she made up her mind about him: within a month of their meeting.

In what an amiable light does this place him!

Elizabeth Bennet, Chapter 43

At Pemberley she judges him again, this time on his housekeeper’s account of how he has behaved for years.

Marriage and money

Every attachment in the novel is quoted with a figure attached, from Bingley’s four or five thousand a year to the hundred a year Mr Bennet is asked to promise for Lydia. Austen does not treat this as vulgar; she treats it as the arithmetic within which affection has to operate.

Through the book Stated as a joke in the first sentence, argued seriously by Charlotte in Chapter 22, and paid in cash by Darcy in the last volume.

In the text

It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.

Chapter 1

The joke. The sentence speaks of what a rich man wants, and the chapter that follows shows that the wanting is Mrs Bennet’s.

I am not romantic you know. I never was. I ask only a comfortable home; and considering Mr. Collins’s character, connections, and situation in life, I am convinced that my chance of happiness with him is as fair, as most people can boast on entering the marriage state.

Charlotte Lucas, Chapter 22

Charlotte says the same thing with no joke in it. She is twenty-seven with little money of her own, and she takes the house.

Rank and connection

The obstacle between Elizabeth and Darcy is her relations more than her money: an uncle in trade, an aunt in Cheapside, a mother who cannot be taken anywhere. The novel dismantles the objection by making the Gardiners the most sensible adults in it.

Through the book Voiced by Caroline Bingley, formalised by Darcy’s first proposal, and shouted by Lady Catherine in the last volume after it has stopped being true for anyone the reader trusts.

In the text

In vain have I struggled. It will not do. My feelings will not be repressed. You must allow me to tell you how ardently I admire and love you.

Mr Darcy, Chapter 34

Darcy begins his proposal with his struggle against making it. He goes on to tell Elizabeth that her family is beneath his.

Are the shades of Pemberley to be thus polluted?

Lady Catherine de Bourgh, Chapter 56

Lady Catherine makes the same objection in its most extreme form, and Elizabeth will not give way to it.

Entail and female security

Longbourn passes to Mr Collins, so five daughters must find husbands or find lodgings. The legal fact sits under every comic scene, and it is why Charlotte’s choice is a calculation and not a failure of feeling.

Through the book Explained early and never forgotten: it makes Mrs Bennet’s campaign rational, Collins’s proposal a favour in his own eyes, and Lydia’s ruin a threat to four other women.

In the text

An unhappy alternative is before you, Elizabeth. From this day you must be a stranger to one of your parents. Your mother will never see you again if you do not marry Mr. Collins, and I will never see you again if you do.

Mr Bennet, Chapter 20

Mr Bennet can make a joke of the proposal. Mrs Bennet cannot, because Mr Collins will inherit the house she lives in.

Unhappy as the event must be for Lydia, we may draw from it this useful lesson; that loss of virtue in a female is irretrievable⁠—that one false step involves her in endless ruin⁠—that her reputation is no less brittle than it is beautiful⁠—and that she cannot be too much guarded in her behaviour towards the undeserving of the other sex.

Mary Bennet, Chapter 47

Lydia’s elopement endangers her sisters as well as herself. With Longbourn entailed away they depend on marrying, and a disgraced family is harder to marry into.

Reputation and family conduct

Character in this novel is public property. A sister’s elopement is treated as a defect in every other sister, and Mary’s moralising about a woman’s irretrievable ruin is contemptible and, within the world of the book, accurate about the consequences.

Through the book From the embarrassment of the Netherfield ball, through Darcy’s letter about Jane’s family, to the Brighton crisis where reputation becomes something that must be bought back.

In the text

That will do extremely well, child. You have delighted us long enough. Let the other young ladies have time to exhibit.

Mr Bennet, Chapter 18

At the Netherfield ball the Bennets are on show and Darcy is watching. His letter later gives the family’s behaviour that evening as a reason for parting Bingley from Jane.

For what do we live but to make sport for our neighbours, and laugh at them in our turn?

Mr Bennet, Chapter 57

Mr Bennet still treats the neighbours’ opinion as a game, not long after Lydia’s elopement has shown what it can cost his daughters.

Self-knowledge

Both leading characters have to change a habit of mind, which is harder than changing an opinion. Elizabeth discovers that she was flattered into belief; Darcy discovers that being brought up on good principles is not the same as being taught to apply them.

Through the book Elizabeth’s reckoning comes in Chapter 36, Darcy’s is reported in Chapter 58, and the novel makes the two confessions echo each other’s grammar.

In the text

But vanity, not love, has been my folly. Pleased with the preference of one, and offended by the neglect of the other, on the very beginning of our acquaintance, I have courted prepossession and ignorance, and driven reason away, where either were concerned. Till this moment, I never knew myself.

Elizabeth Bennet, Chapter 36

Elizabeth’s reckoning. She has read Darcy’s letter twice, and the fault she finds is in her own way of judging.

I have been a selfish being all my life, in practice, though not in principle. As a child I was taught what was right, but I was not taught to correct my temper.

Mr Darcy, Chapter 58

Darcy’s reckoning takes the same form: he was taught the right principles and did not act on them.
