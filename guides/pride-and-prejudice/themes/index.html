Pride and Prejudice · Jane Austen

Themes

First impressions and judgement

The discarded title is still the working one. Almost every character forms a verdict in a single evening and then spends the book defending it, and Austen makes the reader do the same, so the correction lands on both.

Fixed at the Meryton assembly, hardened by Wickham’s story, dismantled by a letter in Chapter 35, and formally re-examined at Pemberley.

She is tolerable; but not handsome enough to tempt me; and I am in no humour at present to give consequence to young ladies who are slighted by other men.

Mr Darcy, Chapter 3

I could easily forgive his pride, if he had not mortified mine.

Elizabeth Bennet, Chapter 5

Marriage and money

Every attachment in the novel is quoted with a figure attached, from Bingley’s four or five thousand a year to the hundred a year Mr Bennet is asked to promise for Lydia. Austen does not treat this as vulgar; she treats it as the arithmetic within which affection has to operate.

Stated as a joke in the first sentence, argued seriously by Charlotte in Chapter 22, and paid in cash by Darcy in the last volume.

It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.

Chapter 1

You mistake me, my dear. I have a high respect for your nerves. They are my old friends. I have heard you mention them with consideration these twenty years at least.

Mr Bennet, Chapter 1

Rank and connection

The obstacle between Elizabeth and Darcy is her relations more than her money: an uncle in trade, an aunt in Cheapside, a mother who cannot be taken anywhere. The novel dismantles the objection by making the Gardiners the most sensible adults in it.

Voiced by Caroline Bingley, formalised by Darcy’s first proposal, and shouted by Lady Catherine in the last volume after it has stopped being true for anyone the reader trusts.

She is tolerable; but not handsome enough to tempt me; and I am in no humour at present to give consequence to young ladies who are slighted by other men.

Mr Darcy, Chapter 3

In vain have I struggled. It will not do. My feelings will not be repressed. You must allow me to tell you how ardently I admire and love you.

Mr Darcy, Chapter 34

Entail and female security

Longbourn passes to Mr Collins, so five daughters must find husbands or find lodgings. The legal fact sits under every comic scene, and it is why Charlotte’s choice is a calculation and not a failure of feeling.

Explained early and never forgotten: it makes Mrs Bennet’s campaign rational, Collins’s proposal a favour in his own eyes, and Lydia’s ruin a threat to four other women.

An unhappy alternative is before you, Elizabeth. From this day you must be a stranger to one of your parents. Your mother will never see you again if you do not marry Mr. Collins, and I will never see you again if you do.

Mr Bennet, Chapter 20

I am not romantic you know. I never was. I ask only a comfortable home; and considering Mr. Collins’s character, connections, and situation in life, I am convinced that my chance of happiness with him is as fair, as most people can boast on entering the marriage state.

Charlotte Lucas, Chapter 22

Reputation and family conduct

Character in this novel is public property. A sister’s elopement is treated as a defect in every other sister, and Mary’s moralising about a woman’s irretrievable ruin is contemptible and, within the world of the book, accurate about the consequences.

From the embarrassment of the Netherfield ball, through Darcy’s letter about Jane’s family, to the Brighton crisis where reputation becomes something that must be bought back.

That will do extremely well, child. You have delighted us long enough. Let the other young ladies have time to exhibit.

Mr Bennet, Chapter 18

Unhappy as the event must be for Lydia, we may draw from it this useful lesson; that loss of virtue in a female is irretrievable⁠—that one false step involves her in endless ruin⁠—that her reputation is no less brittle than it is beautiful⁠—and that she cannot be too much guarded in her behaviour towards the undeserving of the other sex.

Mary Bennet, Chapter 47

Self-knowledge

Both leading characters have to change a habit of mind, which is harder than changing an opinion. Elizabeth discovers that she was flattered into belief; Darcy discovers that being brought up on good principles is not the same as being taught to apply them.

Elizabeth’s reckoning comes in Chapter 36, Darcy’s is reported in Chapter 58, and the novel makes the two confessions echo each other’s grammar.

I could easily forgive his pride, if he had not mortified mine.

Elizabeth Bennet, Chapter 5

Vanity and pride are different things, though the words are often used synonymously. A person may be proud without being vain. Pride relates more to our opinion of ourselves, vanity to what we would have others think of us.

Mary Bennet, Chapter 5
