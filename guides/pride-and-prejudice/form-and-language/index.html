Pride and Prejudice · Jane Austen

Form and language

Free indirect style

Narration that takes on a character’s vocabulary and assumptions without marking them as quotation.

The narrative sits so close to Elizabeth that her errors arrive as facts; the reader distrusts Darcy on her evidence and has to re-read the first half after the letter.

But vanity, not love, has been my folly. Pleased with the preference of one, and offended by the neglect of the other, on the very beginning of our acquaintance, I have courted prepossession and ignorance, and driven reason away, where either were concerned. Till this moment, I never knew myself.

Elizabeth Bennet, Chapter 36

Irony

Saying one thing in a form that invites the opposite judgement.

The famous first sentence states a truth nobody in the book holds and everybody acts on: it is the mothers who are in want of something, and the single men who are wanted.

It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.

Chapter 1

Letters

Written documents inserted into a narrative as evidence and as plot.

Darcy’s letter is the structural hinge, and Austen distinguishes her characters by their prose: Collins pompous, Jane over-careful, Lydia unpunctuated and untroubled.

Be not alarmed, Madam, on receiving this letter, by the apprehension of its containing any repetition of those sentiments, or renewal of those offers, which were last night so disgusting to you.

Mr Darcy, Chapter 35

Dialogue as exposure

Letting characters convict themselves in their own speech, without describing their faults.

Mr Collins numbers his reasons for marrying and Lady Catherine interrogates a stranger about her sisters’ accomplishments; neither needs a word of narrative comment.

You mistake me, my dear. I have a high respect for your nerves. They are my old friends. I have heard you mention them with consideration these twenty years at least.

Mr Bennet, Chapter 1

Epigram

A generalisation compressed into a single balanced sentence.

Characters reach for maxims when they are least reliable: Mary on vanity and pride, Charlotte on happiness in marriage, Mary again on a woman’s ruin.

It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife.

Chapter 1

Parallel courtships

Running several versions of the same situation so that each comments on the others.

Five marriages are set side by side (Jane’s, Charlotte’s, Lydia’s, Elizabeth’s and the Bennets’ own), so that the reader can see what each one was bought with.
