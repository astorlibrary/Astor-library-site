Pride and Prejudice · Jane Austen

Context

Dates

1775
Jane Austen born at Steventon The seventh child of a Hampshire rector, in a household that read aloud and wrote for each other.
1796
First Impressions drafted Written in 1796–97, in Austen’s early twenties. No manuscript of this version survives.
1797
The manuscript is offered to Thomas Cadell Austen’s father wrote to the London publisher, who declined without asking to see it. The novel waited more than a decade.
1811
Sense and Sensibility published Austen’s first published novel, issued anonymously, made a second Austen title commercially plausible.
1813
Pride and Prejudice published Thomas Egerton issued it in three volumes on 28 January, credited to the author of Sense and Sensibility. A second edition followed the same year.
1814
Mansfield Park published The first of Austen’s novels both begun and finished at Chawton, written between 1811 and 1813.
1815
Emma published Issued by John Murray with an 1816 date on the title page.
1817
Austen dies at Winchester She was forty-one. Her name had still not appeared on a title page.
1818
Northanger Abbey and Persuasion published together Issued in the last days of 1817 with 1818 on the title page, and carrying the biographical notice by her brother Henry that named her at last.
1833
Richard Bentley reprints the novels Austen enters the cheap standard-novel market, and stays in print from this point on.
1894
The Peacock edition George Allen publishes Hugh Thomson’s illustrated edition, whose gilt peacock binding became the novel’s most recognisable object.
1940
MGM’s Pride and Prejudice Directed by Robert Z. Leonard, with Greer Garson and Laurence Olivier, and costumed with cheerful disregard for the Regency.
1995
BBC serial adapted by Andrew Davies Jennifer Ehle and Colin Firth in six episodes; the version against which most later screen Darcys are measured.
2005
Joe Wright’s film Keira Knightley and Matthew Macfadyen, in a version that puts mud, livestock and noise back into Longbourn.

Places

Hertfordshire
Longbourn, Meryton and Netherfield: a neighbourhood of twenty-four families, small enough that one new tenant changes everything.
Gracechurch Street, London
The Gardiners’ house, within sight of their own warehouses: an address Caroline Bingley finds fatal and Austen finds respectable.
Kent
Hunsford parsonage and Rosings Park, where Charlotte’s bargain and Darcy’s first proposal are both inspected at close range.
Derbyshire
Pemberley and the village of Lambton, reached on a tour that Elizabeth undertakes only because the Lakes proved too far.
Brighton
The militia’s summer quarters, and the town Lydia is allowed to visit because refusing would have caused a scene.
Newcastle upon Tyne
Where Wickham’s new regiment is quartered after the marriage: far north, and paid for by Darcy.

Critics

Charlotte Brontë

Letter to G. H. Lewes (1848)

Writing on 12 January 1848, Brontë said she found in the novel “a carefully fenced, highly cultivated garden, with neat borders and delicate flowers”, but no open country and no fresh air. She could not see why Lewes ranked Austen so high.

D. W. Harding

“Regulated Hatred: An Aspect of the Work of Jane Austen”, in Scrutiny (1940)

Harding argued against the idea of a gentle, cosy Austen. Her irony, he suggested, lets her say sharply critical things about the people and society around her while remaining on terms with them.

Marilyn Butler

Jane Austen and the War of Ideas (1975)

Butler places Austen in the political argument of the 1790s and after, between radical and conservative novelists. She reads her as broadly conservative, with heroines who learn to correct their own judgement against the standards of their society.

Claudia L. Johnson

Jane Austen: Women, Politics, and the Novel (1988)

Johnson answers Butler. She argues that Austen used the forms of the conservative novel to question the authority of fathers, husbands and landowners, and that Elizabeth Bennet’s independence is part of that questioning.
