Sense and Sensibility · Jane Austen

Characters

Elinor Dashwood

The eldest Dashwood sister, nineteen; first appears Chapter 1

She is nineteen and already the family’s counsellor, which tells the reader what her mother is like. Her self-command is a duty she has taken on, and no coldness, and Austen is careful to show what it costs: four months of knowing about Lucy while listening to her sister complain that she does not feel enough.

Marianne Dashwood

The second Dashwood sister, seventeen; first appears Chapter 1

Everything but prudent, in the novel’s own phrase. Her mistake is to treat shared taste as evidence of shared character, which makes her the ideal audience for a man performing sensibility at her; feeling too much is not the problem.

Edward Ferrars

Fanny Dashwood’s brother; first appears Chapter 3

Diffident, without a profession, and held in place by an engagement he made at nineteen to a woman he no longer loves. His honour consists entirely in keeping a promise that has become a trap, which is admirable and also the reason he is nearly absent from his own love plot.

John Willoughby

Heir to Allenham, of Combe Magna in Somersetshire; first appears Chapter 9

He reads the right poets, rides the right horses and agrees with Marianne about everything, which is exactly how a fortune-hunter would behave and also how a man in love behaves. Austen’s hardest decision is giving him a midnight scene in which his remorse is entirely real and entirely useless.

Colonel Brandon

Of Delaford, thirty-five; first appears Chapter 7

Introduced as a joke about flannel waistcoats and old age, and revealed to be the only man in the book who has ever acted at real cost. The story of the two Elizas gives the novel its darker arithmetic: what happens to a woman with feeling and no protection.

Lucy Steele

Edward’s secret fiancée; first appears Chapter 21

Uneducated, sharp and the best tactician in the book. She makes a confidence into a weapon by making Elinor the one person who cannot repeat it, and she ends the novel richer than anybody, by abandoning the engagement she spent four hundred pages defending.

Mrs Dashwood

The sisters’ mother; first appears Chapter 1

She has her second daughter’s temperament and none of a mother’s caution, which is why nobody ever asks Marianne and Willoughby whether they are engaged. Her romantic delicacy is indistinguishable, in its effects, from neglect.

John Dashwood

The sisters’ half-brother, owner of Norland; first appears Chapter 1

He means to keep his promise to his dying father and is talked out of it in one conversation without ever noticing that he has changed his mind. He is the novel’s demonstration that selfishness does not require a villain, only a wife with an argument and a husband who wants to be persuaded.

Fanny Dashwood

John Dashwood’s wife; first appears Chapter 1

She conducts the second chapter like a barrister, moving from three thousand pounds to five hundred to an annuity to nothing at all, and each step sounds more reasonable than the last. Austen gives her no soliloquy and no villainy, only impeccable argument in her own interest.

Mrs Jennings

Sir John Middleton’s mother-in-law; first appears Chapter 7

Vulgar, inquisitive and the most practically kind person in the novel. She embarrasses both sisters constantly and is also the one who sits up with Marianne when she is dying, which is Austen’s way of separating manners from goodness.
