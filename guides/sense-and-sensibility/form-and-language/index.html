Sense and Sensibility · Jane Austen

Form and language

Free indirect style

Narration that borrows a character’s idiom and assumptions without marking them as quotation.

The narration sits mostly with Elinor, so the reader is kept as ignorant as she is and as bound by her discretion; Marianne is largely observed from outside, which is why her feelings arrive as events.

possessed a strength of understanding, and coolness of judgment, which qualified her, though only nineteen, to be the counsellor of her mother

Chapter 1

Irony

Wording that leads the reader to a judgement the speaker has not made.

The second chapter is conducted entirely in reasonable-sounding speech, and Austen never once says that John Dashwood is being mean; the arithmetic says it.

people always live forever when there is an annuity to be paid them; and she is very stout and healthy, and hardly forty. An annuity is a very serious business; it comes over and over every year, and there is no getting rid of it.

Fanny Dashwood, Chapter 2

Letters

Written documents used as plot, evidence and characterisation.

Marianne writes three unanswered notes and receives one cold reply that was dictated by another woman, and the physical returning of her letters and a lock of hair makes the break final.

Paired plots

Running two versions of a situation side by side so that each measures the other.

Both sisters lose a man to a prior claim and a richer woman; one hides it for four months and one collapses in a ballroom, and the novel does not declare a winner.

Esteem him! Like him! Cold-hearted Elinor! Oh! worse than cold-hearted! Ashamed of being otherwise. Use those words again, and I will leave the room this moment.

Marianne Dashwood, Chapter 4

Embedded histories

A second, older story told by a character to explain the present one.

Brandon’s account of the two Elizas supplies the consequences the main plot is too polite to stage, and makes Willoughby a danger as well as a disappointment.

I acknowledge that her situation and her character ought to have been respected by me. I do not mean to justify myself, but at the same time cannot leave you to suppose that I have nothing to urge⁠—that because she was injured she was irreproachable, and because I was a libertine, she must be a saint.

John Willoughby, Chapter 44

Dialogue as exposure

Allowing characters to convict themselves in their own speech.

Fanny Dashwood argues her husband down from three thousand pounds to nothing in a single conversation, and every step she takes sounds more reasonable than the one before.

people always live forever when there is an annuity to be paid them; and she is very stout and healthy, and hardly forty. An annuity is a very serious business; it comes over and over every year, and there is no getting rid of it.

Fanny Dashwood, Chapter 2
