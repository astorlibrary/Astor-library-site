Sense and sensibility

The title looks like a contest, but Austen does not run one. Elinor’s judgement leaves her carrying knowledge alone; Marianne’s candour makes her legible to a man who reads her for profit. Each sister ends the book holding something she learned from the other.

Through the book Set up in Chapter 1 as a contrast of temperaments, tested through four months of silence, and settled in Chapter 46 when Marianne judges her own conduct by her sister’s standard.

In the text

possessed a strength of understanding, and coolness of judgment, which qualified her, though only nineteen, to be the counsellor of her mother

Chapter 1

A nineteen-year-old is described as her mother’s counsellor, which characterises the mother as much as the daughter. The nouns are all about control (strength, coolness, judgment), and the clause that follows insists she also has strong feelings, so the novel cannot later be read as praising numbness.

My illness has made me think. It has given me leisure and calmness for serious recollection. Long before I was enough recovered to talk, I was perfectly able to reflect. I considered the past: I saw in my own behaviour, since the beginning of our acquaintance with him last autumn, nothing but a series of imprudence towards myself, and want of kindness to others.

Marianne Dashwood, Chapter 46

The self-examination is conducted in the vocabulary of accounting (a series, a beginning, a survey of behaviour since last autumn), which is her sister’s way of thinking and has never been hers. She blames imprudence and unkindness to others, and never the strength of her feelings.

Money and inheritance

The plot is started by a will and finished by a settlement, and every important decision in between has a figure attached. Austen puts the numbers on the page (two thousand a year, five hundred pounds, a hundred a year), so that no reader can treat the feeling as separable from the income.

Through the book From the second chapter’s reduction of a promise to nothing, through Marianne’s argument about competence in Chapter 17, to the livings and settlements distributed in the last chapters.

In the text

people always live forever when there is an annuity to be paid them; and she is very stout and healthy, and hardly forty. An annuity is a very serious business; it comes over and over every year, and there is no getting rid of it.

Fanny Dashwood, Chapter 2

The objection to an annuity is that the recipients will be so inconsiderate as to survive. Notice the shift from a moral question to a bookkeeping one: ‘a very serious business’ and ‘no getting rid of it’ are the phrases of a woman describing a liability, and nobody in the room corrects her.

What have wealth or grandeur to do with happiness?

Marianne Dashwood, Chapter 17

The plot spends the next thirty chapters answering this rhetorical question in detail. Within ten lines Marianne has priced her own competence at about two thousand a year, which is roughly the income of the man she is rejecting as too old.

Secrecy and silence

Almost every crisis in the book is caused by something withheld: an engagement kept for four years, an attachment never declared, a history told too late. Silence is variously honour, weapon and cowardice, and the novel distinguishes carefully between the three.

Through the book Lucy’s disclosure in Chapter 22 loads the gun; Elinor’s four months of secrecy are detonated in Chapter 37; Brandon’s history and Willoughby’s confession arrive when they can no longer help.

In the text

We have been engaged these four years.

Lucy Steele, Chapter 22

Six words, placed after a long approach designed to make Elinor ask for them. The disclosure is an act of aggression disguised as intimacy: once Elinor holds the secret as a confidence, she is the one person in England who cannot use it.

For four months, Marianne, I have had all this hanging on my mind, without being at liberty to speak of it to a single creature; knowing that it would make you and my mother most unhappy whenever it were explained to you

Elinor Dashwood, Chapter 37

The unit is time: four months, without liberty to speak, while being told repeatedly that she does not feel enough. The sentence also explains the novel’s structure: the reader has been kept inside Elinor’s silence too, and only now learns what it weighed.

Dependence

The Dashwood women are not poor in any sense a labourer would recognise, and they have no power whatever over where they live, what they keep or whom they may marry. The novel’s interest is in that specific genteel powerlessness, which looks comfortable and forecloses everything.

Through the book Begins with the entail of Norland, continues at Barton on a relation’s charity, and reaches its sharpest point in the two Elizas, who had feeling and no protection at all.

In the text

Oh! happy house, could you know what I suffer in now viewing you from this spot, from whence perhaps I may view you no more!⁠—And you, ye well-known trees!⁠—but you will continue the same.

Marianne Dashwood, Chapter 5

She addresses a house and then a stand of trees, and neither answers, which she takes personally. The apostrophe is typical of her: feeling counts as real only when it is performed aloud, and the complaint that the trees will continue the same is a complaint that grief has no witness.

to be as solemnly engaged to him, as if the strictest legal covenant had bound us to each other.

Marianne Dashwood, Chapter 29

The comparison is to a legal instrument, which is what she does not have. A private understanding, however sincere, leaves no document and no claim; Lucy Steele’s four-year engagement is equally unwritten and she treats it as property, which is why she wins.

Performance and sincerity

Willoughby’s great advantage is that he is truly charming, so the performance of sensibility and the thing itself cannot be told apart by taste alone. Austen keeps asking what counts as evidence of character, and keeps answering: conduct over time, and nothing else.

Through the book Established in Marianne’s first conversations with Willoughby about Cowper and Scott, and undone by the letter in Chapter 29, copied out from another woman’s words.

In the text

She was sensible and clever; but eager in everything: her sorrows, her joys, could have no moderation. She was generous, amiable, interesting: she was everything but prudent.

Chapter 1

Three compliments and then the single withheld word. ‘Everything but prudent’ concedes that she lacks only one quality and structures the whole plot around its absence, and the list of generous, amiable, interesting is exactly what Willoughby will find useful.

I acknowledge that her situation and her character ought to have been respected by me. I do not mean to justify myself, but at the same time cannot leave you to suppose that I have nothing to urge⁠—that because she was injured she was irreproachable, and because I was a libertine, she must be a saint.

John Willoughby, Chapter 44

He concedes the charge and then immediately reopens it, which is the pattern of the whole confession: an apology with a defence folded inside it. ‘Because I was a libertine, she must be a saint’ is a real argument about evidence, offered by a man who needs it not to be true.

Illness and the body

Marianne’s fever has a plain cause (wet feet, twice), and it nearly kills her. Austen gives grief physical consequences, so the reckoning that follows is forced on a body before it can be reasoned out at leisure.

Through the book Wet grass at Cleveland, a putrid fever, a night when Elinor expects her sister to die, and a recovery that produces the novel’s only formal self-examination.

In the text

My illness has made me think. It has given me leisure and calmness for serious recollection. Long before I was enough recovered to talk, I was perfectly able to reflect. I considered the past: I saw in my own behaviour, since the beginning of our acquaintance with him last autumn, nothing but a series of imprudence towards myself, and want of kindness to others.

Marianne Dashwood, Chapter 46

The self-examination is conducted in the vocabulary of accounting (a series, a beginning, a survey of behaviour since last autumn), which is her sister’s way of thinking and has never been hers. She blames imprudence and unkindness to others, and never the strength of her feelings.
