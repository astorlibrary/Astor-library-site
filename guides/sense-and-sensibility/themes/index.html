Sense and Sensibility · Jane Austen

Themes

Sense and sensibility

The title looks like a contest, but Austen does not run one. Elinor’s judgement leaves her carrying knowledge alone; Marianne’s candour makes her legible to a man who reads her for profit. Each sister ends the book holding something she learned from the other.

Set up in Chapter 1 as a contrast of temperaments, tested through four months of silence, and settled in Chapter 46 when Marianne judges her own conduct by her sister’s standard.

possessed a strength of understanding, and coolness of judgment, which qualified her, though only nineteen, to be the counsellor of her mother

Chapter 1

She was sensible and clever; but eager in everything: her sorrows, her joys, could have no moderation. She was generous, amiable, interesting: she was everything but prudent.

Chapter 1

Money and inheritance

The plot is started by a will and finished by a settlement, and every important decision in between has a figure attached. Austen puts the numbers on the page (two thousand a year, five hundred pounds, a hundred a year), so that no reader can treat the feeling as separable from the income.

From the second chapter’s reduction of a promise to nothing, through Marianne’s argument about competence in Chapter 17, to the livings and settlements distributed in the last chapters.

people always live forever when there is an annuity to be paid them; and she is very stout and healthy, and hardly forty. An annuity is a very serious business; it comes over and over every year, and there is no getting rid of it.

Fanny Dashwood, Chapter 2

What have wealth or grandeur to do with happiness?

Marianne Dashwood, Chapter 17

Secrecy and silence

Almost every crisis in the book is caused by something withheld: an engagement kept for four years, an attachment never declared, a history told too late. Silence is variously honour, weapon and cowardice, and the novel distinguishes carefully between the three.

Lucy’s disclosure in Chapter 22 loads the gun; Elinor’s four months of secrecy are detonated in Chapter 37; Brandon’s history and Willoughby’s confession arrive when they can no longer help.

I think very highly of him⁠—that I greatly esteem, that I like him.

Elinor Dashwood, Chapter 4

We have been engaged these four years.

Lucy Steele, Chapter 22

Dependence

The Dashwood women are not poor in any sense a labourer would recognise, and they have no power whatever over where they live, what they keep or whom they may marry. The novel’s interest is in that specific genteel powerlessness, which looks comfortable and forecloses everything.

Begins with the entail of Norland, continues at Barton on a relation’s charity, and reaches its sharpest point in the two Elizas, who had feeling and no protection at all.

people always live forever when there is an annuity to be paid them; and she is very stout and healthy, and hardly forty. An annuity is a very serious business; it comes over and over every year, and there is no getting rid of it.

Fanny Dashwood, Chapter 2

Oh! happy house, could you know what I suffer in now viewing you from this spot, from whence perhaps I may view you no more!⁠—And you, ye well-known trees!⁠—but you will continue the same.

Marianne Dashwood, Chapter 5

Performance and sincerity

Willoughby’s great advantage is that he is truly charming, so the performance of sensibility and the thing itself cannot be told apart by taste alone. Austen keeps asking what counts as evidence of character, and keeps answering: conduct over time, and nothing else.

Established in Marianne’s first conversations with Willoughby about Cowper and Scott, and undone by the letter in Chapter 29, copied out from another woman’s words.

She was sensible and clever; but eager in everything: her sorrows, her joys, could have no moderation. She was generous, amiable, interesting: she was everything but prudent.

Chapter 1

with me a flannel waistcoat is invariably connected with aches, cramps, rheumatisms, and every species of ailment that can afflict the old and the feeble.

Marianne Dashwood, Chapter 8

Illness and the body

Marianne’s fever has a plain cause (wet feet, twice), and it nearly kills her. Austen gives grief physical consequences, so the reckoning that follows is forced on a body before it can be reasoned out at leisure.

Wet grass at Cleveland, a putrid fever, a night when Elinor expects her sister to die, and a recovery that produces the novel’s only formal self-examination.

My illness has made me think. It has given me leisure and calmness for serious recollection. Long before I was enough recovered to talk, I was perfectly able to reflect. I considered the past: I saw in my own behaviour, since the beginning of our acquaintance with him last autumn, nothing but a series of imprudence towards myself, and want of kindness to others.

Marianne Dashwood, Chapter 46
