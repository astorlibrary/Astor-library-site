The House on the Borderland · William Hope Hodgson

Characters

The Recluse

The writer of the manuscript, never named; first appears The Plain of Silence

A man who bought a house nobody would live in because it was cheap, keeps no servants, and describes himself as not superstitious on the page before the visions start. He is brave under siege, exact in his descriptions, and entirely alone with what he has seen; the diary exists because there is no one he can tell.

Pepper

His dog; first appears The Plain of Silence

The only creature the Recluse says he prefers to the rest of creation, and the one that makes the ending land. Through the siege he is the alarm and the company; in the last chapters he sleeps through the acceleration of time and is touched with a foot and falls to pieces. It is the book’s cruellest and best-judged paragraph.

Mary

His sister, and the housekeeper; first appears The Thing in the Pit

She keeps the house, asks few questions, and is kept out of the story by her brother’s decision. Hodgson uses her to measure how far the Recluse has gone: the more he sees, the less he tells the one person in the building with him.

The Swine-things

What climbs out of the pit; first appears The Thing in the Pit

Human in shape and swine in face, they come in numbers, climb walls, and are stopped only by walls and bullets. Hodgson never explains them or connects them to the cosmic material, and that is why they frighten: they stand for nothing.

The woman of the Sea of Sleep

The love the Recluse lost before the book began; first appears The Sea of Sleep

She appears only in the quiet chapters, on a still sea under a white orb, and is given no history that the reader can hold. Her leaving, forced by a darkness crossing the light, is the emotional rehearsal for the ending, where a bigger darkness takes everything else.

The editor

The voice of the frame and the sixteen footnotes; first appears Author’s Introduction

He prints the manuscript, declines to interpret it, and then cannot help himself: the footnotes offer orbital mechanics, speculation about the speed of time, and finally a note on the ink trailing off the page. The apparatus of scholarship applied to the impossible is the book’s quietest and best joke.
