Isolation

The Recluse keeps no servants, hates his neighbours, protects his sister from the facts and talks only to a dog. The diary exists because of that, and the whole book is the record of a solitude that has no outlet except paper.

Through the book He begins alone by choice, is besieged alone by necessity, and ends alone in a universe with no other living thing in it.

In the text

The peasantry, who inhabit the wilderness beyond, say that I am mad.

The Plain of Silence

He raises the charge himself, in the third line, and disposes of it by explaining that he simply will not deal with people. The manoeuvre makes him sound reliable and also tells us that the only witness we will get has a reputation.

I have one friend, a dog; yes, I would sooner have old Pepper than the rest of Creation together.

The Plain of Silence

Hodgson plants the ending here, two hundred pages early. The rest of Creation is exactly what the last chapters take away, and the reader is told at the start which part of it the Recluse would have kept.

Deep time

Hodgson takes the geological and astronomical timescales that Victorian science had made familiar and puts one man inside them, conscious, in his own study. The horror lies in a rate of change more than in any event.

Through the book Days blur into a band, years into a flicker, and by the last chapters the sun is a dead face the earth crawls across.

In the text

he literally and actually crumbled into a mouldering heap of bones and dust

The Slowing Rotation

‘Literally’ and ‘actually’ are a man insisting that he is not speaking in metaphor. It is also the moment the cosmic chapters start to hurt, because the reader was told early on what the dog was worth.

that little hill of ash

The Slowing Rotation

Four words for a friend. The diminutive is what hurts, and it is also the book’s measure of scale: against a dying sun, a life is a small heap on a study floor.

Unexplained horror

No one in the book understands what has happened. The Recluse reports, the editor annotates, and neither offers a cause for the pit, the creatures or the visions.

Through the book The footnotes get more confident as the events get less explicable, until the last one is reduced to describing the movement of a pen.

In the text

I read, and, in reading, lifted the Curtains of the Impossible that blind the mind, and looked out into the unknown.

Author’s Introduction

The book’s promise, made by the editor who found the manuscript. Capitalising the Curtains of the Impossible is Edwardian in the worst way and exactly right for a man who has just read something he cannot place.

in no particular, save in color and its enormous size, did the lonely structure vary from this house in which I live

The House in the Arena

The clause is careful, legalistic and slightly clumsy, which is how a frightened man checks a fact. What it establishes is that the house is duplicated somewhere else, which goes well beyond a haunting, and nothing in the novel ever qualifies that.

Religion

The introduction says the story carries a shadowed picture of what may be given the accepted titles of Heaven and Hell, and the arena of gods and the Sea of Sleep supply both. No religion is named and no comfort is offered.

Through the book The vision of the gods comes early and is never returned to; what the book ends with instead is physics.

In the text

an enormous amphitheatre of mountains

The House in the Arena

The scale changes without warning and the prose does not raise its voice. Hodgson’s method in the visionary chapters is to describe impossible geography in the tone of a walking guide.

it was imperative she should leave me, before the Sun of Darkness

The Noise in the Night

The phrase is hers, the only piece of vocabulary the book borrows from beyond. Hodgson never resolves the contradiction of a sun that is darkness; it simply arrives again, larger, in the final chapters.

The pit

The chasm is a real feature of the ground, measured at about a hundred and fifty feet, and it is also the way through. Hodgson gets his effects by treating the supernatural as geology.

Through the book It is a curiosity in the first chapters, a source in the middle, and by the cellar chapters it is directly under the floor.

In the text

For a couple of centuries, this house has had a reputation, a bad one

The Plain of Silence

The whole gothic inheritance, disposed of in a clause and followed immediately by the price. Hodgson is signalling that he knows the convention and is not going to spend the book on it.

It was the face of a swine.

The Thing in the Pit

Six words, a full stop and no adjective: after pages of careful approach Hodgson gives the reveal the shortest sentence available, and the plainness makes it land.

The damaged manuscript

The text arrives damaged: damp, abrupt, gapped, and finally broken off. Every uncertainty in the story can be blamed on the document, which is why the document is presented so carefully.

Through the book The frame introduces the damage, the footnotes measure it, and the last word is unfinished.

In the text

I have the queer, faint, pit-water smell of it in my nostrils now as I write

Author’s Introduction

The detail is a smell, and it belongs to the pit the story will turn on. Hodgson authenticates his document with one physical sensation that the editor cannot get rid of, which is worth more than any amount of insistence that the tale is true.

the pen has trailed away over the paper; possibly, through fright and weakness

Editor’s footnote

The book closes on a note about handwriting. After the death of the sun, the editor’s caution (‘possibly, through fright and weakness’) is the most frightening sentence in it, because it is the only one that sounds reasonable.

Lost love

The one relationship in the book is with someone lost before it starts, met again only in a vision, and lost a second time to an advancing darkness.

Through the book The Sea of Sleep chapters supply the loss that the cosmic ending then repeats on the scale of a solar system.

In the text

I have one friend, a dog; yes, I would sooner have old Pepper than the rest of Creation together.

The Plain of Silence

Hodgson plants the ending here, two hundred pages early. The rest of Creation is exactly what the last chapters take away, and the reader is told at the start which part of it the Recluse would have kept.
