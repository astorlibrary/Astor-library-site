The House on the Borderland · William Hope Hodgson

Form and language

The found manuscript

A story presented as a document discovered by someone else.

Two anglers, a ruin and a damp book. The frame lets the second half abandon plot entirely: what we are reading is a record, and records can stop.

I read, and, in reading, lifted the Curtains of the Impossible that blind the mind, and looked out into the unknown.

Author’s Introduction

Withheld explanation

Withholding the causes of the events described.

Why the house, why the pit, what the swine-things are, who the gods in the arena were: none of it is answered, and nothing in the book behaves as though an answer is owed.

It was the face of a swine.

The Thing in the Pit

Precise geography

Description built out of distances, depths and materials.

The pit is about a hundred and fifty feet deep, the garden has a wall, the cellar has a slab. The cosmic chapters work because the domestic ones were surveyed first.

an enormous amphitheatre of mountains

The House in the Arena

Symbolic objects

A single object carrying the book’s meaning without being glossed.

The same house appears in green jade at the centre of an arena of gods, identical in every particular but colour and scale. Hodgson never says what that means and never needs to.

a stupendous structure built apparently of green jade

The House in the Arena

Journal form

A first-person record composed after the events, under pressure.

The prose is stiff and abrupt, as the editor warns, and the stiffness is doing work: a man writing this fast has no time to make it literary.

The peasantry, who inhabit the wilderness beyond, say that I am mad.

The Plain of Silence

The siege

An extended action sequence in a fixed location.

Doors, windows, ammunition, a cellar trap: the middle third is a practical defence story, and the practicality is what keeps the reader for the unreadable wonders on either side of it.

Haunted-house conventions

An isolated building with a history, bought cheap by a man who should know better.

Hodgson sets up the full apparatus in two paragraphs (eighty years empty, a bad name, a ridiculous price) and then spends the novel on things the gothic has no vocabulary for.

For a couple of centuries, this house has had a reputation, a bad one
