The Overcoat · Nikolai Gogol

Form and language

The intruding narrator

A narrator who interrupts the story to comment, digress or talk about the telling.

He declines to name the department in case officials take offence, admits his memory is failing, stops to tell anecdotes about a council-chamber in a cupboard of an office, and closes Akaki’s life with a shrug about who got his things. The interruptions keep the reader at a comic distance from the clerk, which makes the few moments of plain pity stand out.

But it is perhaps better that I do not mention which department it was. There are in the whole of Russia no persons more sensitive than Government officials.

The opening

Skaz

Russian term for narration written to sound like a particular speaker telling the story aloud, with his own habits, errors and asides.

Gogol’s teller hedges (if I am not mistaken), loses his thread and gives small things grand words. Boris Eikhenbaum used The Overcoat in 1919 to show how such a voice can decide the shape of a whole story. In Field’s English the effect survives in the asides and the mock-solemn phrasing; the puns and sound-play that depend on Russian mostly do not.

The comic grotesque

Exaggerated, often physical detail that makes a person or thing ridiculous and strange together.

A neck stretched like the toy cats with movable heads sold on trays in villages, a tailor’s thumbnail as hard as a tortoise shell, a general on a snuff-box whose face has been patched with paper, orange peel on a hat. The details belittle, and Gogol’s reader has to look for the man behind them.

Such was the outer aspect of our hero, as produced by the St Petersburg climate.

The opening

Symbolic names

Names chosen for their meaning or their sound.

Akaki is given his father’s name twice over after a page of real but outlandish saints’ names from the church calendar. The name goes back to a Greek word for guileless, and the surname Bashmatchkin comes from the Russian for a shoe, a joke Field’s translation leaves out. A man who is given nothing of his own is not even given a name of his own.

It was baptised, although it wept and cried and made all kinds of grimaces, as though it had a presentiment that it would one day be a titular councillor.

The christening

Irony

Saying one thing in words whose real meaning is different, or letting events contradict what characters expect.

The thief shouts That is my cloak!; the important personage scolds a man in his fifties for the rebelliousness of the young; the collection for the robbed man fails because the office has just paid for its director’s portrait; the man best placed to help is the one busiest impressing his friend.

But it is perhaps better that I do not mention which department it was. There are in the whole of Russia no persons more sensitive than Government officials.

The opening

Shifts of register

Movement between different levels or kinds of language, such as the comic and the solemn.

Without warning the story moves from farce to pathos and back: the paper-snowflake joke is followed by the young clerk’s shame, the doctor’s pine coffin by a sentence about the city going on as though Akaki had never existed. Readers disagree about which register has the last word.

Do leave me alone! Why do you always want to disturb me at work?

Akaki Akakievitch, The office

Comic repetition

A word, phrase or action repeated until it becomes a joke or a mark of character.

“Severity” said three times in succession; “Do you consider before whom you are standing?” asked and asked again; Akaki’s “Yes—it is indeed true that—”, which never reaches its end. The important personage’s catch-phrase returns after the ghost as the thing he no longer says.

His system might be summed up in a single word—“severity.”

The important personage

Inventory

A list of things that stands in for a description of a person or a life.

Akaki’s estate is a bundle of goose-quills, a notebook of white paper, three pairs of socks, some trouser buttons and the old coat. The ghost takes cloaks lined with wadding, mink, cat, otter, bear or beaverskin. The first list is the whole of one life; the second sorts the city by the fur it can afford.

He had no heir and left nothing behind him but a bundle of goose-quills, a notebook of white paper, three pairs of socks, some trouser buttons, and his old coat.

Akaki’s death

The open ending

An ending that leaves the central question unanswered.

The ghost stops appearing once it has the important personage’s cloak, which reads like a moral ending, and then a taller figure with a moustache is seen walking off into the dark. The last paragraph points back to the thieves and away from any settled meaning.

This shadow, however, was taller than the ghost of the titular councillor, and had an enormous moustache.
