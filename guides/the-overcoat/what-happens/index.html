The Overcoat · Nikolai Gogol

What happens

The copyist A clerk, his name and his desk

The narrator will not name the department, describes a short, pock-marked, balding titular councillor, and explains how he came to be called Akaki Akakievitch. Nobody in the office notices him except to tease him. He loves copying, cannot manage any other task, copies at home for pleasure and asks nothing more of life than four hundred roubles a year.

The opening
Touchy officials, a provincial magistrate’s complaint about a novel, and a hero produced by the Petersburg climate.
The christening
Mokuja, Triphiliy, Pavsikakhiy: his mother gives up on the saints and names him after his father.
The office
The clerks’ jokes, his one protest, and the young clerk who hears it for the rest of his life.
Copying
His favourite letters, the report he cannot redraft, his faded uniform and his evenings at home.

The old cloak Petrovitch’s verdict

The winter cold finds the holes in his cloak, which the office calls a cowl. Petrovitch the tailor, sober and therefore stubborn, will not patch it again and names a price of a hundred and fifty roubles. A Sunday visit and a ten-kopeck piece change nothing. Akaki gives up tea, supper and the fire in his own room to save, the thought of the new cloak becomes his companion, and an unusually large bonus brings the day forward.

The cold
Four hundred roubles a year against a Petersburg winter, and a cloak worn so thin that the light shows through.
At Petrovitch’s
Fish frying, a snuff-box general with a paper face, and the verdict that the old cloak is a wretched rag.
Saving for the cloak
Forty roubles already saved a kopeck at a time, the rest found by going hungry, and monthly visits to talk over the cloth.
The cloth
A bonus of sixty-five roubles, good cloth, a glossy linen lining, and catskin in place of marten.

The new cloak One day in a new cloak

Petrovitch delivers the cloak on a frosty morning. The office crowds round to admire it and demands a celebration, and a superior with a birthday invites them all to tea instead. Akaki walks to the fine quarter, sits out the card games, drinks two glasses of champagne and slips away after midnight. Crossing an enormous empty square on the way home he is knocked down and robbed, and the sentry says he thought the men were his friends.

The new cloak
The tailor lays it on his shoulders, follows him into the street, and the whole office comes to look.
The party
Galoshes in the hall, whist, cold veal and champagne, and the cloak found lying on the floor.
The square
A long dark street, a square like a desert, men with long moustaches, and a sentry who saw nothing wrong.

The petition Two officials and a fever

His landlady sends him to the district superintendent, who wants to know why he was out so late. The office takes up a collection that raises almost nothing. On a colleague’s advice he goes to an important personage, who keeps him waiting to impress an old friend and then shouts at him until he has to be dragged out. Akaki walks home through a storm, catches a fever and dies within days; the office learns of it four days after the funeral.

The district superintendent
Three calls before he is admitted, and questions about suspicious company where there should be questions about the theft.
The important personage
A new official, a visiting friend, a practised stern voice, and a petitioner carried half-conscious down the stairs.
Akaki’s death
Delirium, a doctor who prices the coffin, an estate of a few belongings, and a successor of robuster fibre.

The ghost The ghost at the Katinka Bridge

A dead clerk begins pulling cloaks off passers-by near the Katinka Bridge; the police catch him once and lose him in a sneeze. The important personage, who has felt a pang on hearing of Akaki’s death, is seized by the collar on his way home from a party and hands over his own cloak. After that the ghost is not seen, unless it is the taller shadow with an enormous moustache that a nervous sentry follows towards the Obuchoff Bridge.

The Katinka Bridge
Cloaks of every fur taken without regard to rank, colds in honourable backs, and a sentry’s snuff.
The ghost
A pleasant evening, two glasses of champagne, a hand on the collar in the sledge, and a changed manner afterwards.
The last paragraph
A sentry follows a figure with an enormous moustache, is shouted at, and lets it go.
