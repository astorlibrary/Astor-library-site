Rank

The narrator gives Akaki’s grade before his name, because in Russia, he says, rank must always be given. Everyone in the story knows where they stand. The clerks mock the man below them, the important personage performs severity for the men below him, and the titular councillor who calls his tiny office a Council-chamber copies the manners of his betters. The only figure who ignores the ladder is a ghost, who strips cloaks from clerks and state councillors alike.

Through the book A permanent titular councillor on the first page; porters who do not stand up for him; a question put three times, Do you know with whom you are speaking?; a ghost who pays no attention to rank or title.

In the text

But it is perhaps better that I do not mention which department it was. There are in the whole of Russia no persons more sensitive than Government officials.

The opening

Before the story has properly begun, the narrator loses his nerve. He stops mid-sentence to avoid offending the class the story is about to expose, and his joke about touchy officials, one of whom sent in a whole novel as evidence of an insult, is also a joke about censorship under Nicholas I. From the first lines the reader knows this teller is timid, chatty and not quite in charge of his material.

Instead of confining himself to the principal matter—the theft, he asked the titular councillor how he came to be out so late, and whether he had not been in suspicious company.

The district superintendent

In this office the victim is treated as the suspect. It is the first of Akaki’s two interviews with authority and it prepares the second, in which the official again cares about procedure and respectability and puts his questions to the petitioner. He leaves not knowing whether anything will be done, and the cloak is never found.

The cloak

Clothes show rank in this story, and a cloak is the nearest thing Akaki has to a companion. The old one has been patched with pieces of its own collar until the office calls it a cowl; the new one has catskin where the cloaks of better men have marten; the cloaks in a superior’s hall hang in rows with fur collars. The narrator compares the thought of the new cloak to a wife. Losing it kills Akaki, and as a ghost his only demand is for a cloak.

Through the book Holes you can see through; a hundred and fifty roubles quoted, eighty found; twelve roubles for the tailoring; one day of wearing it; then furs of every kind taken near the Katinka Bridge.

In the text

The collar was so narrow and so tight that his neck, although of average length, stretched far out of it, and appeared extraordinarily long, just like those of the cats with movable heads, which are carried about on trays and sold to the peasants in Russian villages.

Copying

With this simile he shrinks to a cheap toy, the nodding cat on a village pedlar’s tray. Gogol builds the portrait from clothes: the uniform has changed colour, the collar pinches, rubbish sticks to his hat. Before the cloak enters the story the reader already knows that in this world a man is seen through what he wears.

“That is my cloak!” shouted one of the men, and seized him by the collar.

The thieves, The square

Whose cloak is it? The thief claims it, and in a sense everyone has: the office wanted it celebrated, the party wanted it admired, and now the street takes it. The words make ownership a matter of who shouts first. They return at the end, spoken the other way round, when Akaki’s ghost seizes another man by the collar and demands his cloak.

Copying

Akaki’s happiness is copying. He has favourite letters, smiles when he reaches them, takes documents home and copies letters to important people for his own satisfaction. Offered something more responsible, turning a report from the first person into the third, he sweats and begs to go back. Gogol makes the lowest work in the office a kind of art and the whole of a man’s life, and when Akaki dies his place is taken the next day by an official who does not bother with so many fair copies.

Through the book Copying at the office, copying at home, copying at the landlady’s table to save fuel; a slip of the pen when the cloak fills his head; a bundle of goose-quills left behind.

In the text

While he copied official documents, a world of varied beauty rose before his eyes.

Copying

Nobody else in the story loves anything the way Akaki loves the shapes of letters. He has favourites, smiles when he reaches them, and his face shows which letter he is forming. The sentence is half a joke about a clerk’s narrow pleasures and half a portrait of an artist, and it is the happiest thing the narrator ever says about him.

He was fully conscious of his dignity, and of the gulf which separates the tailor who only repairs old clothes from the artist who makes new ones.

The new cloak

Petrovitch has his own Table of Ranks, and today he has been promoted. The mock-solemn word artist is only partly a joke. He has made something fine, he charges less than a Nevski Prospect tailor would, and he follows Akaki down the street to watch it being worn. Gogol gives the craftsman the one uncomplicated pride in the story.

Pity and mockery

The office laughs at Akaki and the narrator often laughs with it. One paragraph goes the other way: a young clerk hears in Akaki’s voice the words Am I not your brother? and hides his face at the memory years later. Critics in the 1840s read it as a plea for the poor clerk, and Dostoevsky’s first novel answered it in those terms; in 1919 Boris Eikhenbaum argued that the humane paragraph is one more voice in a comic performance. Both responses can be supported from the text.

Through the book Paper snowflakes; the young clerk’s memory; a collection that raises almost nothing; some colleagues laughing and most sorry; the important personage’s pang of conscience.

In the text

Sometimes they let a shower of scraps of paper fall on his head, and told him they were snowflakes.

The office

Childish and cruel, the joke is made of paper, the material of his whole working life. It also brings winter indoors. Real Petersburg snow will soon matter more to Akaki than anything in the office, and the story’s weather begins here as a prank.

From that time it seemed as though his nature had completed itself; as though he had married and possessed a companion on his life journey. This companion was the thought of his new cloak, properly wadded and lined.

Saving for the cloak

Gogol gives Akaki a marriage and makes the bride the idea of a coat. It is the story’s central image, and the sentence is funny because it is so exactly observed: the saving, the planning and the monthly visits to Petrovitch really are a courtship. The loss that follows is a bereavement, and the story treats it as one.

Money

Every sum is exact. A salary of four hundred roubles; a Christmas bonus of forty or fifty in an ordinary year and sixty-five in this one; a kopeck put by from every rouble until the box holds forty; eighty roubles in the end, and a charge of twelve for the tailoring. Akaki gives up evening tea, supper and his own fire to find the difference, and walks carefully to save his shoes. The price of one coat is the plot, and the careful accounting is part of the comedy and part of the sorrow.

Through the book Four hundred roubles on the first page of the misfortune; a hundred and fifty named in the attic; the kopeck box; the bonus; a doctor who orders pine because oak would be too dear.

In the text

In St Petersburg all those who draw a salary of four hundred roubles or thereabouts have a terrible enemy in our northern cold, although some assert that it is very good for the health.

The cold

Cold is sorted by income here. A salary figure opens the sentence as if the weather read pay slips, and the last clause, about the cold being healthy, is what comfortable people say. From here the story is about the arithmetic of keeping warm on four hundred roubles a year.

“A hundred and fifty roubles for a cloak!” exclaimed Akaki Akakievitch in a tone which sounded like an outcry—possibly the first he had uttered since his birth.

Akaki Akakievitch, At Petrovitch’s

A man whose sentences usually dissolve into particles finally raises his voice, and the subject is money. The narrator’s aside, possibly the first cry since his birth, makes the moment comic and sad together. The figure explains the shout: it is more than a third of his salary for a year.

St Petersburg

Petersburg is a climate first. The narrator says Akaki’s face was produced by it, the northern cold is every poor clerk’s enemy, and the wind in the narrow streets gives him the illness that kills him. It is also a map of rank, from staircases reeking of spirits to lamp-lit streets full of lacquered sleighs and bearskins, and at night it becomes a desert of a square with a sentry-box at the end of the world. When Akaki dies, the great city carries on as though he had never existed.

Through the book A grey northern sky; tailors on the Nevski Prospect who would charge sixty-five roubles; the smart quarter where the superior lives; the Katinka and Obuchoff bridges, both real bridges over the Fontanka.

In the text

Such was the outer aspect of our hero, as produced by the St Petersburg climate.

The opening

Gogol blames the weather for the man. The line is a comic shrug, but it sets up the logic of the whole story: the northern cold wears out the old cloak, makes a new one necessary, and finally sends the wind that gives Akaki his fatal fever. The city makes him and then disposes of him.

At a great distance—God knows where!—glimmered the light in a sentry-box, which seemed to stand at the end of the world.

The square

Here the comic city of offices and tailors gives way to emptiness. The sentry-box, where help ought to be, is so far off that it seems to belong to another world, and the sentry says a moment later that he saw the men and took them for friends. Akaki keeps his eyes on the ground so as not to see the expanse, and when he raises them the thieves are there.

The ghost

In the last pages the story becomes a Petersburg legend. A dead clerk pulls cloaks off passers-by near the Katinka Bridge, escapes arrest in a sneeze, and stops only when he has the important personage’s cloak. The narrator calls his tale fantastic yet veracious, and then reopens his own ending with a second, taller ghost wearing a moustache. The ghost can be read as justice, as a joke about rumour, or as the city’s bad conscience, and the text leaves room for each.

Through the book A rumour, a sighting by a former colleague, a sentry’s snuff, the personage’s collar seized in his sledge, and a shadow walking towards the Obuchoff Bridge.

In the text

It mattered not whether they were lined with wadding, mink, cat, otter, bear, or beaverskin; he took all he could get hold of.

The Katinka Bridge

Each lining in the list belongs to a rank, from the wadding a clerk might afford to the beaver of a senior man, and the ghost treats them alike. The clerk who could not stretch to marten now takes everyone’s collar, and state councillors catch colds in their honourable backs. The revenge is levelling, and it is also a joke about the city’s appetite for rumour.

At last I have you—by the collar! I need your cloak. You did not trouble about me when I was in distress; you thought it necessary to reprimand me. Now give me your cloak.

Akaki Akakievitch, The ghost

Dead, the man who could never finish a sentence speaks in complete, accusing ones. The words repeat the thief’s act in the square, the grip on the collar, and they make the charge the living Akaki never could: you did not trouble about me. The important personage hands over his cloak with a trembling hand and tells the coachman to drive home.
