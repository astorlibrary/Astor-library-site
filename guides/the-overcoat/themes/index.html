The Overcoat · Nikolai Gogol

Themes

Rank

The narrator gives Akaki’s grade before his name, because in Russia, he says, rank must always be given. Everyone in the story knows where they stand. The clerks mock the man below them, the important personage performs severity for the men below him, and the titular councillor who calls his tiny office a Council-chamber copies the manners of his betters. The only figure who ignores the ladder is a ghost, who strips cloaks from clerks and state councillors alike.

A permanent titular councillor on the first page; porters who do not stand up for him; a question put three times, Do you know with whom you are speaking?; a ghost who pays no attention to rank or title.

But it is perhaps better that I do not mention which department it was. There are in the whole of Russia no persons more sensitive than Government officials.

The opening

As regards his official rank—for with us Russians the official rank must always be given—he was what is usually known as a permanent titular councillor

The opening

The cloak

Clothes show rank in this story, and a cloak is the nearest thing Akaki has to a companion. The old one has been patched with pieces of its own collar until the office calls it a cowl; the new one has catskin where the cloaks of better men have marten; the cloaks in a superior’s hall hang in rows with fur collars. The narrator compares the thought of the new cloak to a wife. Losing it kills Akaki, and as a ghost his only demand is for a cloak.

Holes you can see through; a hundred and fifty roubles quoted, eighty found; twelve roubles for the tailoring; one day of wearing it; then furs of every kind taken near the Katinka Bridge.

The collar was so narrow and so tight that his neck, although of average length, stretched far out of it, and appeared extraordinarily long, just like those of the cats with movable heads, which are carried about on trays and sold to the peasants in Russian villages.

No, it won’t bear anything more; cloth after all is only cloth, and in its present condition a gust of wind might blow the wretched mantle into tatters.

Copying

Akaki’s happiness is copying. He has favourite letters, smiles when he reaches them, takes documents home and copies letters to important people for his own satisfaction. Offered something more responsible, turning a report from the first person into the third, he sweats and begs to go back. Gogol makes the lowest work in the office a kind of art and the whole of a man’s life, and when Akaki dies his place is taken the next day by an official who does not bother with so many fair copies.

Copying at the office, copying at home, copying at the landlady’s table to save fuel; a slip of the pen when the cloak fills his head; a bundle of goose-quills left behind.

Do leave me alone! Why do you always want to disturb me at work?

Akaki Akakievitch, The office

While he copied official documents, a world of varied beauty rose before his eyes.

Pity and mockery

The office laughs at Akaki and the narrator often laughs with it. One paragraph goes the other way: a young clerk hears in Akaki’s voice the words Am I not your brother? and hides his face at the memory years later. Readers have divided over which of these the story means. Critics in the 1840s read it as a plea for the poor clerk, and Dostoevsky’s first novel answered it in those terms; in 1919 Boris Eikhenbaum argued that the humane paragraph is one more voice in a comic performance. Both responses can be supported from the text.

Paper snowflakes; the young clerk’s memory; a collection that raises almost nothing; some colleagues laughing and most sorry; the important personage’s pang of conscience.

Even the office attendants did not rise from their seats when he entered, nor look at him; they took no more notice than if a fly had flown through the room.

The office

Sometimes they let a shower of scraps of paper fall on his head, and told him they were snowflakes.

The office

Money

Every sum is exact. A salary of four hundred roubles; a Christmas bonus of forty or fifty in an ordinary year and sixty-five in this one; a kopeck put by from every rouble until the box holds forty; eighty roubles in the end, and a charge of twelve for the tailoring. Akaki gives up evening tea, supper and his own fire to find the difference, and walks carefully to save his shoes. The price of one coat is the plot, and the careful accounting is part of the comedy and part of the sorrow.

Four hundred roubles on the first page of the misfortune; a hundred and fifty named in the attic; the kopeck box; the bonus; a doctor who orders pine because oak would be too dear.

In St Petersburg all those who draw a salary of four hundred roubles or thereabouts have a terrible enemy in our northern cold, although some assert that it is very good for the health.

The cold

“A hundred and fifty roubles for a cloak!” exclaimed Akaki Akakievitch in a tone which sounded like an outcry—possibly the first he had uttered since his birth.

Akaki Akakievitch, At Petrovitch’s

St Petersburg

Petersburg is a climate first. The narrator says Akaki’s face was produced by it, the northern cold is every poor clerk’s enemy, and the wind in the narrow streets gives him the illness that kills him. It is also a map of rank, from staircases reeking of spirits to lamp-lit streets full of lacquered sleighs and bearskins, and at night it becomes a desert of a square with a sentry-box at the end of the world. When Akaki dies, the great city carries on as though he had never existed.

A grey northern sky; tailors on the Nevski Prospect who would charge sixty-five roubles; the smart quarter where the superior lives; the Katinka and Obuchoff bridges, both real bridges over the Fontanka.

Such was the outer aspect of our hero, as produced by the St Petersburg climate.

The ghost

In the last pages the story becomes a Petersburg legend. A dead clerk pulls cloaks off passers-by near the Katinka Bridge, escapes arrest in a sneeze, and stops only when he has the important personage’s cloak. The narrator calls his tale fantastic yet veracious, and then reopens his own ending with a second, taller ghost wearing a moustache. The ghost can be read as justice, as a joke about rumour, or as the city’s bad conscience, and the text leaves room for each.

A rumour, a sighting by a former colleague, a sentry’s snuff, the personage’s collar seized in his sledge, and a shadow walking towards the Obuchoff Bridge.

It mattered not whether they were lined with wadding, mink, cat, otter, bear, or beaverskin; he took all he could get hold of.

The Katinka Bridge

At last I have you—by the collar! I need your cloak. You did not trouble about me when I was in distress; you thought it necessary to reprimand me. Now give me your cloak.
