The Overcoat · Nikolai Gogol

Context

Dates

1722
Peter the Great introduces the Table of Ranks Fourteen grades each for the army, the navy and the civil service, with promotion meant to depend on service. A titular councillor stood in the ninth civil grade.
1809
Nikolai Gogol is born at Sorochyntsi, in the Poltava province of the Russian Empire, now Ukraine He grew up on his family’s small estate and went to school at Nizhyn.
1829
Gogol takes a clerk’s post in St Petersburg Until 1831 he served in minor posts in the Department of State Economy and Public Buildings and then the Department of Appanages, the world of the copying clerk.
1834
Around this time Gogol hears the story of a clerk and his gun As P. V. Annenkov recalled it, a poor clerk saved for months to buy a fine gun and lost it from his boat on his first day’s shooting; he fell ill with grief, and his colleagues bought him another.
1835
Arabesques includes Nevsky Prospect, The Portrait and Diary of a Madman The first of the stories later grouped as Gogol’s Petersburg tales.
1836
The Nose appears, The Government Inspector is staged, and Gogol leaves Russia He spent most of the next twelve years abroad, much of it in Rome.
1839
Pogodin takes down a first draft at Marienbad Gogol dictated it in July under the working title A Tale of a Clerk Who Steals Overcoats.
1842
The Overcoat is printed in the third volume of Gogol’s Works The volume was printed at the end of the year and went on sale in January 1843 after a delay at the censor. The first part of Dead Souls had appeared that spring.
1846
Dostoevsky’s Poor Folk In his first novel the poor clerk Makar Devushkin reads The Overcoat and is hurt by it, because he recognises himself.
1852
Gogol dies in Moscow On 4 March 1852, 21 February in the Russian calendar of the time, days after burning the manuscript of the second part of Dead Souls.
1886
Vogüé prints the saying about Gogol’s overcoat In Le Roman russe the French critic Eugène-Melchior de Vogüé credits an unnamed Russian with the words We have all come out of Gogol’s Overcoat. It is often given to Dostoevsky, without evidence.
1916
Claud Field’s translation, The Mantle and Other Stories Published in London by T. Werner Laurie and in New York by Frederick A. Stokes, with The Nose, Memoirs of a Madman, A May Night and The Viy.
1919
Boris Eikhenbaum’s essay How Gogol’s Overcoat Is Made A founding text of Russian Formalist criticism, which reads the story through its narrator’s voice.
1926
Grigori Kozintsev and Leonid Trauberg film The Overcoat A silent film made in Leningrad, with a script by Yury Tynyanov that draws on Nevsky Prospect as well.

Places

Nevsky Prospect, St Petersburg
The city’s main street, where, Petrovitch says, a tailor would have charged at least sixty-five roubles for the same work.
Kalinkin Bridge, St Petersburg
Field’s Katinka Bridge over the Fontanka, near which the ghost first starts taking cloaks.
Obukhov Bridge, St Petersburg
Field’s Obuchoff Bridge, towards which the last shadow with the enormous moustache strides off.
Marienbad (Mariánské Lázně)
The spa town where Gogol stayed with Mikhail Pogodin in the summer of 1839; the opening of the earliest draft is in Pogodin’s handwriting.
Via Sistina, Rome
Gogol lodged here during his Roman years, when much of the story was written.

Critics

Boris Eichenbaum

“How Gogol’s “Overcoat” Is Made”, in Poetika (1919)

Eichenbaum argued that the story is made by its narrator’s voice, its puns, digressions and sudden changes of tone, not by its plot. Even the famous passage of pity for Akaky is one more effect of style.

Vladimir Nabokov

Nikolai Gogol (1944)

Nabokov rejected the reading of the story as a plea for the downtrodden clerk. For him it is a grotesque, a glimpse of an irrational world behind the ordinary one, and its power lies in its sentences.
