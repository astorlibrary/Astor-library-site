King John · William Shakespeare

Themes

Right and possession

A man who holds the crown is set against a boy who has the better descent, and Shakespeare does not decide between them. Eleanor’s whisper in the first scene, that possession is doing more work than right, is the nearest thing to a ruling anyone gives.

John answers France with war in 1.1; Angers opens its gates to neither king in 2.1; John has himself crowned a second time in 4.2; and the crown reaches Prince Henry in 5.7 with the question still open.

A strange beginning: 'borrow'd majesty'!

Queen Eleanor, 1.1.5

Your strong possession much more than your right, Or else it must go wrong with you and me: So much my conscience whispers in your ear, Which none but heaven and you and I shall hear.

Queen Eleanor, 1.1.40

Commodity

The Bastard’s word for the advantage that changes men’s minds. Watching two armies make peace by marriage at Angers, he gives the thing a face, a manner and a career, and then says he will worship it himself, on the grounds that nobody has offered him any yet.

Named in the soliloquy that closes 2.1, priced by Pandulph in 3.1 when a curse is weighed against a league, and dropped without comment by the Bastard once Arthur is dead.

Sweet, sweet, sweet poison for the age's tooth, Which, though I will not practise to deceive, Yet, to avoid deceit, I mean to learn;

The Bastard, 1.1.213

That smooth-fac'd gentleman, tickling commodity, Commodity, the bias of the world,

The Bastard, 2.1.577

Rome and the crown

Pandulph comes to ask why Stephen Langton is being kept out of Canterbury and leaves having started a war. John’s answer, that no Italian priest will tithe in his dominions, is the loudest thing he says in the play, and he takes every word of it back in the first scene of Act V.

Excommunication in 3.1, the legate coaching the Dauphin in 3.4, the crown handed over and handed back on Ascension Day in 5.1, and a monk’s poison at Swinstead.

Since kings break faith upon commodity, Gain, be my lord, for I will worship thee!

The Bastard, 2.1.601

no Italian priest Shall tithe or toll in our dominions; But as we, under heaven, are supreme head, So under Him that great supremacy, Where we do reign, we will alone uphold

King John, 3.1.153

Loyalty

The English lords serve John until they find Arthur’s body, then swear themselves to a French prince on an altar at Bury, and come back when a dying Frenchman tells them what Louis means to do with them afterwards. Nobody in this play changes sides for nothing.

Pembroke asks for Arthur’s release in 4.2, the lords kneel over his body in 4.3, they take the sacrament with Louis in 5.2, and Melun turns them in 5.4.

That can we not; but he that proves the King, To him will we prove loyal. Till that time Have we ramm'd up our gates against the world.

A Citizen of Angiers, 2.1.271

Whoever wins, on that side shall I lose; Assured loss before the match be play'd.

Blanche, 3.1.337

Inheritance

The first scene brings on two brothers arguing about five hundred pounds a year and closes with a nine-year-old taking a kingdom. In between, land moves by a dying man’s will, by marriage settlement, by conquest, and by the death of the boy who stood next in line.

Robert Faulconbridge’s suit in 1.1, the five provinces given with Blanche in 2.1, Louis claiming through his wife in 3.4, and Prince Henry succeeding in 5.7.

Your strong possession much more than your right, Or else it must go wrong with you and me: So much my conscience whispers in your ear, Which none but heaven and you and I shall hear.

Queen Eleanor, 1.1.40

And have is have, however men do catch. Near or far off, well won is still well shot, And I am I, howe'er I was begot.

The Bastard, 1.1.173

Grief

Constance will not let her loss be folded into a settlement, and she is given three long scenes in which to say so. Kings ask her to bind up her hair, a cardinal tells her she is uttering madness, and she answers both of them before dying offstage between scenes.

She sits on the ground in 3.1, unbinds her hair in 3.4, and is reported dead in a messenger’s speech in 4.2, three days before Eleanor.

I will instruct my sorrows to be proud, For grief is proud and makes his owner stoop. … Here I and sorrows sit; Here is my throne, bid kings come bow to it.

Constance, 3.1.68

Whoever wins, on that side shall I lose; Assured loss before the match be play'd.

Blanche, 3.1.337

Speech and rhetoric

Two kings argue their titles to a town wall, a citizen answers both of them with a marriage proposal, a cardinal unpicks an oath by means of another oath, and a bastard heckles the lot of them. The battles in this play settle little, and the real turns come in speeches.

The rival addresses to Angers in 2.1, Pandulph’s argument about vows in 3.1, John’s roundabout order for a murder in 3.3 and his attempt to disown it in 4.2, and the Bastard’s defiance of Louis in 5.2.
