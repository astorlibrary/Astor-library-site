Right and possession

A man who holds the crown is set against a boy who has the better descent, and Shakespeare does not decide between them. Eleanor’s whisper in the first scene, that possession is doing more work than right, is the nearest thing to a ruling anyone gives.

Through the play John answers France with war in 1.1; Angers opens its gates to neither king in 2.1; John has himself crowned a second time in 4.2; and the crown reaches Prince Henry in 5.7 with the question still open.

In the text

A strange beginning: ‘borrow’d majesty’!

Queen Eleanor, 1.1.5

She objects to the adjective and leaves the noun alone, and by objecting she repeats it. ‘Borrow’d’ implies an owner somewhere else and a term that will expire, which is the charge, and Eleanor has put it into the air of the English court herself before anyone has had time to answer it.

That can we not; but he that proves the King, To him will we prove loyal. Till that time Have we ramm’d up our gates against the world.

A Citizen of Angiers, 2.1.271

The town will obey whoever can demonstrate kingship, and declines to say what a demonstration would look like. It is the most damaging answer either king receives, because it treats the crown as a claim requiring evidence, and neither of the men outside the wall can produce any that the other cannot match.

Commodity

The Bastard’s word for the advantage that changes men’s minds. Watching two armies make peace by marriage at Angers, he gives the thing a face, a manner and a career, and then says he will worship it himself, on the grounds that nobody has offered him any yet.

Through the play Named in the soliloquy that closes 2.1, priced by Pandulph in 3.1 when a curse is weighed against a league, and dropped without comment by the Bastard once Arthur is dead.

In the text

Sweet, sweet, sweet poison for the age’s tooth, Which, though I will not practise to deceive, Yet, to avoid deceit, I mean to learn;

The Bastard, 1.1.213

The triple ‘sweet’ makes flattery taste of sugar before the word ‘poison’ arrives, and the excuse that follows is the most honest thing in the speech: he will study deceit in order to spot it. That distinction will be tested at Angers, where he understands every lie in the room and joins the transaction anyway.

That smooth-fac’d gentleman, tickling commodity, Commodity, the bias of the world,

The Bastard, 2.1.577

The word is given a face and a touch before it is given a definition. ‘Bias’ is the weight in a bowls ball that makes it curve away from the line it was sent along, so the metaphor says the ground itself is tilted, and a straight throw arrives somewhere else; it does not say men are wicked.

Rome and the crown

Pandulph comes to ask why Stephen Langton is being kept out of Canterbury and leaves having started a war. John’s answer, that no Italian priest will tithe in his dominions, is the loudest thing he says in the play, and he takes every word of it back in the first scene of Act V.

Through the play Excommunication in 3.1, the legate coaching the Dauphin in 3.4, the crown handed over and handed back on Ascension Day in 5.1, and a monk’s poison at Swinstead.

In the text

no Italian priest Shall tithe or toll in our dominions; But as we, under heaven, are supreme head, So under Him that great supremacy, Where we do reign, we will alone uphold

King John, 3.1.153

‘Supreme head’ is Henry VIII’s title, lent to John, and an Elizabethan audience would have heard their own settlement defended three centuries early. The speech is the boldest John ever makes and it is about revenue as much as doctrine: ‘tithe or toll’ puts the argument where the money is, and he sells it back in 5.1.

Thus have I yielded up into your hand The circle of my glory.

King John, 5.1.1

The act begins with the transfer already made, so the audience never sees him decide. ‘Circle’ is the crown’s shape and also a closed loop, which is what the gesture turns out to be: Pandulph hands it straight back, and John holds the same object under a new tenancy, with the pope now his landlord.

Loyalty

The English lords serve John until they find Arthur’s body, then swear themselves to a French prince on an altar at Bury, and come back when a dying Frenchman tells them what Louis means to do with them afterwards. Nobody in this play changes sides for nothing.

Through the play Pembroke asks for Arthur’s release in 4.2, the lords kneel over his body in 4.3, they take the sacrament with Louis in 5.2, and Melun turns them in 5.4.

In the text

It is the curse of kings to be attended By slaves that take their humours for a warrant To break within the bloody house of life,

King John, 4.2.210

The man who never gave the order now complains about servants who act on moods, and the grammar gives him away: he talks about ‘kings’ and ‘slaves’ in general, never about himself and the man standing there. Hubert answers by producing the hand and seal, which is the one thing John had hoped never to see again.

This England never did, nor never shall, Lie at the proud foot of a conqueror, But when it first did help to wound itself.

The Bastard, 5.7.113

The boast has a clause attached, and the clause is the play. England has just spent two acts helping to wound itself, and the invasion ends by treaty, without a defeat. The speaker has no title, no land and no legitimate name, which is the last of the play’s questions about who gets to speak for a country.

Inheritance

The first scene brings on two brothers arguing about five hundred pounds a year and closes with a nine-year-old taking a kingdom. In between, land moves by a dying man’s will, by marriage settlement, by conquest, and by the death of the boy who stood next in line.

Through the play Robert Faulconbridge’s suit in 1.1, the five provinces given with Blanche in 2.1, Louis claiming through his wife in 3.4, and Prince Henry succeeding in 5.7.

In the text

Your strong possession much more than your right, Or else it must go wrong with you and me: So much my conscience whispers in your ear, Which none but heaven and you and I shall hear.

Queen Eleanor, 1.1.40

She corrects the weighting and leaves the facts alone, and then seals the correction with a promise of secrecy. The word ‘conscience’ is doing odd work: it tells him to be accurate in private and to keep saying something else in public. Nothing in the next four acts contradicts her.

Till you compound whose right is worthiest, We for the worthiest hold the right from both.

First Citizen, 2.1.282

The town appoints itself trustee of a disputed inheritance and holds the property until the claimants settle. ‘Compound’ is a word from law and from money, and the citizens use it to treat a war of succession as a matter awaiting probate, which is how the marriage they later propose settles it.

Grief

Constance will not let her loss be folded into a settlement, and she is given three long scenes in which to say so. Kings ask her to bind up her hair, a cardinal tells her she is uttering madness, and she answers both of them before dying offstage between scenes.

Through the play She sits on the ground in 3.1, unbinds her hair in 3.4, and is reported dead in a messenger’s speech in 4.2, three days before Eleanor.

In the text

I will instruct my sorrows to be proud, For grief is proud and makes his owner stoop. … Here I and sorrows sit; Here is my throne, bid kings come bow to it.

Constance, 3.1.68

She takes the one position nobody can order her out of and calls it a throne, and her refusal becomes a ceremony. The paradox in ‘proud’ and ‘stoop’ is not decoration: grief has made her lower than everyone at court and more unassailable, and both kings do eventually come and stand over her.

Must you with hot irons burn out both mine eyes?

Arthur, 4.1.41

He has read the paper and makes it a question, which forces Hubert to answer in the first person. The whole scene proceeds by this method: the boy never disputes the politics, he makes the man say out loud what he is going to do, and the plain statement is what finally becomes impossible.

Speech and rhetoric

Two kings argue their titles to a town wall, a citizen answers both of them with a marriage proposal, a cardinal unpicks an oath by means of another oath, and a bastard heckles the lot of them. The battles in this play settle little, and the real turns come in speeches.

Through the play The rival addresses to Angers in 2.1, Pandulph’s argument about vows in 3.1, John’s roundabout order for a murder in 3.3 and his attempt to disown it in 4.2, and the Bastard’s defiance of Louis in 5.2.

In the text

By heaven, these scroyles of Angiers flout you, kings, And stand securely on their battlements As in a theatre, whence they gape and point At your industrious scenes and acts of death.

The Bastard, 2.1.376

He treats the town wall as a gallery and the two armies as a company performing for it, which is how the staging has looked since the citizens first appeared above. ‘Scenes and acts of death’ makes the battle a production with a running order, and the line lands on an audience watching from their own seats.

To gild refined gold, to paint the lily, To throw a perfume on the violet,

Salisbury, 4.2.11

A list of improvements that spoil what they are applied to, offered as polite counsel by a man who will be in arms against the king within two scenes. The irony is that the gold is not refined: John has himself crowned again because he knows the first ceremony settled nothing, and the flattery diagnoses him exactly.
