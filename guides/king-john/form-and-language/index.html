King John · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Antithesis

Two matched phrases set against each other, so that the gap between them carries the meaning.

John says ‘our strong possession and our right for us’ and his mother repeats the phrase back with the balance altered. Pandulph builds a whole argument out of ‘All form is formless, order orderless’. The play thinks in paired terms because it has two candidates and no way of choosing.

Your strong possession much more than your right, Or else it must go wrong with you and me: So much my conscience whispers in your ear, Which none but heaven and you and I shall hear.

Queen Eleanor, 1.1.40

Personification

An abstraction given a body, a manner and a will of its own.

Commodity becomes a smooth-faced gentleman who tickles kings and breaks the pate of faith. Death lines his jaws with steel and feasts at Angers, and in Constance’s account Fortune is a woman who adulterates hourly with John.

By heaven, these scroyles of Angiers flout you, kings, And stand securely on their battlements As in a theatre, whence they gape and point At your industrious scenes and acts of death.

The Bastard, 2.1.376

Refrain

A line brought back at intervals, so that its meaning shifts with what has happened since.

‘And hang a calf’s-skin on those recreant limbs’ is Constance’s insult to Austria, and the Bastard picks it up and says it five times in 3.1, with variations. By the last repetition the peace made by a marriage has been broken and a king excommunicated, and the joke is being made over the wreckage.

Indirection

Ordering a thing without naming it, so that the words can be disowned later.

In 3.3 John takes nearly fifty lines to arrive at ‘Death’ and ‘A grave’. In 4.2 he turns on Hubert for having understood him: he broke with him only faintly, and a shaken head or a doubtful look would have stopped the whole thing.

He is a very serpent in my way; And wheresoe'er this foot of mine doth tread, He lies before me. Dost thou understand me? Thou art his keeper.

King John, 3.3.64

Prophecy

A statement about the future that changes the action by being believed, whether or not it is true.

Peter of Pomfret says John will give up his crown before noon on Ascension Day. John orders him hanged on the day he named, and then, holding the crown Pandulph has just handed back, works out what day it is and decides the prophecy has come true on his own terms.

It was my breath that blew this tempest up, Upon your stubborn usage of the pope; But since you are a gentle convertite, My tongue shall hush again this storm of war

Cardinal Pandulph, 5.1.18

Foils

A character built to throw another’s choices into relief by making different ones.

Arthur and the Bastard are both Plantagenet boys without a crown: one has the descent and no power, the other the energy and no lawful name. John and Philip both claim to be speaking for right, and both let it go for the same wedding.

Sweet, sweet, sweet poison for the age's tooth, Which, though I will not practise to deceive, Yet, to avoid deceit, I mean to learn;

The Bastard, 1.1.213

Wordplay

Puns and turned words, doing more than raising a laugh.

The Bastard prices his brother’s thin face as ‘a half-fac’d groat’ worth five hundred pounds a year, and answers the name Philip with ‘sparrow’. Constance asks for ‘room with Rome to curse awhile’, and the pun is the only revenge available to her.

A strange beginning: 'borrow'd majesty'!

Queen Eleanor, 1.1.5
