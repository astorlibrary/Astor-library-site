The Merchant of Venice · William Shakespeare

Context

Dates

1558
Il Pecorone reaches print Ser Giovanni Fiorentino’s fourteenth-century collection contains the bond, the trial and the lady disguised as a lawyer.
1594
Roderigo Lopez is executed in London The queen’s Portuguese physician, of Jewish descent, was convicted of conspiracy; the trial fed a wave of public feeling in the years just before the play.
1598
Entered in the Stationers’ Register Registered to James Roberts as the Merchant of Venice, two years before it was printed.
1600
First Quarto The earliest printed text, and the basis of most modern editions.
1605
First recorded performance, at court The first recorded performance, on 10 February, with a repeat before the court two days later.
1623
First Folio Printed among the comedies, following the quarto text closely.
1741
Charles Macklin plays Shylock at Drury Lane He abandoned the comic red wig of the Restoration stage and played the part seriously, which changed the play’s reputation.
1814
Edmund Kean’s London debut as Shylock A performance that made the moneylender a figure of tragic force, no longer a stage villain.
1879
Henry Irving’s Shylock at the Lyceum Irving’s dignified, sympathetic reading dominated the late Victorian stage and shaped expectations for decades.
2004
Michael Radford’s film A screen version set in a carefully reconstructed Venice, with Al Pacino as Shylock and an opening sequence about the ghetto.

Places

Venice
The city of the bond, the street insults and the court of justice.
The Rialto
The commercial exchange where news of ships travels and where Antonio has abused Shylock in public.
Genoa
Where Tubal reports Jessica spending her father’s ducats and trading her mother’s ring.
Padua
Home of Doctor Bellario, whose legal opinion and borrowed credentials get Portia into the courtroom.
Tripoli
One of the ports named for Antonio’s argosies, and the port the ship reported lost was sailing home from.

Critics

William Hazlitt

Characters of Shakespear’s Plays (1817)

Hazlitt, writing after Edmund Kean’s Shylock, found the Jew a good hater and “a man no less sinned against than sinning”, whose revenge is sharpened by the contempt the Christians show him. As Shylock ceased to be a popular bugbear, Hazlitt noted, he became something of a favourite with the more thoughtful part of the audience.

Heinrich Heine

Shakespeares Mädchen und Frauen (1838)

Heine remembered a performance at Drury Lane where a pale English woman wept at the end of the trial scene and cried out that the poor man was wronged. He suggested that the play could be seen as Shylock’s tragedy.
