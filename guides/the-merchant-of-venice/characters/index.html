The Merchant of Venice · William Shakespeare

Characters

Shylock

A Jewish moneylender of Venice; first appears 1.3

He appears in five scenes and dominates the play. Shakespeare gives him the abuse he has taken in detail (the spitting, the name-calling, the kicks) and then has him use that history as a legal instrument. The play neither excuses what he does nor lets his enemies off the account he reads them.

Portia

An heiress of Belmont; first appears 1.2

Rich, quick and confined: her father’s will means she may neither choose nor refuse. She takes the only freedom available by leaving Belmont in a man’s gown, and the same intelligence that saves Antonio is later used to trap her own husband over a ring.

Antonio

A merchant of Venice; first appears 1.1

The title character speaks the first line of the play and almost nothing in the last scene. His sadness is never explained; his willingness to die for Bassanio is stated plainly, and so is his intention to go on spitting on Shylock. He is the only major figure who ends the play unpaired.

Bassanio

A Venetian gentleman, Antonio’s friend; first appears 1.1

He has run through his money and proposes to repair it by marrying a fortune, which he explains to Antonio without embarrassment. His casket speech rejects outward show in terms that apply uncomfortably well to his own courtship, financed by a borrowed appearance of wealth.

Jessica

Shylock’s daughter; first appears 2.3

She robs her father, sells her mother’s ring for a monkey, and converts. Shakespeare gives her the discomfort as well as the escape: she is ashamed of her boy’s clothes, and in the last act she and Lorenzo exchange a string of stories about lovers whose nights ended badly.

Lorenzo

A friend of Bassanio and Antonio; first appears 1.1

He plans the elopement as a raid, is pleased about the gold, and then speaks the play’s most beautiful verse about music and the harmony of the spheres. The distance between those two facts is one of the things the last act is for.

Nerissa

Portia’s waiting-gentlewoman; first appears 1.2

She doubles Portia at every level: she gives the sceptical answer in 1.2, marries Bassanio’s friend, disguises herself as a clerk, and runs the same trick with the same ring. The play works by these pairings, and she is the clearest of them.

Gratiano

A friend of Bassanio; first appears 1.1

Bassanio says he speaks an infinite deal of nothing, and for three acts that is a joke about a harmless talker. In the courtroom he becomes the voice that cheers each reversal and asks for Shylock to be hanged, which is where the comic licence stops being comic.
