The Merchant of Venice · William Shakespeare

Themes

Bonds and binding

The word runs through every plot in the play: a bond is a loan agreement, a tie of friendship, a marriage, a ring, and finally a piece of writing that can take a man’s life. Venice runs on written obligation, and Shylock treats the writing as meaning exactly what it says, until Portia reads it more literally still.

A bond is sealed in 1.3, tested in 4.1 and replaced in 5.1 by a ring, which is another bond, and also broken.

'Hath a dog money? is it possible A cur can lend three thousand ducats?'

Shylock, 1.3.116

I am as like to call thee so again, To spit on thee again, to spurn thee too.

Antonio, 1.3.125

Mercy and justice

Portia’s argument is that mercy cannot be compelled, which is true, and she then compels Shylock into receiving it. The court offers him his life on condition that he give up his religion, and calls that leniency. The play stages the gap between the doctrine and the settlement enacted in its name.

Mercy is preached at 4.1.184, refused by Shylock at 4.1.206, and delivered at 4.1.386 as a set of conditions including forced conversion.

Hie thee, gentle Jew. The Hebrew will turn Christian: he grows kind.

Antonio, 1.3.174

The villany you teach me, I will execute, and it shall go hard but I will better the instruction.

Shylock, 3.1.58

Antisemitism and the outsider

The Christians of Venice call Shylock dog, devil and cur to his face and in his absence, and Antonio says plainly that he will do it again. The play lets Shylock answer, at length and in prose, and then ends with his enemies in a garden and his name unmentioned.

The abuse is itemised by its victim in 1.3, answered in 3.1, and given legal form in 4.1, where an alien is found to have no standing against a citizen.

Still have I borne it with a patient shrug, For sufferance is the badge of all our tribe.

Shylock, 1.3.104

'Hath a dog money? is it possible A cur can lend three thousand ducats?'

Shylock, 1.3.116

Worth, ornament and risk

Three caskets ask what a thing is worth; three suitors answer with what they think they deserve. The same question runs through the merchant plot, where ships are venture capital and a pound of flesh is valued, out loud, against mutton and beef.

Morocco chooses the outside, Arragon his own merit, Bassanio the risk, and the man who hazards everything has borrowed the stake.

so is the will of a living daughter curbed by the will of a dead father

Portia, 1.2.20

Our house is hell, and thou, a merry devil, Didst rob it of some taste of tediousness.

Jessica, 2.3.2

Friendship, marriage and rivalry

Antonio’s feeling for Bassanio is the strongest attachment in the play and has no name available to it. Portia understands the competition exactly, which is why the ring she gives her husband is the one thing she makes him swear never to surrender, and why she has it taken from him in a courtroom.

Antonio offers his body for Bassanio in Act I, is outbid in Act IV when Bassanio says he would give up his wife to save him, and is given a ring to return in Act V.

In sooth, I know not why I am so sad:

Antonio, 1.1.1

How sweet the moonlight sleeps upon this bank! Here will we sit and let the sounds of music Creep in our ears:

Lorenzo, 5.1.55

Disguise and the law

Nothing in the trial scene is what it appears: the lawyer is a woman, the clerk is her maid, the mercy is a threat and the rescue is a confiscation. Belmont’s women enter the Venetian court by the only door available to them and leave it having beaten the men at their own document.

Jessica’s boy’s clothes in Act II rehearse the device; Portia’s gown in Act IV makes it the machinery of the plot.

so is the will of a living daughter curbed by the will of a dead father

Portia, 1.2.20

So may the outward shows be least themselves: The world is still deceived with ornament.

Bassanio, 3.2.74
