Bonds and binding

The word runs through every plot in the play: a bond is a loan agreement, a tie of friendship, a marriage, a ring, and finally a piece of writing that can take a man’s life. Venice runs on written obligation, and Shylock treats the writing as meaning exactly what it says, until Portia reads it more literally still.

Through the play A bond is sealed in 1.3, tested in 4.1 and replaced in 5.1 by a ring, which is another bond, and also broken.

In the text

Give him direction for this merry bond,

Shylock, 1.3.170

Shylock calls the bond ‘merry’, and nobody in the scene challenges the word. The forfeit is framed as a joke between men who have just exchanged threats, which is what allows Antonio to sign it: a penalty that cannot be meant does not need to be feared.

My deeds upon my head! I crave the law,

Shylock, 4.1.206

He takes the consequences onto himself in the words of a formula that Shakespeare’s audience would have heard as an echo of the Gospel account of Christ’s condemnation, and which was used for centuries to justify persecution. Giving Shylock that sentence is the harshest thing the play does to him, and he says it in the act of asking only for what he is owed.

Mercy and justice

Portia’s argument is that mercy cannot be compelled, which is true, and she then compels Shylock into receiving it. The court offers him his life on condition that he give up his religion, and calls that leniency. The play stages the gap between the doctrine and the settlement enacted in its name.

Through the play Mercy is preached at 4.1.184, refused by Shylock at 4.1.206, and delivered at 4.1.386 as a set of conditions including forced conversion.

In the text

Hie thee, gentle Jew. The Hebrew will turn Christian: he grows kind.

Antonio, 1.3.174

‘Gentle’ and ‘gentile’ were near-homophones, so the compliment is also a joke about conversion, and the joke turns out to be a prophecy the court will enforce at knife-point in Act IV. Antonio can praise the man only by imagining him becoming someone else.

The quality of mercy is not strain’d, It droppeth as the gentle rain from heaven Upon the place beneath: it is twice blest; It blesseth him that gives and him that takes:

Portia, 4.1.184

The whole speech depends on mercy being uncompelled, which is a direct answer to the word ‘compulsion’ in Shylock’s question. What follows in the scene contradicts it: the court will compel mercy out of Shylock at the price of his religion, and the word for what he receives will still be mercy.

Antisemitism and the outsider

The Christians of Venice call Shylock dog, devil and cur to his face and in his absence, and Antonio says plainly that he will do it again. The play lets Shylock answer, at length and in prose, and then ends with his enemies in a garden and his name unmentioned.

Through the play The abuse is itemised by its victim in 1.3, answered in 3.1, and given legal form in 4.1, where an alien is found to have no standing against a citizen.

In the text

Still have I borne it with a patient shrug, For sufferance is the badge of all our tribe.

Shylock, 1.3.104

A badge in Venice was not a metaphor: Jews were required to wear identifying marks, and Shylock takes that fact and makes endurance itself the uniform. Look at the shrug: a small physical gesture, repeated over years, standing in for everything he has swallowed.

I am a Jew. Hath not a Jew eyes? hath not a Jew hands, organs, dimensions, senses, affections, passions? fed with the same food, hurt with the same weapons, subject to the same diseases, healed by the same means, warmed and cooled by the same winter and summer, as a Christian is?

Shylock, 3.1.48

The argument is built entirely on the body, with no appeal to doctrine, and the repetitions of ‘the same’ make the shared ground impossible to deny. It is in prose, so it sounds like a man talking, and its logic is unfinished: everything so far has been about suffering, and the next sentence will apply the same equality to revenge.

Worth, ornament and risk

Three caskets ask what a thing is worth; three suitors answer with what they think they deserve. The same question runs through the merchant plot, where ships are venture capital and a pound of flesh is valued, out loud, against mutton and beef.

Through the play Morocco chooses the outside, Arragon his own merit, Bassanio the risk, and the man who hazards everything has borrowed the stake.

In the text

so is the will of a living daughter curbed by the will of a dead father

Portia, 1.2.20

The sentence turns on one word used twice: ‘will’ as desire and ‘will’ as legal document. Portia’s problem is stated as a pun, which is a warning about the kind of play this is: the trial in Act IV will also be won by a word with two meanings.

All that glisters is not gold; Often have you heard that told: Many a man his life hath sold But my outside to behold:

Morocco, 2.7.66

The verse is in short rhyming lines, a nursery metre for a lesson everybody claims to know already, and the casket answers in the first person as though the gold were speaking. Morocco has just argued himself into gold by reasoning about what the world values, and the scroll faults him for reasoning at all.

Friendship, marriage and rivalry

Antonio’s feeling for Bassanio is the strongest attachment in the play and has no name available to it. Portia understands the competition exactly, which is why the ring she gives her husband is the one thing she makes him swear never to surrender, and why she has it taken from him in a courtroom.

Through the play Antonio offers his body for Bassanio in Act I, is outbid in Act IV when Bassanio says he would give up his wife to save him, and is given a ring to return in Act V.

In the text

In sooth, I know not why I am so sad:

Antonio, 1.1.1

A comedy begins with a man announcing an unexplained depression, and no explanation is ever supplied: his friends propose his ships and then love, and he denies both. Leaving it unaccounted for puts a hole at the centre of the title role that every production has to fill with something.

How sweet the moonlight sleeps upon this bank! Here will we sit and let the sounds of music Creep in our ears:

Lorenzo, 5.1.55

The last act opens by changing the weather completely: after four hundred lines of courtroom prose the play produces its most lyrical verse, and does it in the mouth of the man who organised the elopement and the theft. Shakespeare buys the comedy its ending with music, and the person who says she is never merry when she hears it is sitting next to him.

Disguise and the law

Nothing in the trial scene is what it appears: the lawyer is a woman, the clerk is her maid, the mercy is a threat and the rescue is a confiscation. Belmont’s women enter the Venetian court by the only door available to them and leave it having beaten the men at their own document.

Through the play Jessica’s boy’s clothes in Act II rehearse the device; Portia’s gown in Act IV makes it the machinery of the plot.

In the text

So may the outward shows be least themselves: The world is still deceived with ornament.

Bassanio, 3.2.74

He wins by distrusting appearances, having arrived in Belmont on borrowed money so as to look the part. ‘Still’ means always, and the line condemns a world Bassanio has been working rather successfully; Shakespeare lets him say the true thing without noticing that it applies to him.

Which is the merchant here, and which the Jew?

Portia, 4.1.171

The question establishes her impartiality and is also unanswerable by sight, which is the play’s point about what Venice thinks it can see. She already knows which is which, so the line is a performance of neutrality by the one person in the room who has come to win.
