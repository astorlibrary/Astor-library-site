The Merchant of Venice · William Shakespeare

Form and language

The legal quibble

A judgement that depends on the exact words of a document, whatever its evident intention.

Portia defeats the bond by noting that it grants flesh and says nothing of blood: a stricter reading than Shylock’s own, from someone who has just argued that strictness damns us all.

so is the will of a living daughter curbed by the will of a dead father

Portia, 1.2.20

Parallel plotting

Two or more actions with the same shape, so that each comments on the other.

The caskets, the bond and the rings are all tests in which a man must choose what to give up, and all three are set by someone with more control than he realises.

All that glisters is not gold; Often have you heard that told: Many a man his life hath sold But my outside to behold:

Morocco, 2.7.66

Prose against verse

The shift between unmetered speech and blank verse as a marker of register, class or feeling.

Shylock’s great answer to Salarino is in prose, which gives it the sound of a man speaking more than performing; Portia’s mercy speech is verse, and sounds like a set piece because it is one.

so is the will of a living daughter curbed by the will of a dead father

Portia, 1.2.20

Rhetorical questions

A run of questions whose answers are obvious, used to make denial impossible.

Eyes, hands, organs, dimensions, senses, affections, passions: the questions pile up until the last one changes direction and asks about revenge instead of about bleeding.

'Hath a dog money? is it possible A cur can lend three thousand ducats?'

Shylock, 1.3.116

Body imagery

A physical image repeated until it binds unrelated scenes together.

The bond’s pound of flesh is only the plainest instance; Shylock calls Jessica his own flesh and blood, and the court eventually divides his property along the same line between body and estate.

I am a Jew. Hath not a Jew eyes? hath not a Jew hands, organs, dimensions, senses, affections, passions? fed with the same food, hurt with the same weapons, subject to the same diseases, healed by the same means, warmed and cooled by the same winter and summer, as a Christian is?

Shylock, 3.1.48

Scriptural argument

Quoting or contesting scripture as a way of settling a dispute.

Shylock and Antonio argue over Jacob’s sheep in 1.3, each claiming the Old Testament, and Portia’s mercy speech borrows the language of the Lord’s Prayer to address a man who does not say it.

Hie thee, gentle Jew. The Hebrew will turn Christian: he grows kind.

Antonio, 1.3.174
