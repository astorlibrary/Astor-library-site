Cymbeline · William Shakespeare

What happens

Act I Imogen’s marriage, and Posthumus banished

Two gentlemen tell the audience what the court cannot say aloud: the princess has married a poor gentleman, the king has banished him, and every courtier is privately glad. Imogen and Posthumus exchange a ring and a bracelet before he sails. Within a single evening at Philario’s table in Rome, a stranger has made that marriage into a bet, and by the end of the act he has talked his way into her bedroom in a trunk.

1.1.1–70
A courtier lays out the marriage, the banishment, and the two royal sons stolen from the nursery twenty years ago and never traced.
1.1.71–192
Imogen and Posthumus part under the Queen’s eye and exchange two gifts that the rest of the play will use as evidence. Cymbeline arrives and drives him out.
1.2–1.3
Cloten swaggers about a duel he lost while two lords mock him in asides. Imogen makes Pisanio describe her husband’s departure until the ship is the size of a gnat.
1.4
Iachimo needles Posthumus into staking Imogen’s diamond on her fidelity. Philario tries to stop it; the terms are drawn up by lawyers instead.
1.5–1.6
The Queen collects drugs from her doctor and plants a box on Pisanio. Iachimo tries Imogen directly, is rebuffed, apologises handsomely, and asks her to keep a trunk of plate in her bedchamber overnight.

Act II Iachimo in the trunk, and the wager lost

Iachimo climbs out of the trunk at midnight, writes down the pictures, the chimneypiece and the andirons, slips the bracelet off Imogen’s arm and notes the mole on her breast. In Rome every item he recites is correct, and Posthumus believes the one thing that is not. Between the two scenes Cloten wakes Imogen with hired musicians and is told that his rival’s oldest coat is worth more than the whole of him.

2.1
Two lords flatter Cloten to his face and insult him in asides. Left alone, one of them prays that Imogen’s honour will hold.
2.2
Iachimo leaves the trunk, inventories the room, takes the bracelet, finds the mole, and notices she fell asleep over the tale of Tereus.
2.3
Cloten hires music to wake her, and Imogen tells him Posthumus’s meanest garment is dearer to her than every hair on his head.
2.4
Iachimo describes the tapestry, the chimneypiece and the andirons, produces the bracelet, then names the mole. Posthumus hands over the diamond.
2.5
Alone, Posthumus decides every vice in man is inherited from his mother, and resolves to write against women for the rest of his life.

Act III Two letters, and the road to Milford

Britain will not pay the Roman tribute, and in the same hours two letters from Posthumus reach the court: one telling Imogen to meet him at Milford Haven, one telling Pisanio to kill her when she gets there. Pisanio shows her the second, refuses to use his sword, and dresses her as a boy. In the mountains above the haven an exiled lord is raising two young men who do not know they are the king’s sons.

3.1
Lucius asks for the three thousand pounds a year Cassibelan granted. The Queen and Cloten talk Cymbeline into refusing, and Rome declares war.
3.2
Pisanio reads his order to commit murder, hides it, and hands Imogen the letter that sends her galloping for Wales.
3.3
Belarius takes his boys hunting past a door so low it bows them, then tells the audience who they are and why he stole them.
3.4
Imogen reads the charge of adultery, offers Pisanio her heart and his sword, and is talked instead into the doublet and hose of Fidele.
3.5–3.7
Cloten wrings Posthumus’s suit out of Pisanio and rides for Milford with rape in mind; Imogen, starving, walks into the cave and is made welcome; Rome levies gentlemen volunteers under Iachimo.

Act IV Cloten’s death, and Fidele’s burial

Guiderius kills Cloten in Posthumus’s clothes and sends the head down the creek. Imogen swallows the Queen’s drug, is laid out as dead, and is buried with a song by two brothers who think she is a boy called Fidele. She wakes beside a headless corpse in her husband’s suit, reads it as her husband, and is picked up by the Roman general as his page.

4.1
Wearing Posthumus’s suit, Cloten measures his own legs against his rival’s and rehearses the murder and the rape he means to carry out in it.
4.2
Cloten loses his head to Guiderius. Imogen is laid beside his body as dead, the brothers speak the dirge, and she wakes to the wrong conclusion.
4.3
Cymbeline’s daughter and stepson are missing, his queen is dangerously ill with a fever, and the legions have landed on his coast.
4.4
Guiderius and Arviragus refuse to hide higher in the mountains, and Belarius gives way and follows them down to the British army.

Act V The battle, Jupiter, and the recognitions

Posthumus lands with the Roman army, changes out of his Italian clothes and fights for the country whose princess he believes he has had killed. An old man and two boys hold a narrow lane and change the rout into a British victory. In prison Jupiter comes down on an eagle and leaves a riddle on the sleeper’s chest. In Cymbeline’s tent every disguise, lie and supposed death is unpicked in front of everybody.

5.1
Holding the handkerchief he asked for as proof of the murder, Posthumus resolves to die in British clothes for the wife he had killed.
5.2
Posthumus disarms Iachimo without either knowing the other; Belarius and the boys rescue a captured Cymbeline.
5.3
Posthumus tells a fleeing lord how two striplings and an old man turned the battle, loses his temper at him, and is taken prisoner as a Roman.
5.4
The ghosts of the Leonati circle the sleeping Posthumus and petition the god, who throws a thunderbolt, leaves a tablet on his chest and ascends.
5.5
The Queen’s deathbed confession, Iachimo’s confession, the ring, the page who is a wife, Cloten’s head, the mole on a neck, and peace with Rome.
