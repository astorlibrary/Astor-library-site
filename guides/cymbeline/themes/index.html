Proof

Iachimo’s report is accurate in every particular and wrong in its conclusion. The bracelet was on her arm, the mole is under her breast, the andirons are two winking Cupids of silver, and none of it touches the question Posthumus actually asked.

Through the play Objects carry the case in the first half and are overturned in the second, when a second mole, on Guiderius’s neck, gives a father back his son.

In the text

It is a manacle of love; I’ll place it Upon this fairest prisoner.

Posthumus, 1.1.128

The love-token is named a fetter and the wife a prisoner in the same breath, and Posthumus means it as gallantry. The play takes him at his word instead: the bracelet is the thing that will be used against her, and the Queen has just called Imogen her prisoner in the scene’s opening line.

Our Tarquin thus Did softly press the rushes ere he waken’d The chastity he wounded.

Iachimo, 2.2.13

He compares himself to a rapist before he has done anything, and the comparison is his own idea. Shakespeare had written Tarquin’s approach at length in The Rape of Lucrece, and the echo tells an audience exactly how to feel about a man who will in fact only look. The word our makes Rome the shared ancestor of both crimes.

Belief and doubt

Posthumus goes from staking everything on his wife’s honour to vowing to tear her limb-meal inside a single scene, and the turn happens on a bracelet he has already been told could have been stolen by a servant.

Through the play Philario argues for doubt in 2.4 and is brushed aside; by the fifth act Posthumus is making the same case against himself and asking for a cord or a knife.

In the text

O blessed that I might not! I chose an eagle, And did avoid a puttock.

Imogen, 1.1.147

A puttock is a kite, the carrion hawk that will not hunt. Imogen answers a king in the middle of a rage with a falconer’s joke, and picks the comparison that ranks men the way a court ranks birds. It is the first sign that she will argue rank on its own ground and win.

Could I find out The woman’s part in me! For there’s no motion That tends to vice in man but I affirm It is the woman’s part.

Posthumus, 2.5.19

Having decided his wife is false, he works outward until every fault in the world belongs to women, including the faults in himself. The speech is a man building a general law out of one bad evening, and its logic is the same as the wager’s: a single case is made to stand for all cases. He ends by resolving to write against them.

Disguise

Imogen becomes Fidele, Belarius answers to Morgan, the princes are Polydore and Cadwal, Cloten dies in Posthumus’s suit and Posthumus fights in a peasant’s. Almost nobody in Wales is called by their own name.

Through the play Disguise keeps people alive until Cloten borrows a suit, after which it starts killing them; the last scene works by taking every borrowed name back.

In the text

Hark, hark! the lark at heaven’s gate sings, And Phœbus ’gins arise,

A Musician, 2.3.17

One of the loveliest songs in Shakespeare is paid for by the nastiest man in the play, who has just told the musicians to try whether they can penetrate her with their fingering. The beauty is real and so is the purpose behind it, and the scene gives an audience no way of keeping them apart.

Poor I am stale, a garment out of fashion, And for I am richer than to hang by th’ walls I must be ripp’d.

Imogen, 3.4.51

She reaches for clothing, the play’s currency for worth, and describes herself as a gown too good to be left hanging and so worth cutting up. The bitterness is in richer: being valuable is the reason for the destruction. Within two hundred lines she will be putting on a boy’s doublet to stay alive.

Service and loyalty

Pisanio is ordered to commit murder by a master he loves, and decides that keeping faith means refusing. The Queen tries to buy him with preferment, Cloten tries to frighten him with a drawn sword, and he goes on serving Imogen through both.

Through the play Posthumus himself supplies the verdict in 5.1, saying that every good servant does not all commands; by then Pisanio has been proved right for two acts.

In the text

’tis slander, Whose edge is sharper than the sword, whose tongue Outvenoms all the worms of Nile

Pisanio, 3.4.33

Pisanio names the play’s real weapon. Nobody in Cymbeline is killed by a lie’s author; the lie travels by letter and post-horse from Rome to Wales and does its work at a distance. He has just said the paper cut her throat already, which leaves his sword with nothing to do and makes his disobedience possible.

You must forget to be a woman; change Command into obedience

Pisanio, 3.4.166

The instruction is a list of subtractions, and the first thing to go is the right to give orders. A princess must learn to be spoken to as a servant is spoken to, and Pisanio goes on to add gibes, sauciness and a sunburnt cheek. Imogen cuts him off to say she is almost a man already.

Royal birth

Two boys brought up in a cave stand and fight like princes the moment a battle reaches them, and Belarius says openly that he has never been able to hide it. Shakespeare takes it for granted that breeding will out, and the cave has done them no harm at all.

Through the play What starts as Belarius’s private astonishment in 3.3 becomes state business in 5.5, when the succession he stole is handed back over a mantle and a mole.

In the text

How hard it is to hide the sparks of nature!

Belarius, 3.3.82

Belarius has spent twenty years raising princes as hunters and cannot keep the blood down. He goes on to describe Guiderius flushing and straining at a war story, acting out the feats in his sleep. The play backs him: the boys’ courage in the lane wins the battle, and nobody has taught it to them.

No, my lord; I have got two worlds by’t.

Imogen, 5.5.413

Cymbeline counts in thrones and his daughter counts in brothers, and she has the better arithmetic. She has lost the succession and gained two men she already chose to love in a cave when she had no reason to. The line also disposes of the one grievance that would have made the reunion political.

Britain, Rome and the tribute

Cymbeline refuses three thousand pounds a year and goes to war over it, egged on by a queen with her own reasons and a stepson who thinks Britain is a world by itself. Britain wins the battle and then agrees to pay anyway.

Through the play Roman arrogance in 3.1 becomes Roman defeat in 5.2, and the settlement pays the money without giving back the victory.

In the text

Britain is a world by itself, and we will nothing pay for wearing our own noses.

Cloten, 3.1.12

The play gives its most quotable statement of British independence to its greatest fool, and puts it in prose between two speeches of the Queen’s ringing verse. The argument is not silly, which is the difficulty: Cymbeline acts on it, goes to war, wins, and then in the last scene pays the money anyway.

Although the victor, we submit to Cæsar And to the Roman empire, promising To pay our wonted tribute

Cymbeline, 5.5.508

Britain wins the war and pays the money, and the king blames the refusal on his dead wife. No Roman asks for this; it is offered. The play ends its imperial plot the way it ends its marriage plot, by handing back what was taken and calling the result peace, and the last word of the scene is exactly that.

Pardon

Posthumus, standing over the man who destroyed his marriage, tells him the only power he has is to spare him. Cymbeline then pardons everybody in the tent, including the army he has just beaten and the boy who beheaded his stepson.

Through the play The play withholds punishment from almost everyone. The two who die, Cloten and the Queen, are the two who never ask for anything.

In the text

The heaviness and guilt within my bosom Takes off my manhood. I have belied a lady, The Princess of this country

Iachimo, 5.2.1

Iachimo explains his own defeat as a physical effect of guilt, which is the only supernatural claim he ever makes. The play lets him be right: he loses to the husband he cheated, without either man knowing. Everything he says here he will say again, at greater length and unprompted, in the tent.

Whom best I love I cross; to make my gift, The more delay’d, delighted.

Jupiter, 5.4.102

The god arrives to tell four ghosts that their suffering was never their business. It is the play’s only direct statement of how it works, and it is unsatisfying on purpose: a divine plan that explains an ordered murder as a delay in the delivery of happiness. The audience has watched Imogen buried alive and is entitled to be unconvinced.
