The Railway Children · E. Nesbit

Form and language

Omission

Something left out on purpose, at a point where the reader will notice the gap and fill it.

The arrest happens behind a library door; the trial happens between chapters; the prison is never entered. Nesbit puts the reader inside the children’s ignorance and then leaves clues only they would miss: a trunk of men’s clothes that have not gone with their owner, a mother who posts her own letters, a bitter aside about English judges. The reader solves it a page or two before Bobbie does, which is exactly the margin Nesbit wants.

“Father's been called away--on business,” said Mother. “Come, darlings, go at once.”

Mother, Chapter 1

Direct address

A narrator or speaker turning outward to address the reader or the audience by name.

‘I don’t suppose they had ever thought about railways’ is the second sentence, and the narrator never afterwards pretends to be absent. She admits she is growing fond of Roberta, drops the name Roberta because nobody else uses it, calls you the Gentle Reader when the subject is priggishness, and at the end tells you that you and she are not wanted in the house and had better go. The device makes a confidante of the reader, which is why the withheld secret is so uncomfortable.

They were not railway children to begin with.

Chapter 1

Refrain

A phrase brought back at intervals, so that its meaning shifts with what has happened since.

‘All prisoners and captives’ is said three times in one paragraph by a mother who cannot say why, and comes back in Bobbie’s letter as ‘She told us once to pray for all prisoners and captives. I see now.’ The daily cry of ‘Take our love to Father’ works the same way: a game in Chapter 3, and in the last chapter a message that has, without anyone knowing it, been going to the right address.

“Yes,” said Mother, “upon all prisoners and captives. All prisoners and captives.”

Mother, Chapter 5

Symbolic objects

An object the narrative comes back to until it stands for the argument itself.

A toy engine bursts in Chapter 1 and is mended by a stranger in Chapter 4, and the plot follows the same course: a family broken, and mended with a stranger’s help. Two red flannel petticoats become six flags; a sheet of newspaper round a parcel of magazines becomes the verdict; three gold watches are offered and Bobbie proposes selling them to find somebody else’s wife. Nesbit prefers to let a household object do the work a speech would otherwise have to do.

“Oh!” said Roberta, drawing a long breath; “it was like a great dragon tearing by. Did you feel it fan us with its hot wings?”

Roberta, Chapter 2

The set piece

A self-contained episode built to a single effect and complete in itself, whether comic or ceremonial.

The landslide, the birthday tea, the burning barge, the tunnel: each occupies a chapter, has its own shape and could be read aloud on its own, which is what serial publication in a monthly magazine asks for. The device has a price (the chapters can be shuffled in the memory), and Nesbit offsets it by threading the father’s absence through every one of them.

“We never got any cherries, after all,” said Bobbie.

Bobbie, Chapter 6

Dialect

Spelling and grammar used to mark where a speaker comes from, and what being heard that way costs them.

Perks, Mrs Viney, Bill the Bargee, the engine-driver and the signalman are written phonetically; the family and the old gentleman are not. The spelling is therefore a map of who is being listened to, and the book notices it more than once: a porter is surprised that anyone answers his questions, and the same porter has to be shown written labels before he will believe his neighbours respect him.

“Well, you see, you must take one side or the other. Same as with Liberals and Conservatives. The great thing is to take your side and then stick to it, whatever happens.”

Perks, Chapter 6

Foils and doubles

A character constructed to throw another’s choices into relief by making different ones.

The Russian is the father with the lid off: a man imprisoned for something he wrote, separated from a wife and children who do not know where he is. He arrives in Chapter 5, wears the father’s clothes, sits in the father’s chair, and is restored to his family long before the father is. The three children are graded the same way: Bobbie who knows, Peter who acts, Phyllis who says the thing out loud.

“Of course they can. Girls are just as clever as boys, and don't you forget it! How would you like to be an engine-driver, Phil?”

Father, Chapter 1
