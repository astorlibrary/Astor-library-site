The Railway Children · E. Nesbit

Themes

Secrets

At the centre of the novel is a fact kept from three children, from two of them to the last page, and from the reader for two thirds of the book. Nesbit makes the withholding itself the action, so that tact, silence and the decision not to ask become the things her characters actually do.

The mother forbids questions in Chapter 1 and Bobbie immediately resolves not to know she has been crying; the unopened trunk of a man’s clothes in Chapter 5 is almost too much for her; the newspaper gives her the answer in Chapter 10; and in Chapter 11 the mother asks whether Bobbie will tell the others, hears ‘Because—’, and says ‘Exactly’, which is the whole book in one word.

“Father's been called away--on business,” said Mother. “Come, darlings, go at once.”

Mother, Chapter 1

“If Mother doesn't want us to know she's been crying,” she said to herself as she heard through the dark the catching of her Mother's breath, “we WON'T know it. That's all.”

Bobbie, Chapter 1

Justice

Three people in this book are punished for things they did not do, or did not do in the way the charge says: a Russian writer sent to the mines for a book about the poor, a father convicted on letters somebody else put in his desk, and a boy who believes that taking coal from the top of the heap is mining. Nesbit is careful to show that the machinery of judgement is the same in each case, and that only influence moves it.

A Station Master decides privately to look over one case; the mother explains that English judges only think a man has done wrong; she says bitterly that nobody will listen to her; and what finally works is a rich acquaintance who had doubts when he read the papers; no legal appeal comes into it.

“I'm not a thief,” said Peter, as firmly as he could. “I'm a coal-miner.”

Peter, Chapter 2

“Or when the Judges THINK they've done wrong,” said Mother. “Yes, that's so in England. But in Russia it was different.”

Mother, Chapter 5

Poverty

The family’s poverty is real enough to mean jam or butter but not both, and unreal enough to be reversible, and Nesbit knows the difference better than her children do. The book’s hardest chapter sets their idea of a kind present against a porter’s idea of what a man loses when the village decides he cannot make both ends meet.

The pretty furniture is left behind and the useful furniture packed; Peter takes coal because they cannot afford a fire; the children are forbidden to ask strangers for things and go on doing it for other people; and Perks makes them read out every label before he will accept a bun.

“We're taking the useful ones,” said Mother; “we've got to play at being Poor for a bit, my chickabiddy.”

Mother, Chapter 1

“I'm not a thief,” said Peter, as firmly as he could. “I'm a coal-miner.”

Peter, Chapter 2

Railway workers

Nesbit’s railway is a workplace before it is a playground, and almost everyone the children love is somebody at work: a porter cleaning lamps, an engine-driver explaining the injector, a signalman who has not slept in five days because his son has pneumonia and nobody can be found to take his duty. The mother’s writing is put on the same footing: so many pages, so many buns.

The whitewash line on the coal heap is there because coal gets stolen; Perks is paid in respect as well as wages; the signalman’s exhaustion is a staffing failure and no moral one; and the book’s comfortable ending is partly a woman being able to stop writing for money.

“Of course they can. Girls are just as clever as boys, and don't you forget it! How would you like to be an engine-driver, Phil?”

Father, Chapter 1

“Well, you see, you must take one side or the other. Same as with Liberals and Conservatives. The great thing is to take your side and then stick to it, whatever happens.”

Perks, Chapter 6

Home

A house that is first a dark lump with no supper in it becomes the place where a stranger is put to bed in another man’s night-shirt and an injured boy is carried in without anyone being asked. Nesbit measures the family’s recovery by how much of the house they can give away, and she ends the novel on a threshold, outside the house.

The arrival by candlelight; the forgotten room with the cold beef in it; the Russian in the chair that had been the father’s; the hospital with one patient; and the last page, where the narrator stands outside the back door and then walks off across the field.

They were not railway children to begin with.

Chapter 1

Mother, Chapter 1

The railway

The trains are named like beasts, spoken to, waved at and thanked, and the children’s affection for them is treated as a real relationship. It stays clear of whimsy because Nesbit also gives the machine its technical vocabulary (couplings, injector, automatic brake, the communication cord), so the dragon and the engine are the same object seen twice.

The 9.15 becomes the Green Dragon and the tunnel its lair; Bobbie is taught the brake by the driver she has frightened; the train she stops seems almost to have heard her; and on the last morning the whole train answers, which is the only moment the book lets the fancy come true.

They were not railway children to begin with.

Charity and friendship

The children make friends across every line the period draws (a porter, a bargee, a doctor, a schoolboy, a director of the railway), and Nesbit is exact about what makes such a friendship possible and what breaks it. What matters is less the generosity than whether the person on the receiving end can accept without being diminished.

Peter apologises on a lonely road to a man who has caught him stealing; Perks is angrier at being pitied than at being ignored; a bargeman who has twisted Peter’s ear gives the children the freedom of nineteen locks; and a clergyman supplies the word Nesbit wants, loving-kindness.

He was a very nice-looking old gentleman, and he looked as if he were nice, too, which is not at all the same thing.

Chapter 3

It just means that a person is able to know that you are unhappy, and to love you extra on that account, without bothering you by telling you all the time how sorry she is for you.

Chapter 7

The narrator

The story is told by someone who interrupts it. She admits preferences, addresses the reader, changes a character’s name because she has tired of the longer one, and says plainly when she is keeping something back. The children’s ignorance of their father is held in place by a teller who knows and chooses her moment.

The aside on Roberta’s name in Chapter 2 gives the eldest child two names, and the book uses both: Bobbie to her family, Roberta when she turns on herself. Later the narrator steps in to say what she will not describe, so that the reunion at the station is given in a few words and left to the reader.

I am tired of calling Roberta by her name. I don't see why I should. No one else did. Everyone else called her Bobbie, and I don't see why I shouldn't.

Chapter 2
