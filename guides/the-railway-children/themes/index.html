Secrets

At the centre of the novel is a fact kept from three children, from two of them to the last page, and from the reader for two thirds of the book. Nesbit makes the withholding itself the action, so that tact, silence and the decision not to ask become the things her characters actually do.

Through the book The mother forbids questions in Chapter 1 and Bobbie immediately resolves not to know she has been crying; the unopened trunk of a man’s clothes in Chapter 5 is almost too much for her; the newspaper gives her the answer in Chapter 10; and in Chapter 11 the mother asks whether Bobbie will tell the others, hears ‘Because—’, and says ‘Exactly’, which is the whole book in one word.

In the text

“Father’s been called away—on business,” said Mother. “Come, darlings, go at once.”

Mother, Chapter 1

The dash is where the arrest is hidden. Everything before it is true and everything after it is a formula, and the pause in the middle is the only sign that she is putting the sentence together as she speaks. For nine chapters nothing said to the children is a lie, and nothing said to them is the truth either.

“Yes,” said Mother, “upon all prisoners and captives. All prisoners and captives.”

Mother, Chapter 5

The words come from the litany, where they are safely general, and the mother is using the form of a prayer to say something she cannot say in her own voice. The repetition matters: she says it once as instruction and once as her own petition, and the second time there is nobody in the sentence but her husband. Bobbie will quote it back in a letter six chapters later with the three words that close the circle: ‘I see now.’

Justice

Three people in this book are punished for things they did not do, or did not do in the way the charge says: a Russian writer sent to the mines for a book about the poor, a father convicted on letters somebody else put in his desk, and a boy who believes that taking coal from the top of the heap is mining. Nesbit is careful to show that the machinery of judgement is the same in each case, and that only influence moves it.

Through the book A Station Master decides privately to look over one case; the mother explains that English judges only think a man has done wrong; she says bitterly that nobody will listen to her; and what finally works is a rich acquaintance who had doubts when he read the papers; no legal appeal comes into it.

In the text

“I’m not a thief,” said Peter, as firmly as he could. “I’m a coal-miner.”

Peter, Chapter 2

Peter’s defence is a real argument (he has reasoned that the outside of the heap might be stealing and the middle only mining), and he is brave enough to make it while being held by the jacket. The Station Master’s reply is the book’s plainest sentence about property: stealing is stealing, and what’s mine isn’t yours, whether you call it mining or whether you don’t. Eight chapters later a jury will decide what to call something a different man did not do.

“Or when the Judges THINK they’ve done wrong,” said Mother. “Yes, that’s so in England. But in Russia it was different.”

Mother, Chapter 5

The most exposed sentence in the novel, and the children walk straight past it. She corrects her son’s idea of justice, concedes that the correction applies to England, and then covers the concession with a contrast that lets her go on talking about somewhere else. The capitals mark the stress she puts on THINK; the full stop after England is where her own case is, and she steps over it.

Poverty

The family’s poverty is real enough to mean jam or butter but not both, and unreal enough to be reversible, and Nesbit knows the difference better than her children do. The book’s hardest chapter sets their idea of a kind present against a porter’s idea of what a man loses when the village decides he cannot make both ends meet.

Through the book The pretty furniture is left behind and the useful furniture packed; Peter takes coal because they cannot afford a fire; the children are forbidden to ask strangers for things and go on doing it for other people; and Perks makes them read out every label before he will accept a bun.

In the text

“We’re taking the useful ones,” said Mother; “we’ve got to play at being Poor for a bit, my chickabiddy.”

Mother, Chapter 1

‘Play at’, ‘for a bit’ and a nursery endearment make three softenings in one short sentence, and the capital P on ‘Poor’ makes poverty a part in a game. The children take her at her word and hear ‘poor’ as a way of speaking, and it takes three wet days in June and a refused fire to teach them otherwise.

He was a very nice-looking old gentleman, and he looked as if he were nice, too, which is not at all the same thing.

Chapter 3

A whole theory of appearances in one sentence, and one of the few moments where Nesbit’s adult irony is aimed at her adult reader over the children’s heads. The distinction matters later: this is a book in which a man’s good looks and good standing are what finally get another man out of prison, and the narrator has taken care to note that the two things are usually confused.

Railway workers

Nesbit’s railway is a workplace before it is a playground, and almost everyone the children love is somebody at work: a porter cleaning lamps, an engine-driver explaining the injector, a signalman who has not slept in five days because his son has pneumonia and nobody can be found to take his duty. The mother’s writing is put on the same footing: so many pages, so many buns.

Through the book The whitewash line on the coal heap is there because coal gets stolen; Perks is paid in respect as well as wages; the signalman’s exhaustion is a staffing failure and no moral one; and the book’s comfortable ending is partly a woman being able to stop writing for money.

In the text

“Of course they can. Girls are just as clever as boys, and don’t you forget it! How would you like to be an engine-driver, Phil?”

Father, Chapter 1

This is almost the last thing the father says before the knock at the door, and Nesbit puts it where it will be remembered. It licenses everything the girls do afterwards (the petticoat flags, the ride in the cab, the nursing in the tunnel), and it sets up the novel’s one real disagreement with itself, when Dr Forrest explains to Peter, as science, that girls are softer and weaker and have to be.

“Well, you see, you must take one side or the other. Same as with Liberals and Conservatives. The great thing is to take your side and then stick to it, whatever happens.”

Perks, Chapter 6

This is the book’s only sustained piece of political comedy, and it is sharper than it looks. Perks has already said that ‘a man’s got to stand up for his own side whatever they does’, and that foreigners are ‘all tarred with the same brush’. Nesbit lets a porter explain loyalty to a side, whatever it does, four chapters before Bobbie learns what has happened to her father.

Home

A house that is first a dark lump with no supper in it becomes the place where a stranger is put to bed in another man’s night-shirt and an injured boy is carried in without anyone being asked. Nesbit measures the family’s recovery by how much of the house they can give away, and she ends the novel on a threshold, outside the house.

Through the book The arrival by candlelight; the forgotten room with the cold beef in it; the Russian in the chair that had been the father’s; the hospital with one patient; and the last page, where the narrator stands outside the back door and then walks off across the field.

In the text

They were not railway children to begin with.

Chapter 1

The title is contradicted in the first line, and the tense matters: ‘were not, to begin with’ implies a middle and an end already known to the teller. It also warns that the railway is something that happened to these children, which is the difference between this book and an adventure story about trains.

“Oh! my Daddy, my Daddy!” That scream went like a knife into the heart of everyone in the train, and people put their heads out of the windows to see a tall pale man with lips set in a thin close line, and a little girl clinging to him with arms and legs, while his arms went tightly round her.

Chapter 14

Nesbit holds back from the close-up. The reunion is shown from the carriage windows, at the distance of strangers, so that the reader is given the sound and then the picture other people see; the father is a tall pale man before he is anybody’s father, and his set lips record ten months that the book has declined to describe. It is the loudest moment in a novel that has been quiet on purpose, and it lasts one sentence.

The railway

The trains are named like beasts, spoken to, waved at and thanked, and the children’s affection for them is treated as a real relationship. It stays clear of whimsy because Nesbit also gives the machine its technical vocabulary (couplings, injector, automatic brake, the communication cord), so the dragon and the engine are the same object seen twice.

Through the book The 9.15 becomes the Green Dragon and the tunnel its lair; Bobbie is taught the brake by the driver she has frightened; the train she stops seems almost to have heard her; and on the last morning the whole train answers, which is the only moment the book lets the fancy come true.

In the text

“Oh!” said Roberta, drawing a long breath; “it was like a great dragon tearing by. Did you feel it fan us with its hot wings?”

Roberta, Chapter 2

The simile is the child’s, and Nesbit lets it stand, uncorrected, as a description of a real machine. Everything the book later does with the railway grows from here: the 9.15 becomes the Green Dragon, the tunnel becomes its lair, and Phyllis decides in the dark of Chapter 11 that a train takes its own shape in there. Nesbit never says the children are wrong; she simply also teaches them what an injector is.

“We never got any cherries, after all,” said Bobbie.

Bobbie, Chapter 6

The others think her rather heartless, and the narrator lets that judgement stand for a chapter before explaining that Bobbie wakes screaming for months afterwards. The line is displacement at its neatest: she cannot talk about the trustful engine rushing towards the mound, so she talks about fruit. It is the same manoeuvre her mother makes with Russia, learned from her without either of them noticing.

Charity and friendship

The children make friends across every line the period draws (a porter, a bargee, a doctor, a schoolboy, a director of the railway), and Nesbit is exact about what makes such a friendship possible and what breaks it. What matters is less the generosity than whether the person on the receiving end can accept without being diminished.

Through the book Peter apologises on a lonely road to a man who has caught him stealing; Perks is angrier at being pitied than at being ignored; a bargeman who has twisted Peter’s ear gives the children the freedom of nineteen locks; and a clergyman supplies the word Nesbit wants, loving-kindness.

In the text

It just means that a person is able to know that you are unhappy, and to love you extra on that account, without bothering you by telling you all the time how sorry she is for you.

Chapter 7

The definition is given in the plainest possible words and then qualified: ‘This needs practice. It is not so easy as you might think.’ Nesbit is praising a skill, and she treats tact as an achievement of the same order as stopping a train. The whole passage is addressed to ‘you’, so the reader is being taught as well as told.

“Oh, yes,” said Bobbie; “I think everyone in the world is friends if you can only get them to see you don’t want to be UN-friends.”

Bobbie, Chapter 8

Nesbit gives her heroine the book’s most hopeful proposition and then puts an adult sigh immediately after it, which is the most efficient piece of undercutting in the novel. Bobbie’s rule has worked on a bargee, a porter and a Station Master; it has not worked on a jury, and her mother knows it. The coined word UN-friends is the giveaway that this is still a child’s formulation.

The narrator

The story is told by someone who interrupts it. She admits preferences, addresses the reader, changes a character’s name because she has tired of the longer one, and says plainly when she is keeping something back. The children’s ignorance of their father is held in place by a teller who knows and chooses her moment.

Through the book The aside on Roberta’s name in Chapter 2 gives the eldest child two names, and the book uses both: Bobbie to her family, Roberta when she turns on herself. Later the narrator steps in to say what she will not describe, so that the reunion at the station is given in a few words and left to the reader.

In the text

I am tired of calling Roberta by her name. I don’t see why I should. No one else did. Everyone else called her Bobbie, and I don’t see why I shouldn’t.

Chapter 2

A narrator announcing that she has got bored of her own formality is a small joke, but it changes the book. From here the eldest child has two names, and Nesbit uses the split: Bobbie is the girl her family sees, Roberta the one she addresses when she is angry with herself in the tunnel. The aside also establishes that this teller will interrupt, admit preferences and keep back whatever she likes.
