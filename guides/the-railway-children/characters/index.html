The Railway Children · E. Nesbit

Characters

Roberta (Bobbie)

The eldest child; the one who is told; first appears Chapter 1

She is eleven at the start and twelve by her birthday, and her distinguishing gift is tact, more than courage: she works out early that her mother is unhappy, decides not to find out why, and goes on deciding it for ten chapters. When the newspaper hands her the answer she carries it alone, which makes her the only child in the book living in the adult world and the only one who cannot say so.

Peter

The middle child, aged ten at the start; would be an engineer; first appears Chapter 1

He is the one who acts and then works out afterwards whether he was right. He takes coal from the station heap on a fine distinction between mining and stealing, goes back to apologise when the distinction collapses, and later knocks two shillings out of a signalman’s hand for offering them. His vanity is drawn exactly: he must have led the forlorn hope, and he must be told he is not a coward.

Phyllis

The youngest; the one who means extremely well; first appears Chapter 1

Introduced in half a sentence as the child who meant extremely well, and kept to it: her bootlace is always undone, she upsets the lemonade, she crushes the clay nest she made. She is also the only one who forgives the signalman out loud, and the only one who says plainly that she was asking for the strawberries when Perks accuses her of it, because she knows she will not like herself otherwise.

Mother

The children’s mother; a writer of stories by necessity; first appears Chapter 1

She is never given a name and is the most controlled figure in the book. She laughs while she is tucking Peter up on the drawing-room sofa on the last night in the old house, writes all day to buy the mutton, will not let the children accept a stranger’s hamper, and tells them nothing. Her one slip is the sentence about English judges thinking a man has done wrong, said to three children who do not know that a jury has just thought it of their father.

Father

A clerk in a Government Office, convicted of selling State secrets; first appears Chapter 1

He is present for one evening and one chapter: mending a toy engine, telling his daughters that girls are as clever as boys, joking about moats and drawbridges. Then he is a cab door closing. Everything afterwards reaches the reader at second or third hand (an unopened trunk of his clothes, a name in a newspaper column, a letter that does not arrive) until he walks off the 11.54 with his lips set in a thin close line.

Albert Perks

The porter at the station; thirty-two, married, three children; first appears Chapter 3

He answers questions beginning with ‘Why’, which people in higher ranks of life often seem weary of, and he is the book’s sharpest instrument for measuring class. He is offended when the children keep the Russian’s story from him, and far more offended by a heap of birthday presents, because a man who has kept out of debt on a porter’s wages cannot afford to be an object of charity in his own village.

The old gentleman

A daily passenger on the 9.15; a director of the railway; first appears Chapter 3

He is never named, by the children or by the narrator, who keeps that as the one secret the book will not give up. He waves back, sends a hamper, finds an exile’s family, gives three gold watches and, without being asked, starts looking into a conviction he had doubted when he read about it. His power is money and acquaintance, and Nesbit lets him use both without ever making a speech about either.

The Russian gentleman (Szezcpansky)

A writer imprisoned and exiled for a book about the poor; first appears Chapter 5

He arrives without a ticket, without English, and without any idea where in England his wife and children are. Three years in a dungeon, a chained march to Siberia, the mines for life, and a desertion during the war have left him crying at a country station. The children’s mother tells his story to her own children with the word Russia doing a great deal of work, and none of them notices what it is standing in for.

Jim

A schoolboy from Maidbridge; the hound in the red jersey; first appears Chapter 11

He trips on a wire in the tunnel, breaks his leg, and is carried up to Three Chimneys because Bobbie is certain her mother would want it. His arrival converts the house into a hospital and his grandfather’s money into a cook, a housemaid and a rest for a woman who has been writing to feed three children. He is also, at first, the only person in the book who does not know anything about the family.

The Station Master

Mr Gills, who keeps the station and the inner temple behind the ticket window; first appears Chapter 2

He catches Peter on the coal heap in a pair of old seaside sand shoes, tells him that stealing is stealing, and then lets him go and never mentions it again. He is the local representative of authority throughout (official with a crowd, doubtful about a foreigner without a ticket, formal about affairs of State), and he is also the man who wrings Bobbie’s hand like a pump-handle on the last morning without explaining why.

Dr W. W. Forrest

The village doctor, and rather a poor one himself; first appears Chapter 3

He orders grapes, beef essence and the best brandy for a patient who cannot pay for any of it, and when Bobbie asks whether the family can join a club like Mrs Viney’s he agrees to invent one. He is also the man who takes Peter for a walk and explains, as science, that girls are softer and weaker and must be handled carefully, a speech the novel has already contradicted twice and will contradict again.
