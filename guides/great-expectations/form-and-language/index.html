Great Expectations · Charles Dickens

Form and language

Retrospective first person

A narrator recalling their own past, so that experience and judgement arrive together.

The older Pip supplies verdicts the younger one could not reach (“It is a most miserable thing to feel ashamed of home”) and occasionally addresses the reader outright to make the moral general.

My father’s family name being Pirrip, and my Christian name Philip, my infant tongue could make of both names nothing longer or more explicit than Pip. So, I called myself Pip, and came to be called Pip.

Chapter 1

Landscape and mood

Weather and place used to carry the emotional weight of a scene.

Mists rise as Pip leaves the forge and rise again in the last sentence of the book; the marshes are flat, wet and open, and the first thing they teach him is the identity of things.

My first most vivid and broad impression of the identity of things seems to me to have been gained on a memorable raw afternoon towards evening.

Chapter 1

Symbolic objects

A physical thing that returns at intervals, gathering meaning each time.

The file stolen for Magwitch reappears in a stranger’s hands, and the leg-iron it cut through becomes the weapon used on Mrs Joe; the bride-cake, the stopped clocks and the single shoe do the same work at Satis House.

“You get me a file.” He tilted me again. “And you get me wittles.” He tilted me again. “You bring ’em both to me.”

Magwitch, Chapter 1

Doubling

Two characters built so that each exposes something in the other.

Orlick voices Pip’s own resentments back at him in the sluice-house; Magwitch and Miss Havisham are two benefactors making two children; Compeyson is the gentleman Magwitch is punished for not being.

Miss Havisham would embrace her with lavish fondness, murmuring something in her ear that sounded like “Break their hearts my pride and hope, break their hearts and have no mercy!”

Miss Havisham, Chapter 12

Speech and class

Giving each character a distinct vocabulary, grammar and rhythm.

Joe’s tangled syntax, Magwitch’s ‘wittles’, Jaggers’s refusal of any sentence that is not evidence and Wemmick’s two registers do more social work than any description in the novel.

“You get me a file.” He tilted me again. “And you get me wittles.” He tilted me again. “You bring ’em both to me.”

Magwitch, Chapter 1

Weekly serial structure

Chapters shaped to end an instalment with a reason to return.

Written for thirty-six weekly numbers instead of monthly parts, the book is unusually tight for Dickens, and its two great reversals both fall at the end of a stage.
