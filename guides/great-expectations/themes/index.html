Becoming a gentleman

Becoming a gentleman in this book is a change of accent, boots and address, paid for by someone else, and no moral improvement at all. Dickens sets Pip’s education against Joe’s illiteracy and Biddy’s self-teaching, and notes without fuss that the least educated characters make the fewest mistakes about how to behave.

Through the book The shame begins with a pair of hands in Chapter 8, becomes an income in Chapter 18, and is exposed as convict money in Chapter 39.

In the text

I took the opportunity of being alone in the courtyard to look at my coarse hands and my common boots. My opinion of those accessories was not favorable. They had never troubled me before, but they troubled me now, as vulgar appendages.

Chapter 8

Pip takes Estella’s adjectives for his own within a page. The older narrator marks the moment a happy childhood became a grievance.

“There’s one thing you may be sure of, Pip,” said Joe, after some rumination, “namely, that lies is lies. Howsever they come, they didn’t ought to come, and they come from the father of lies, and work round to the same.

Joe Gargery, Chapter 9

Joe’s grammar is wrong in every clause and his reasoning cannot be answered. He also throws out the scale Pip has just begun to measure himself by.

“Yes, Pip, dear boy, I’ve made a gentleman on you! It’s me wot has done it! I swore that time, sure as ever I earned a guinea, that guinea should go to you.

Magwitch, Chapter 39

The gentleman was paid for in the grammar Pip learned to be ashamed of, and “made” turns him into a thing somebody manufactured.

Guilt, crime and complicity

Pip’s first act is a theft committed under threat, and he never stops feeling like a criminal. Dickens keeps him close to the law (Newgate, the hulks, Jaggers’s office, the condemned cell), so that the gap between the gentleman and the convict never properly opens.

Through the book The stolen pork pie leads to a man who takes the blame; the man leads to the money; the money leads Pip back to a prison, where he ends up praying for a dying convict.

In the text

“You get me a file.” He tilted me again. “And you get me wittles.” He tilted me again. “You bring ’em both to me.”

Magwitch, Chapter 1

Pip’s first act is a theft under threat, and what he brings comes back: the file in a stranger’s hand, and the leg-iron it cut through as the weapon used on his sister.

he would wash his hands, and wipe them and dry them all over this towel, whenever he came in from a police court or dismissed a client from his room

Chapter 26

Jaggers washes after every client and never states an opinion about guilt. The gesture is Pilate’s.

I thought of the two men who went up into the Temple to pray, and I knew there were no better words that I could say beside his bed, than “O Lord, be merciful to him a sinner!”

Chapter 56

Pip ends praying beside a dying convict, and the prayer he chooses is the humble publican’s from the parable.

Class and money

Every fortune in the book has a source somebody would rather not name: Miss Havisham’s brewery, Magwitch’s sheep and his dead master’s legacy, Jaggers’s fees from the dock. The novel will not accept that money can be laundered by manners, and Magwitch’s return is the plot making that argument in person.

Through the book Pip’s debts rise with his status; his one honourable transaction, buying Herbert’s partnership, is the one he keeps secret.

In the text

“He calls the knaves Jacks, this boy!” said Estella with disdain, before our first game was out. “And what coarse hands he has! And what thick boots!”

Estella, Chapter 8

Estella finds contempt in his words, his hands and his boots in three sentences, and speaks about him, never to him.

“Mr. Pip,” he replied, with gravity, “Walworth is one place, and this office is another. Much as the Aged is one person, and Mr. Jaggers is another. They must not be confounded together.

Wemmick, Chapter 36

Wemmick keeps office and home apart as a rule. Pip does the same with London and the forge, and calls it shame.

Love and harm

Miss Havisham’s instruction to Pip is to love Estella whatever it does to him, and to keep loving as it tears deeper. Estella tells him plainly that she has no heart and will hurt him. Love in this novel is usually something done to a person; it is rarely something between two people.

Through the book The engineered attachment of the Satis House visits becomes an obsession Pip can defend only by admitting it is unreasonable, and Miss Havisham’s collapse in Chapter 49 is the moment she recognises what she built.

In the text

“Love her, love her, love her! If she favors you, love her. If she wounds you, love her. If she tears your heart to pieces⁠—and as it gets older and stronger it will tear deeper⁠—love her, love her, love her!”

Miss Havisham, Chapter 29

Nine commands, arranged so that being favoured and being wounded call for the same response. Miss Havisham prescribes a wound and calls it love.

“You must know,” said Estella, condescending to me as a brilliant and beautiful woman might, “that I have no heart⁠—if that has anything to do with my memory.”

Estella, Chapter 29

Estella warns him plainly, and suggests that feeling and memory were both denied her. Pip hears only a coquette’s line.

I knew to my sorrow, often and often, if not always, that I loved her against reason, against promise, against peace, against hope, against happiness, against all discouragement that could be.

Chapter 29

Six times “against”, and no reason on the other side. The older Pip admits his love as a fact he could not argue with.

Parents and substitutes

Neither Pip nor Estella is raised by a parent. Pip has a sister, a brother-in-law, a guardian and two self-appointed benefactors; Estella has a jilted bride for a mother and a convict for a father. The recurring question is whether a child is made by design, and whether the designer can be blamed.

Through the book Miss Havisham’s “What have I done!” and Magwitch’s “I’m your second father” come from opposite ends of the same claim, that a person can be manufactured, and the novel judges them differently.

In the text

“Look’ee here, Pip. I’m your second father. You’re my son⁠—more to me nor any son. I’ve put away money, only for you to spend.

Magwitch, Chapter 39

Magwitch claims Pip as a son, and the claim holds: nobody else has spent a life on him. “Second” also puts him behind Joe.

“So,” said Estella, “I must be taken as I have been made. The success is not mine, the failure is not mine, but the two together make me.”

Estella, Chapter 38

Estella takes neither the credit nor the blame. She was made, and she says so in the passive voice.

“O!” she cried, despairingly. “What have I done! What have I done!”

Miss Havisham, Chapter 49

Miss Havisham sees what she has built in Estella, and cries the words over and over.

Memory and the older narrator

The story is told by a man who already knows the answer to every question the boy is asking. That gap is the novel’s method: the reader gets the misjudgement and the verdict on it in the same sentence, which is why the snobbery can be shown at full strength without being endorsed.

Through the book The narrator stops at the end of Chapter 9 to name a memorable day and the long chain it begins, and turns to the reader directly at the points where the younger Pip behaves worst.

In the text

My first most vivid and broad impression of the identity of things seems to me to have been gained on a memorable raw afternoon towards evening.

Chapter 1

A child’s first sense of the world is dated to the afternoon a man threatened to cut his throat.

Pause you who read this, and think for a moment of the long chain of iron or gold, of thorns or flowers, that would never have bound you, but for the formation of the first link on one memorable day.

Chapter 9

The older narrator stops the story to speak to the reader, and declines to say whether Pip’s chain was iron or gold.

“Lookee here, old chap,” said Joe, bending over me. “Ever the best of friends; ain’t us, Pip?”

Joe Gargery, Chapter 57

Joe comforts the grown man with the words he used for the crying child. His speech has not changed; Pip’s has been remade at great expense.
