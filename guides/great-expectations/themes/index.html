Becoming a gentleman

Becoming a gentleman in this book is a change of accent, boots and address, paid for by someone else, and no moral improvement at all. Dickens sets Pip’s education against Joe’s illiteracy and Biddy’s self-teaching, and notes without fuss that the least educated characters make the fewest mistakes about how to behave.

Through the book The shame begins with a pair of hands in Chapter 8, becomes an income in Chapter 18, and is exposed as convict money in Chapter 39.

In the text

“He calls the knaves Jacks, this boy!” said Estella with disdain, before our first game was out. “And what coarse hands he has! And what thick boots!”

Estella, Chapter 8

Estella finds three grounds for contempt in three sentences: vocabulary, body and clothing. She never speaks to Pip in this scene, only about him, and the whole novel’s machinery of shame is installed here with a card game and a piece of vocabulary Joe taught him.

“Yes, Pip, dear boy, I’ve made a gentleman on you! It’s me wot has done it! I swore that time, sure as ever I earned a guinea, that guinea should go to you.

Magwitch, Chapter 39

The grammar Pip has spent three hundred pages learning to be ashamed of is the grammar that paid for the lessons. ‘Made a gentleman on you’ is a maker’s claim over an object, and Pip is horrified by the source of the money and also by being the thing that was made.

Guilt, crime and complicity

Pip’s first act is a theft committed under threat, and he never stops feeling like a criminal. Dickens keeps him close to the law (Newgate, the hulks, Jaggers’s office, the condemned cell), so that the gap between the gentleman and the convict never properly opens.

Through the book The stolen pork pie leads to a man who takes the blame; the man leads to the money; the money leads Pip back to a prison, where he ends up praying for a dying convict.

In the text

“Hold your noise!” cried a terrible voice, as a man started up from among the graves at the side of the church porch. “Keep still, you little devil, or I’ll cut your throat!”

Magwitch, Chapter 1

The man rises out of the family graves, which puts him structurally in the place of Pip’s dead parents some four hundred pages before the plot gets there. He is also, at this moment, simply a voice: Dickens gives the sound before the body, so that the marshes seem to produce him.

he would wash his hands, and wipe them and dry them all over this towel, whenever he came in from a police court or dismissed a client from his room

Chapter 26

The ritual is Pilate’s, performed several times a day by a man who never states an opinion about guilt. Dickens attaches the smell of the soap to him everywhere he goes, so that the reader is reminded of what is being washed off at exactly the moments Jaggers is at his most impressive.

Class and money

Every fortune in the book has a source somebody would rather not name: Miss Havisham’s brewery, Magwitch’s sheep and his dead master’s legacy, Jaggers’s fees from the dock. The novel will not accept that money can be laundered by manners, and Magwitch’s return is the plot making that argument in person.

Through the book Pip’s debts rise with his status; his one honourable transaction, buying Herbert’s partnership, is the one he keeps secret.

In the text

It is a most miserable thing to feel ashamed of home. There may be black ingratitude in the thing, and the punishment may be retributive and well deserved; but that it is a miserable thing, I can testify.

Chapter 14

The older narrator concedes the charge against the boy in full (ingratitude, deserved punishment) and then insists on the misery anyway, in the language of a witness box. It is the book’s characteristic move: guilt admitted so completely that pity becomes possible.

“Mr. Pip,” he replied, with gravity, “Walworth is one place, and this office is another. Much as the Aged is one person, and Mr. Jaggers is another. They must not be confounded together.

Wemmick, Chapter 36

He states as a rule of hygiene what the rest of the novel suffers as a problem: that the person you are at work and the person you are at home are not obliged to agree. Pip has spent the book trying to keep the forge and London apart and calling it shame; Wemmick does the same thing and calls it an address.

Love and harm

Miss Havisham’s instruction to Pip is to love Estella whatever it does to him, and to keep loving as it tears deeper. Estella tells him plainly that she has no heart and will hurt him. Love in this novel is usually something done to a person; it is rarely something between two people.

Through the book The engineered attachment of the Satis House visits becomes an obsession Pip can defend only by admitting it is unreasonable, and Miss Havisham’s collapse in Chapter 49 is the moment she recognises what she built.

In the text

Miss Havisham would embrace her with lavish fondness, murmuring something in her ear that sounded like “Break their hearts my pride and hope, break their hearts and have no mercy!”

Miss Havisham, Chapter 12

Pip reports it as something that ‘sounded like’ the words, so the most damning line in Estella’s upbringing reaches the reader through a child who did not quite catch it. The endearments and the instruction are in the same breath: pride and hope are what she calls the girl she is training to be unable to love.

“O!” she cried, despairingly. “What have I done! What have I done!”

Miss Havisham, Chapter 49

Pip says she cried it ‘twenty, fifty times over’, and when she is dying she repeats it with two other sentences, never changing their order. She had asked him to write ‘I forgive her’ under her name, and the whole scene is a woman trying to get a sentence exactly right after twenty-five years of leaving everything unfinished.

Parents and substitutes

Neither Pip nor Estella is raised by a parent. Pip has a sister, a brother-in-law, a guardian and two self-appointed benefactors; Estella has a jilted bride for a mother and a convict for a father. The recurring question is whether a child is made by design, and whether the designer can be blamed.

Through the book Miss Havisham’s “What have I done!” and Magwitch’s “I’m your second father” come from opposite ends of the same claim, that a person can be manufactured, and the novel judges them differently.

In the text

My father’s family name being Pirrip, and my Christian name Philip, my infant tongue could make of both names nothing longer or more explicit than Pip. So, I called myself Pip, and came to be called Pip.

Chapter 1

He names himself in the first line, which is the only act of self-creation in the book that works. Everything after this is somebody else’s attempt to name him (Handel, my boy, sir, a gentleman), and the final clause, ‘came to be called’, already concedes that identity is something other people do to you.

“So,” said Estella, “I must be taken as I have been made. The success is not mine, the failure is not mine, but the two together make me.”

Estella, Chapter 38

The passive voice is exact and deliberate: she has been made, and she declines both the credit and the blame. It is also an answer to the whole Victorian plot of self-improvement that Pip is living out three streets away: one character is trying to make himself, and the other knows she was manufactured.

Memory and the older narrator

The story is told by a man who already knows the answer to every question the boy is asking. That gap is the novel’s method: the reader gets the misjudgement and the verdict on it in the same sentence, which is why the snobbery can be shown at full strength without being endorsed.

Through the book The narrator stops at the end of Chapter 9 to name a memorable day and the long chain it begins, and turns to the reader directly at the points where the younger Pip behaves worst.

In the text

My first most vivid and broad impression of the identity of things seems to me to have been gained on a memorable raw afternoon towards evening.

Chapter 1

A child’s first understanding of the world is dated to the afternoon a man threatened to cut his throat, and the phrase for it is legal and exact. ‘The identity of things’ is what the rest of the novel takes away from him: nothing turns out to be what it was labelled.

Pause you who read this, and think for a moment of the long chain of iron or gold, of thorns or flowers, that would never have bound you, but for the formation of the first link on one memorable day.

Chapter 9

The narrator stops the story to address the reader directly, and reaches for a chain, an image already attached to leg-irons and, in Dickens’s own earlier Christmas book, to a dead man’s punishment. Offering iron or gold, thorns or flowers, he declines to say which Pip’s turned out to be.
