Great Expectations · Charles Dickens

Themes

Becoming a gentleman

Becoming a gentleman in this book is a change of accent, boots and address, paid for by someone else, and no moral improvement at all. Dickens sets Pip’s education against Joe’s illiteracy and Biddy’s self-teaching, and notes without fuss that the least educated characters make the fewest mistakes about how to behave.

The shame begins with a pair of hands in Chapter 8, becomes an income in Chapter 18, and is exposed as convict money in Chapter 39.

“He calls the knaves Jacks, this boy!” said Estella with disdain, before our first game was out. “And what coarse hands he has! And what thick boots!”

Estella, Chapter 8

I took the opportunity of being alone in the courtyard to look at my coarse hands and my common boots. My opinion of those accessories was not favorable. They had never troubled me before, but they troubled me now, as vulgar appendages.

Chapter 8

Guilt, crime and complicity

Pip’s first act is a theft committed under threat, and he never stops feeling like a criminal. Dickens keeps him close to the law (Newgate, the hulks, Jaggers’s office, the condemned cell), so that the gap between the gentleman and the convict never properly opens.

The stolen pork pie leads to a man who takes the blame; the man leads to the money; the money leads Pip back to a prison, where he ends up praying for a dying convict.

“Hold your noise!” cried a terrible voice, as a man started up from among the graves at the side of the church porch. “Keep still, you little devil, or I’ll cut your throat!”

Magwitch, Chapter 1

“You get me a file.” He tilted me again. “And you get me wittles.” He tilted me again. “You bring ’em both to me.”

Magwitch, Chapter 1

Class and money

Every fortune in the book has a source somebody would rather not name: Miss Havisham’s brewery, Magwitch’s sheep and his dead master’s legacy, Jaggers’s fees from the dock. The novel will not accept that money can be laundered by manners, and Magwitch’s return is the plot making that argument in person.

Pip’s debts rise with his status; his one honourable transaction, buying Herbert’s partnership, is the one he keeps secret.

“He calls the knaves Jacks, this boy!” said Estella with disdain, before our first game was out. “And what coarse hands he has! And what thick boots!”

Estella, Chapter 8

It is a most miserable thing to feel ashamed of home. There may be black ingratitude in the thing, and the punishment may be retributive and well deserved; but that it is a miserable thing, I can testify.

Chapter 14

Love and harm

Miss Havisham’s instruction to Pip is to love Estella whatever it does to him, and to keep loving as it tears deeper. Estella tells him plainly that she has no heart and will hurt him. Love in this novel is usually something done to a person; it is rarely something between two people.

The engineered attachment of the Satis House visits becomes an obsession Pip can defend only by admitting it is unreasonable, and Miss Havisham’s collapse in Chapter 49 is the moment she recognises what she built.

Miss Havisham would embrace her with lavish fondness, murmuring something in her ear that sounded like “Break their hearts my pride and hope, break their hearts and have no mercy!”

Miss Havisham, Chapter 12

“Love her, love her, love her! If she favors you, love her. If she wounds you, love her. If she tears your heart to pieces⁠—and as it gets older and stronger it will tear deeper⁠—love her, love her, love her!”

Miss Havisham, Chapter 29

Parents and substitutes

Neither Pip nor Estella is raised by a parent. Pip has a sister, a brother-in-law, a guardian and two self-appointed benefactors; Estella has a jilted bride for a mother and a convict for a father. The recurring question is whether a child is made by design, and whether the designer can be blamed.

Miss Havisham’s “What have I done!” and Magwitch’s “I’m your second father” come from opposite ends of the same claim, that a person can be manufactured, and the novel judges them differently.

My father’s family name being Pirrip, and my Christian name Philip, my infant tongue could make of both names nothing longer or more explicit than Pip. So, I called myself Pip, and came to be called Pip.

Chapter 1

“Hold your noise!” cried a terrible voice, as a man started up from among the graves at the side of the church porch. “Keep still, you little devil, or I’ll cut your throat!”

Magwitch, Chapter 1

Memory and the older narrator

The story is told by a man who already knows the answer to every question the boy is asking. That gap is the novel’s method: the reader gets the misjudgement and the verdict on it in the same sentence, which is why the snobbery can be shown at full strength without being endorsed.

The narrator stops at the end of Chapter 9 to name a memorable day and the long chain it begins, and turns to the reader directly at the points where the younger Pip behaves worst.

My father’s family name being Pirrip, and my Christian name Philip, my infant tongue could make of both names nothing longer or more explicit than Pip. So, I called myself Pip, and came to be called Pip.

Chapter 1

My first most vivid and broad impression of the identity of things seems to me to have been gained on a memorable raw afternoon towards evening.
