Great Expectations · Charles Dickens

Characters

Pip

Philip Pirrip; narrator, blacksmith’s apprentice, then a gentleman on someone else’s money; first appears Chapter 1

Two people in one: the boy who behaves badly and the man telling you about it, who supplies the judgements the boy could not. The book’s great difficulty is that his snobbery is presented from inside, so the reader is made to feel the shame of the thick boots before being invited to despise him for feeling it.

Joe Gargery

Blacksmith, married to Pip’s sister; first appears Chapter 1

He cannot spell, will not raise his hand in his own house, and is the moral centre of the novel without ever being told so. His explanation of why he allows his wife to rule (his own father beat his mother, and he would rather be hit himself) is the best piece of reasoning anyone in the book manages.

Mrs Joe Gargery

Pip’s sister, twenty years older; first appears Chapter 2

She has brought Pip up by hand and never stops saying so; the phrase covers both the rearing and the beating. Dickens complicates the caricature by having her, after Orlick’s attack, become mild and anxious to please the man who hurt her, which is harder to read than the tyranny was.

Biddy

Schoolmistress’s granddaughter, later Joe’s wife; first appears Chapter 7

She teaches Pip everything he learns before Jaggers arrives and tells him the truth about his conduct to his face. The novel gives her one of its sharpest lines, when she asks Pip whether he has never considered that Joe may be proud, and then rewards her with the life Pip threw away.

Estella

Miss Havisham’s adopted daughter; first appears Chapter 8

Trained from childhood to break hearts, and honest about it to the one person who will not listen: she tells Pip repeatedly and in plain terms that she has no heart and will do him harm. The plot’s cruellest turn is that her real parents are a convict and a murderess.

Miss Havisham

Of Satis House, jilted on her wedding morning; first appears Chapter 8

She has stopped the clocks, kept on one shoe and left the cake to the mice, and she is aware the whole arrangement is theatre. What she has done to Estella is worse than what was done to her, and she says so herself before the fire: she wanted to save a child from misery and made one instead.

Abel Magwitch

Transported convict; Pip’s benefactor, under the name Provis; first appears Chapter 1

He begins as a threat in a graveyard and ends as the one character who has given everything he had for another person. His speech about making a gentleman is the book’s hinge: he has bought Pip the way a man buys a racehorse, out of love, and what revolts Pip is being owned, and being owned by a working man.

Mr Jaggers

Criminal lawyer of Little Britain; first appears Chapter 11

He bites his forefinger, never admits a fact and washes his hands after every client. The routine is the only defence available to a man who knows exactly where the money in London comes from, and who has secretly saved one woman from the gallows and placed one child in a rich house.

Wemmick

Jaggers’s clerk; proprietor of the Castle at Walworth; first appears Chapter 21

He states as policy what everyone else in the novel does by accident: that the self at work and the self at home are different people and must not be confounded. At the office he collects portable property from condemned men; at Walworth he fires a gun at nine so his deaf father can feel it.

Herbert Pocket

Pip’s friend and fellow lodger at Barnard’s Inn; first appears Chapter 11

The pale young gentleman Pip fought in a garden, now the one person in London who improves him. He is hopeless at making money and excellent at everything else, and the best thing Pip does in the novel is to buy him a partnership in secret and never tell him.

Dolge Orlick

Journeyman at the forge; first appears Chapter 15

He is what Pip’s resentments would look like if they were acted on. He attacks Mrs Joe with the convict’s leg-iron, and in the sluice-house he accuses Pip of the very things Pip accuses himself of: that he came between a man and his prospects, and that he was always the favourite.
