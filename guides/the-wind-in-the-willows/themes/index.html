Home

Every animal in the book is defined by a dwelling (a hole in a bank, a house under a wood, a hall with a banqueting room), and the plot repeatedly takes one away. Grahame’s most serious writing is about the moment a house you abandoned calls you back, and about how difficult it is to admit that the call has been heard.

Through the book Mole leaves his house in Chapter 1 without a backward look, is ambushed by the smell of it in Chapter 5, and by Chapter 12 is planning the recovery of somebody else’s.

In the text

Spring was moving in the air above and in the earth below and around him, penetrating even his dark and lowly little house with its spirit of divine discontent and longing.

Chapter 1

The season is the subject of the sentence and Mole merely the place it reaches, which sets up a book in which characters are moved by weather, smells and calls more than by decisions. ‘Divine discontent’ is a phrase from the pulpit and the essay, and it arrives four lines before the word ‘Bother!’, so the two registers of the whole novel are set out on its first page.

Home! That was what they meant, those caressing appeals, those soft touches wafted through the air, those invisible little hands pulling and tugging, all one way!

Chapter 5

The summons is written entirely as touch (appeals, touches, hands), so homesickness arrives as a physical assault before it is a thought. Grahame describes the summons for a whole paragraph before the word ‘Home!’ explains it, which reproduces the order in which Mole himself works it out.

Wandering

Against the riverbank sits everything outside it: the Wide World Rat refuses to discuss, the open road Toad takes up as a fashion, and the southern ports the Sea Rat describes until Rat starts packing. The book is honest enough to make the case for leaving eloquent, and then to treat it as an illness to be nursed.

Through the book Refused as a topic in Chapter 1, played as farce by Toad in Chapter 2, and taken seriously enough in Chapter 9 to require physical restraint and a sheet of paper.

In the text

“Beyond the Wild Wood comes the Wide World,” said the Rat. “And that’s something that doesn’t matter, either to you or me. I’ve never been there, and I’m never going, nor you either, if you’ve got any sense at all. Don’t ever refer to it again, please.

Water Rat, Chapter 1

Rat answers a question about geography with a prohibition, and the sentence that closes the subject, ‘Don’t ever refer to it again, please’, is the politest thing in the book and the most frightened. Chapter 9 collects on it: the animal who will not discuss the world outside turns out to be the one most easily carried off by a stranger’s description of it.

“And you, you will come too, young brother; for the days pass, and never return, and the South still waits for you. Take the adventure, heed the call, now ere the irrevocable moment passes!

The Sea Rat, Chapter 9

The rhythm is deliberately liturgical (three clauses, then a command, then a warning), and it works on Rat because he has no counter-vocabulary for it. Set beside Chapter 7, the chapter is unnerving: the same trance-like language that produced a god here produces a travel brochure.

Friendship

Loyalty here looks like supervision: Rat feeds and teaches Mole, Badger and Rat put Toad under house arrest, and Mole holds a struggling friend down on the floor until the fit passes. Affection in this book is rarely declared and almost always expressed as taking charge of somebody.

Through the book Hospitality in Chapter 1, rescue in Chapter 3, house arrest in Chapter 6, physical restraint in Chapter 9, and a military operation in Chapter 12.

In the text

We river-bankers, we hardly ever come here by ourselves. If we have to come, we come in couples at least; then we’re generally all right.

Water Rat, Chapter 3

‘We river-bankers’ is the first time the book names a class, and it does so to explain where that class does not go. The reassurance is really a set of rules of caste (passwords, signs, sayings which have power and effect), and the Wild Wood is dangerous chiefly because it belongs to animals who have not been introduced.

“Badger’s was the master mind; the Mole and the Water Rat bore the brunt of the fighting; I merely served in the ranks and did little or nothing.”

Toad, Chapter 12

It is an accurate account of the battle, delivered by the one animal who has never given an accurate account of anything. The modesty is a new performance by the same Toad, and the narrator confirms it two lines later by noting how closely Toad watches Badger and Rat for their reaction.

Class

Toad has a hall, a boathouse and a solicitor; the Wild Wooders have holes and no names. The novel’s comedy of a gentleman in a washerwoman’s dress depends on the assumption that women and stoats alike are types more than people, and its happy ending restores a landowner to his property by force.

Through the book Signalled in the ‘Private. No landing allowed’ notice-board in Chapter 2, tested in the barge-woman’s kitchen, and settled at the point of a cudgel in Chapter 12.

In the text

“Ho, ho!” he said to himself, as he marched along with his chin in the air, “what a clever Toad I am! There is surely no animal equal to me for cleverness in the whole world!

Toad, Chapter 10

Every escape in the second half has been arranged by somebody else (a girl with a dress, an engine-driver, a Gypsy with cash), and Toad’s self-congratulation edits all of them out. The narrator lets him do it at length and then puts him in a river, which is the book’s standard method of argument.

The Toad⁠—came⁠—home!

Toad, Chapter 12

The dashes are stage directions for a singer pausing to be admired, and the third person gives him away: Toad writes about himself as a public event. The chapter is called ‘The Return of Ulysses’, and the returning hero’s first act is to compose his own ballad.

The motorcar

The car arrives first as a noise, then as a cloud of dust, then as a court case, and the book records what it does to everyone else on the road: a horse in the ditch, a cart on its side, a canary in a cage. Grahame published in the years when speed limits, licences and registration plates had just become law.

Through the book A hum like a distant bee in Chapter 2, an obsession by the end of it, twenty years’ imprisonment in Chapter 6, and in Chapter 11 a claim to be less keen on motorcars, followed at once by a brilliant idea about motor-boats.

In the text

They found him in a sort of a trance, a happy smile on his face, his eyes still fixed on the dusty wake of their destroyer. At intervals he was still heard to murmur “Poop-poop!”

Chapter 2

Toad has kept nothing of the car but its sound, and he repeats it as though it were already his: he has learned the vocabulary of the new machine before he has touched one. ‘Their destroyer’ is the narrator’s phrase, and with it the wrecked cart and the terrified horse stay in view while the joke is being made.

He was indeed an altered Toad!

Chapter 12

One sentence, one paragraph, and an exclamation mark that could be admiration or disbelief. The reform is real enough to be observed and light enough to be withdrawn, which is why the book can end with a promise about motorcars that nobody in the room believes.

Wonder

One chapter steps outside the comedy altogether: two animals hear piping at dawn, see something they can only call the Friend and Helper, and are then given oblivion as a kindness. The book will not let awe be kept, catalogued or used later, which is why the experience leaves no mark on the plot.

Through the book Announced by music on an island in Chapter 7, granted and immediately withdrawn, and echoed faintly in the Sea Rat’s spell two chapters later.

In the text

he looked in the very eyes of the Friend and Helper; saw the backward sweep of the curved horns, gleaming in the growing daylight; saw the stern, hooked nose between the kindly eyes that were looking down on them humorously

Chapter 7

The god is never named in the chapter, only titled, and the description rests on a repeated ‘saw’, so that looking becomes a liturgy. Note the adverb ‘humorously’ dropped into the middle of an awe-struck sentence: even here Grahame will not let the sublime arrive without a domestic detail to steady it.

“Afraid?” murmured the Rat, his eyes shining with unutterable love. “Afraid! Of Him? O, never, never! And yet⁠—and yet⁠—O, Mole, I am afraid!”

Water Rat, Chapter 7

The speech contradicts itself twice and means all three positions, which is an exact account of religious awe. Rat, the competent one, stumbles in mid-sentence; when he does so elsewhere it is from impatience or tact, never from awe.
