The Wind in the Willows · Kenneth Grahame

Characters

Mole

A householder who leaves his house; first appears Chapter 1

He begins the book as the reader’s proxy (ignorant, enthusiastic and easily impressed) and ends it as the animal who thinks of the tunnel and holds the line in the fight. His crisis in Chapter 5 is the emotional centre of the novel: he is the one who discovers that leaving home does not end the claim it has on you.

Water Rat

Riverbank poet, boatman and host; first appears Chapter 1

He is competent in everything the river requires and incurious about everything beyond it, and the book loves him for the first and tests him on the second. His collapse in Chapter 9, when a wandering Sea Rat almost carries him off southward, shows how thin the difference is between a settled life and a refusal to look up.

Toad of Toad Hall

A rich animal with a new enthusiasm every season; first appears Chapter 2

Boats, caravans and motorcars pass through him in turn, each one described as a conversion. He is the only character with money, a hall and a name in the newspapers, and the plot punishes him with prison and dispossession while the narration keeps enjoying him, a tension the ending resolves only by making him behave.

Badger

The authority of the Wild Wood; first appears Chapter 4

He hates society, keeps no fixed hours and is treated by every other animal as the last word. His house, built into the cellars of a city that has disappeared, gives him the long view that the rest of the book lacks, and it is why his rebukes to Toad carry weight that Rat’s do not.

Otter

A riverbank neighbour, and Portly’s father; first appears Chapter 1

He appears in the margins of the sociable chapters and then, in Chapter 7, supplies the emergency that takes Rat and Mole up the river at night. His grief is reported and never shown, which allows the vision on the island to happen to somebody else.

The Sea Rat

A wayfarer out of the south; first appears Chapter 9

He arrives for one chapter, eats a meal, talks about harbours, and leaves the Water Rat glassy-eyed and packing. He is the book’s argument against itself: everything the river stands for is put at risk by a stranger who is better at describing places than Rat is at defending his own.

The gaoler’s daughter

A girl in the castle where Toad is imprisoned; first appears Chapter 8

She is fond of animals, sees through Toad completely, and arranges his escape by lending him her aunt’s washerwoman’s dress for a consideration. She is one of the very few humans in the book with a will of her own, and the plot of the second half depends entirely on her.

The stoats, weasels and ferrets

Inhabitants of the Wild Wood, and squatters at Toad Hall; first appears Chapter 3

They frighten Mole in Chapter 3 and occupy Toad Hall in Chapter 11, and the book never grants them a named individual or a line of reported thought. They are the clearest sign of the novel’s politics: the riverbankers own houses, and the animals from the wood get them only by seizure.
