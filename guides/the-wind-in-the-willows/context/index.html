The Wind in the Willows · Kenneth Grahame

Context

Dates

1859
Kenneth Grahame born in Edinburgh He was brought up largely by relatives in Berkshire after his mother’s death, on the stretch of the Thames the book returns to.
1879
Grahame enters the Bank of England He worked there for nearly thirty years while publishing essays and stories in his own time.
1895
The Golden Age published Sketches of childhood seen from the child’s side; Dream Days followed in 1898.
1898
Grahame becomes Secretary of the Bank of England The most senior post he held, and the one he resigned a few months before the novel appeared.
1903
The Motor Car Act Registration, driving licences and a twenty-mile-an-hour limit: the legal background to Toad’s career on the road.
1907
Grahame’s story letters to his son Alastair Grahame sent his young son a series of animal-story letters; fifteen survive in the Bodleian with manuscript material for the novel.
1908
Published by Methuen Grahame resigned from the Bank in June; Methuen published The Wind in the Willows on 8 October.
1920
Alastair Grahame dies at Oxford The son the stories were first told to; he was nineteen.
1929
A. A. Milne’s Toad of Toad Hall staged The dramatisation keeps the Toad plot and drops the chapters that have no jokes in them, a division most adaptations have followed.
1931
E. H. Shepard illustrates the book His drawings fixed the appearance of the four animals for most readers; the 1908 first edition had a frontispiece and nothing else.
1932
Grahame dies at Pangbourne He is buried at Oxford, beside Alastair.

Places

Cookham Dean, Berkshire
Where Grahame spent part of his childhood beside the Thames, and the country the riverbank chapters draw on.
Quarry Wood, near Marlow
The wood on the hill above Cookham Dean, long identified locally with the Wild Wood.
Pangbourne
Grahame’s last home, on the same river, where he died in 1932.
Fowey, Cornwall
The harbour town Grahame knew well, and the kind of grey sea town the Sea Rat describes to a spellbound listener.
Bank of England, London
Grahame’s office for nearly thirty years, and the working life the riverbank is usually read against.
Bodleian Library, Oxford
Holds the surviving letters to Alastair and manuscript material for the novel.
