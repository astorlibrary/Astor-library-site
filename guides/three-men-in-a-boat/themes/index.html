Three Men in a Boat · Jerome K. Jerome

Themes

Work and idleness

The holiday begins with a diagnosis of overwork made by three men whose chief symptom is a disinclination to work of any kind. From there the book never stops counting labour: who packs, who tows, who sculls, who steers, who washes up. Each man is sure he is doing everything. The joke is old, but Jerome gives it to a new class of reader, the clerk with a fixed holiday, for whom rest is something earned and rowing is work pretending to be pleasure.

A disease called laziness in chapter I, Harris’s method of doing work in III, J. superintending the packing in IV, George introduced to the tow-line in IX, the confession of love for work in XV, and a boat abandoned in XIX.

1 ten-mile walk every morning. 1 bed at 11 sharp every night. And don’t stuff up your head with things you don’t understand.

J.’s doctor, Chapter I

From my earliest infancy I have been a martyr to it. As a boy, the disease hardly ever left me for a day. They did not know, then, that it was my liver. Medical science was in a far less advanced state than now, and they used to put it down to laziness.

Chapter I

The narrator’s vanity

J. is never caught in a lie. He reports what happened and what he thought of himself, and the two do not match. He packs better than any man living and forgets the boots; he hates steam launches until he is towed by one; he is careful of his work and never touches it. Harris and George get the same treatment through his eyes, which lets the reader enjoy the others’ vanity and miss the narrator’s until it is pointed out.

Every disease but one; the expert packer; Harris in the maze and at the piano; the posers at the lock; the trout that four men caught; and the pretence, in the rain, that Nature is beautiful even in her tears.

I plodded conscientiously through the twenty-six letters, and the only malady I could conclude I had not got was housemaid’s knee.

Objects

Objects in the book have wills, and they are hostile. Tow-lines knit themselves into antimacassars, kettles refuse to boil while watched, canvas hoops nip and attack, a tin of pineapple defeats three men and a mast, and a tooth-brush haunts its owner. The comedy lets the men blame the world for their own incompetence, and it describes something real about a holiday that needs more equipment than the life it was meant to escape.

The luggage heap and the packing in IV, the tow-line in IX, the hoops and kettle in X, the pineapple tin in XII, the potatoes in XIV and the plaster trout in XVII.

“There you are,” he would say, stepping heavily off the chair on to the charwoman’s corns, and surveying the mess he had made with evident pride. “Why, some people would have had a man in to do a little thing like that!”

Chapter III

Friendship

The three are old friends and speak to one another almost entirely in insults, complaints and accusations of shirking. Nobody in the book says he is fond of anyone. The friendship shows itself in other ways: a shared sense of grievance, jokes at each other’s expense, and the moment in the rain when each sees his own mean thoughts in the others’ faces and they run for the train together.

The motion carried three to one, the tent that each man thinks the other is spoiling, the wet shirt, the swans, and a toast drunk together after deserting the boat.

There’s nothing for me to do. Scenery is not in my line, and I don’t smoke. If I see a rat, you won’t stop; and if I go to sleep, you get fooling about with the boat, and slop me overboard. If you ask me, I call the whole thing bally foolishness.

Chapter II

Nature and sentiment

The river gets the book’s most ambitious prose: sunsets personified, the moon kissing the water, woods in their spring dress. Jerome writes these passages with conviction and then lets somebody ask about rain. The weather is the other half of the argument. Forecasts are always wrong, the barometer points to set fair in a flood, and in the last chapter the golden fairy stream turns into a spirit-haunted water through the land of vain regrets.

The purple passage and Harris’s question in II, the lumber sermon in III, the weather forecast in V, Cliveden woods in XII, and the rain on the way down in XIX.

Chapter II

History

Every town the boat passes has a past, and J. retells it: Saxon kings at Kingston, Queen Elizabeth in every public-house, King John at Runnymede, Henry VIII courting at Ankerwyke, Parliament fleeing plague to Reading. Some of it is pageant, written straight; much of it is deflated by a detail from the present. The book also knows that the past is partly a trick of distance, as in J.’s prediction that his landlady’s china dog will one day be a treasure.

Kyningestun and the china dog in VI, the pageant of 1215 in XI, the wet shirt that ends it in XII, Bisham and Medmenham in XIII, and Reading’s history in XVI.

In 2288 people will gush over it. The making of such dogs will have become a lost art. Our descendants will wonder how we did it, and say how clever we were. We shall be referred to lovingly as “those grand old artists that flourished in the nineteenth century, and produced those china dogs.”

Chapter VI

A bolder hand might have turned the game even at that point. Had it been a Richard there! the cup of liberty might have been dashed from England’s lips, and the taste of freedom held back for a hundred years.

Chapter XI

Class on the river

The Thames in 1889 was a pleasure-ground contested by different kinds of money. Riverside owners put up chains and notice-boards, steam launches whistle rowing boats out of the way, Maidenhead is full of swells and showy hotels, and the three men, who describe themselves as without position or influence, resent all of it. The book is written from the rowing boat and for its readers. Its narrator is also quite ready to look down on errand-boys, riverside roughs and anybody with less than he has.

Biggs’s boy and the Waterloo porters in V, the Kempton Park blackmailer and the notice-boards in VIII, Maidenhead in XII, the launches in XIII and XVI, and the woman on twelve shillings a week in XVI.

Anyhow, if he wasn’t the 11.5 for Kingston, he said he was pretty confident he was the 9.32 for Virginia Water, or the 10 a.m. express for the Isle of Wight, or somewhere in that direction, and we should all know when we got there. We slipped half-a-crown into his hand, and begged him to be the 11.5 for Kingston.

Chapter V

I do hate steam launches: I suppose every rowing man does. I never see a steam launch but I feel I should like to lure it to a lonely part of the river, and there, in the silence and the solitude, strangle it.
