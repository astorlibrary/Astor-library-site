Work and idleness

The holiday begins with a diagnosis of overwork made by three men whose chief symptom is a disinclination to work of any kind. From there the book never stops counting labour: who packs, who tows, who sculls, who steers, who washes up. Each man is sure he is doing everything. The joke is old, but Jerome gives it to a new class of reader, the clerk with a fixed holiday, for whom rest is something earned and rowing is work pretending to be pleasure.

Through the book A disease called laziness in chapter I, Harris’s method of doing work in III, J. superintending the packing in IV, George introduced to the tow-line in IX, the confession of love for work in XV, and a boat abandoned in XIX.

In the text

From my earliest infancy I have been a martyr to it. As a boy, the disease hardly ever left me for a day. They did not know, then, that it was my liver. Medical science was in a far less advanced state than now, and they used to put it down to laziness.

Chapter I

The deadpan keeps the vocabulary of medicine while the facts describe idleness. Martyr, disease, medical science are solemn words; the symptom is not wanting to work, and the old treatment was a clump on the head, which J. admits often worked. The reader supplies the diagnosis the narrator will not make. It is also the book’s first statement of its real subject: three young men who call their boredom overwork and prescribe themselves a holiday.

It is not that I object to the work, mind you; I like work: it fascinates me. I can sit and look at it for hours. I love to keep it by me: the idea of getting rid of it nearly breaks my heart.

Chapter XV

The paradox works because fascinates is the right word used wrongly. Work becomes first a spectacle, something to watch for hours, and then a possession, kept, dusted and never touched. Every clause sounds like an industrious man’s boast and describes an idle one. J. never concedes the point. He goes on to observe that every man in a boat believes he is doing everything, and does not apply the rule to himself, which is the book’s method with its narrator in one paragraph.

The narrator’s vanity

J. is never caught in a lie. He reports what happened and what he thought of himself, and the two do not match. He packs better than any man living and forgets the boots; he hates steam launches until he is towed by one; he is careful of his work and never touches it. Harris and George get the same treatment through his eyes, which lets the reader enjoy the others’ vanity and miss the narrator’s until it is pointed out.

Through the book Every disease but one; the expert packer; Harris in the maze and at the piano; the posers at the lock; the trout that four men caught; and the pretence, in the rain, that Nature is beautiful even in her tears.

In the text

I plodded conscientiously through the twenty-six letters, and the only malady I could conclude I had not got was housemaid’s knee.

Chapter I

The joke is built like a proof. J. goes through the alphabet as a conscientious student would, so the absurd conclusion arrives by method, and the one exception becomes the punchline. The next paragraph improves it: he feels slighted not to have housemaid’s knee, then grows less grasping and decides to do without. Hypochondria becomes acquisitiveness, a man collecting diseases. And the missing one is a servant’s complaint, the ailment of kneeling to scrub, which a man who never does that could hardly catch.

Harris drew out his map again, after a while, but the sight of it only infuriated the mob, and they told him to go and curl his hair with it. Harris said that he couldn’t help feeling that, to a certain extent, he had become unpopular.

Chapter VI

Understatement is the joke. The scene has built a crowd of twenty lost people, a woman with a baby who calls Harris an impostor, and return after return to the centre; to a certain extent, he had become unpopular is the mildest possible summary. The phrasing is Harris’s own, reported by J., so it is also a portrait: Harris survives humiliation by describing it in the language of a committee. The maze is a small model of the river trip, a simple route, a confident guide and a map that makes things worse.

Objects

Objects in the book have wills, and they are hostile. Tow-lines knit themselves into antimacassars, kettles refuse to boil while watched, canvas hoops nip and attack, a tin of pineapple defeats three men and a mast, and a tooth-brush haunts its owner. The comedy lets the men blame the world for their own incompetence, and it describes something real about a holiday that needs more equipment than the life it was meant to escape.

Through the book The luggage heap and the packing in IV, the tow-line in IX, the hoops and kettle in X, the pineapple tin in XII, the potatoes in XIV and the plaster trout in XVII.

In the text

My friend got rid of them, at last, by taking them down to a sea-side town, and burying them on the beach. It gained the place quite a reputation. Visitors said they had never noticed before how strong the air was, and weak-chested and consumptive people used to throng there for years afterwards.

Chapter IV

The cheese story is a digression inside a digression, and each stage is the same joke at a larger scale: the smell wakes a somnambulist cab-horse into a gallop of four miles an hour, empties a railway carriage, drives a wife and children to a hotel, sickens the bargemen and outrages a coroner. At the end the escalation becomes advertising. A resort’s bracing air is a buried cheese, and the invalids who flock there are the book’s hypochondriacs seen from outside. Deadpan keeps it all plausible: nobody in the story ever admits the absurdity.

Anyhow, if he wasn’t the 11.5 for Kingston, he said he was pretty confident he was the 9.32 for Virginia Water, or the 10 a.m. express for the Isle of Wight, or somewhere in that direction, and we should all know when we got there. We slipped half-a-crown into his hand, and begged him to be the 11.5 for Kingston.

Chapter V

Nobody at Waterloo knows where any train goes, and Jerome escalates the ignorance in lists: platform two, platform one, the local, the Southampton express, the Windsor loop, and now the driver’s own three candidates. The solution is a bribe, and ‘begged him to be the 11.5’ treats a train as something a man can choose to become. The joke is about the new machinery of Victorian leisure, timetables and officials, and how little it resembles the order it promises. The train turns out to be the Exeter mail.

Friendship

The three are old friends and speak to one another almost entirely in insults, complaints and accusations of shirking. Nobody in the book says he is fond of anyone. The friendship shows itself in other ways: a shared sense of grievance, jokes at each other’s expense, and the moment in the rain when each sees his own mean thoughts in the others’ faces and they run for the train together.

Through the book The motion carried three to one, the tent that each man thinks the other is spoiling, the wet shirt, the swans, and a toast drunk together after deserting the boat.

In the text

There’s nothing for me to do. Scenery is not in my line, and I don’t smoke. If I see a rat, you won’t stop; and if I go to sleep, you get fooling about with the boat, and slop me overboard. If you ask me, I call the whole thing bally foolishness.

Montmorency, Chapter I

Montmorency is the only member of the party allowed to speak without irony, and his objections are exact: a rowing holiday offers a dog no rats, no smoking and no scenery he cares for. The comedy is in the register. J. gives him the grumble of a man dragged on somebody else’s holiday, and through him the book says what none of the men will admit about their plan. The motion is then carried three to one, like a committee vote, and the minority report is the accurate one.

“Well,” said Harris, reaching his hand out for his glass, “we have had a pleasant trip, and my hearty thanks for it to old Father Thames—but I think we did well to chuck it when we did. Here’s to Three Men well out of a Boat!”

Harris, Chapter XIX

The book ends with the holiday abandoned and its own title turned round. Harris thanks Father Thames in a toastmaster’s phrase and then throws the whole project away in a slang verb, chuck. It is the last bathos and the most honest, since the trip has consisted largely of things going wrong and the best evening in it is spent in town. Montmorency, who voted against the river in chapter I, stands at the window and barks his agreement, so the minority report is carried at last.

Nature and sentiment

The river gets the book’s most ambitious prose: sunsets personified, the moon kissing the water, woods in their spring dress. Jerome writes these passages with conviction and then lets somebody ask about rain. The weather is the other half of the argument. Forecasts are always wrong, the barometer points to set fair in a flood, and in the last chapter the golden fairy stream turns into a spirit-haunted water through the land of vain regrets.

Through the book The purple passage and Harris’s question in II, the lumber sermon in III, the weather forecast in V, Cliveden woods in XII, and the rain on the way down in XIX.

In the text

we fall asleep beneath the great, still stars, and dream that the world is young again … Harris said: “How about when it rained?”

Chapter II

The sentence runs for most of a page, through the moon’s sister’s kiss and the wiles of painted civilisation, and Harris answers it in five words. The fall works only because J. writes the passage in earnest enough to carry the reader a little way. It also sets out the friendship: J. supplies the feeling and Harris the practical question, and the book gives Harris the better of it, since the next two pages describe exactly what happens to a tent in the rain.

We pretended, at first, that we enjoyed it. We said it was a change, and that we liked to see the river under all its different aspects. We said we could not expect to have it all sunshine, nor should we wish it. We told each other that Nature was beautiful, even in her tears.

Chapter XIX

In the rain the three try to keep up the tone of the book’s own lyrical passages and cannot. Each ‘we said’ introduces a line from the sentimental guide to nature (a change, not all sunshine, beautiful even in her tears), and the repetition sounds like people agreeing in order to keep their spirits up. The purple passages of the earlier chapters come back as dialogue, and they sound like things one says. The chapter has just offered its own fine writing about the river in rain, and this puts it in brackets.

History

Every town the boat passes has a past, and J. retells it: Saxon kings at Kingston, Queen Elizabeth in every public-house, King John at Runnymede, Henry VIII courting at Ankerwyke, Parliament fleeing plague to Reading. Some of it is pageant, written straight; much of it is deflated by a detail from the present. The book also knows that the past is partly a trick of distance, as in J.’s prediction that his landlady’s china dog will one day be a treasure.

Through the book Kyningestun and the china dog in VI, the pageant of 1215 in XI, the wet shirt that ends it in XII, Bisham and Medmenham in XIII, and Reading’s history in XVI.

In the text

In 2288 people will gush over it. The making of such dogs will have become a lost art. Our descendants will wonder how we did it, and say how clever we were. We shall be referred to lovingly as “those grand old artists that flourished in the nineteenth century, and produced those china dogs.”

Chapter VI

The musing ends in the future. J. has described his landlady’s china dog without mercy, its expression amiability carried to verge of imbecility, and now imagines it dug up legless and admired in a glass case. The comedy is a transfer of value by time: he mocks the collectors of his own day by imagining their descendants, and the prophecy has partly come true, since Victorian pottery dogs are collected now. History in the book is often like this, a matter of distance: what one age throws away, a later one puts in a glass case.

A bolder hand might have turned the game even at that point. Had it been a Richard there! the cup of liberty might have been dashed from England’s lips, and the taste of freedom held back for a hundred years.

Chapter XI

For two pages the comedy stops. J. fills Staines with soldiers and the river with barges, in a present-tense pageant of casques and banners, and writes it straight, excited exclamation and all. The history is the popular kind, a nation’s liberty turning on one king’s nerve. Its placing is the joke. The chapter closes on the cornerstone of England’s temple of liberty, and the next one opens with George asking whether J. would mind helping to wash up.

Class on the river

The Thames in 1889 was a pleasure-ground contested by different kinds of money. Riverside owners put up chains and notice-boards, steam launches whistle rowing boats out of the way, Maidenhead is full of swells and showy hotels, and the three men, who describe themselves as without position or influence, resent all of it. The book is written from the rowing boat and for its readers. Its narrator is also quite ready to look down on errand-boys, riverside roughs and anybody with less than he has.

Through the book Biggs’s boy and the Waterloo porters in V, the Kempton Park blackmailer and the notice-boards in VIII, Maidenhead in XII, the launches in XIII and XVI, and the woman on twelve shillings a week in XVI.

In the text

I do hate steam launches: I suppose every rowing man does. I never see a steam launch but I feel I should like to lure it to a lonely part of the river, and there, in the silence and the solitude, strangle it.

Chapter XIII

J. speaks for every rowing man and wishes on a machine the death of a villain in a melodrama, lured, isolated and strangled. The violence is a joke of proportion, like the tombstone he would make from a notice-board in chapter VIII, but the grievance behind it is real: launches belonged to people with money, and they whistled rowing boats out of the way. The chapter then describes the three drifting across the channel with their backs turned. Three chapters later J. is towed by a launch, and his opinions turn inside out.

It is very delightful being towed up by a launch. I prefer it myself to rowing. The run would have been more delightful still, if it had not been for a lot of wretched small boats that were continually getting in the way of our launch.

Chapter XVI

The chapter argument calls this the irritating behaviour of small boats, and the joke is that J. now speaks as he said launch-owners speak. In chapter XIII small boats were a noble resistance; now they are wretched and ought to be run down now and then to teach them a lesson. Jerome never points out the reversal. He leaves the reader to remember, which is how the book treats its narrator throughout: not exposing him, only placing his opinions where they can be compared.
