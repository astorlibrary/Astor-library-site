Three Men in a Boat · Jerome K. Jerome

Characters

J.

The narrator; a writer who believes he is industrious, practical and robust; first appears Chapter I

He is never named beyond the initial the others use. He finds he has every disease in the dictionary but one, packs better than any man living, loves work so much he cannot bear to part with it, and hates steam launches until one tows him. Nearly every joke in the book passes through his account of himself, and the account is always a little more flattering than the events it reports. He also writes the purple passages, and is the one who breaks them off.

George

A bank clerk, the sleepiest of the three and the one with the sensible ideas; first appears Chapter I

He goes to sleep at a bank from ten to four, as J. puts it, and has to be fetched from the City. He proposes the river, lays down the rule about things you cannot do without, reads out the weather forecast, learns the banjo, and is behind several of the disasters, from flannels washed in the Thames to the trout-case he pulls off the wall. Jerome drew him from his friend George Wingrave.

William Samuel Harris

The practical one, who always knows a place round the corner; first appears Chapter I

Harris is appetite and certainty. He is sure of the maze, sure he can sing a comic song, sure of his scrambled eggs, and sure that the answer to any mood is a drink. J. makes him the book’s philistine, with no poetry in him and no yearning for the unattainable, and then gives him the last word. Jerome drew him partly from Carl Hentschel, a teetotaller, so the drinking is a joke about a type more than a portrait.

Montmorency

A fox terrier, and the fourth member of the party; first appears Chapter I

He looks like an angel sent to earth in the shape of a small dog and behaves like a street fighter: a dozen chickens, a hundred and fourteen fights, rats killed against time. He is the only member of the party who states his view of the trip plainly, that there is nothing in it for him, and he is not far wrong. On the river he attacks a kettle, spares a cat, and brings a dead water-rat to the stew. His idea of heaven turns out to be Oxford, which is full of dogs.

Uncle Podger

J.’s uncle, the family man who puts up a picture; first appears Chapter III

He exists for one set piece in chapter III, told to show what Harris will become. Offered a picture to hang, he mobilises the whole house, sends out for nails, loses the coat he is sitting on, measures, loses the mark, falls on the piano, smashes his thumb, and at midnight admires a crooked picture on a ruined wall. Aunt Maria’s response is to ask for notice next time, so that she can spend the week with her mother.

J.’s doctor

His medical man and old chum; first appears Chapter I

He usually feels J.’s pulse and talks about the weather for nothing. Presented with a patient who claims every disease except housemaid’s knee, he thumps him on the chest, butts him with his head and writes a prescription no chemist can make up: beefsteak, beer, a ten-mile walk, bed at eleven, and no more reading. He is the book’s first straight man, and the cure he orders is more or less the holiday the three go on to take and fail at.

Biggs’s boy

The greengrocer’s errand-boy, who gathers the crowd on the doorstep; first appears Chapter V

Biggs the greengrocer is said to employ the most abandoned errand-boys civilisation has yet produced, and his latest leans on the railings, chews a straw and settles in to watch the luggage. Within minutes the grocer’s boy, the boot-shop boy and the pot-boy from the Blue Posts have joined him, and the street is debating whether it is a wedding or a funeral. He sees the party off with a carrot.

The engine-driver at Waterloo

Driver of an unidentified train on the high-level platform; first appears Chapter V

Nobody at Waterloo knows where the 11.5 for Kingston goes from, and this man cannot say for certain what train he is driving either. For half a crown he agrees to be the Kingston train, on the reasonable grounds that some train has to go there. The three men learn afterwards that they travelled on the Exeter mail. He is the book’s portrait of Victorian organisation, and he is entirely obliging.

The drowned woman

Unnamed; found in the river near Goring; first appears Chapter XVI

She is the one death in the book, and J. tells her story as a tract: a woman who had sinned, shut out by respectable relations, keeping herself and a child on twelve shillings a week, who kisses the child, buys it a penny box of chocolate and takes a ticket to Goring. The comedy stops for her, though not entirely, and the passage shows what the book leaves out the rest of the time: people who cannot afford a fortnight on the river.
