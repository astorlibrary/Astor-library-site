Three Men in a Boat · Jerome K. Jerome

What happens

Chapters I–IV Three invalids, a list and a cheese

J., George and Harris compare symptoms, decide that they are overworked, and settle on a fortnight up the Thames. The planning takes four chapters and holds most of the book’s best-known set pieces: the medical dictionary, the tent in the rain, Uncle Podger and his picture, the sermon on lumber, the Liverpool cheeses and a night of packing that ends with the butter in the teapot.

Chapter I
The liver-pill circular, a hundred and seven fatal maladies, the doctor’s prescription, the case against a sea trip, and Montmorency outvoted three to one.
Chapter II
Camping out on fine nights and on wet ones, a compromise between tent and inn, and a first account of Montmorency, who looks like an angel.
Chapter III
Harris’s method of doing work, Uncle Podger hangs a picture, George’s rule about the things we can’t do without, and the bathing nobody does.
Chapter IV
Paraffin oil that ruins the sunset, the cheeses from Liverpool, J.’s packing and the tooth-brush, then George and Harris pack the hampers.

Chapters V–VIII Waterloo, Kingston and the maze

They oversleep, gather a crowd of errand-boys round the luggage, bribe an engine-driver at Waterloo, and take the boat out from below Kingston bridge. Kingston sets J. musing on Saxon kings, carved oak and china dogs until he steers into the tow-path; Hampton Court reminds Harris of the maze. At Kempton Park they are moved on by a man who wants a shilling, and J. describes Harris singing a comic song.

Chapter V
The weather forecast, the luggage on the doorstep, Biggs’s boy, and a train that turns out to be the Exeter mail.
Chapter VI
Queen Elizabeth’s public-houses, carved oak papered over in blue, the china dog admired in 2288, a collision with the bank, and Harris in the maze.
Chapter VII
Boating costume, a picnic with two young women dressed for a photographer’s studio, and Harris’s wish to see Mrs Thomas’s tomb.
Chapter VIII
The man at Kempton Park, the notice-boards of riverside owners, Harris’s comic song, a German song that is not comic, and George buys a banjo.

Chapters IX–XII Tow-lines, a first night and Magna Charta

George is put to towing and the tow-line knots itself. After a long haul through Bell Weir lock they camp at Picnic Point, fight the canvas, trick the kettle and eat in silence. Next morning J. imagines King John arriving at the island in 1215, and is then asked to wash up. The pineapple tin that cannot be opened is thrown into the river before Maidenhead, and after they sail into three old anglers George takes the lines up to Marlow.

Chapter IX
The heathen instincts of tow-lines, two lovers who lose their boat and tow George’s instead, being towed by girls, and a lock that seems to have vanished.
Chapter X
The hoops and the canvas, the kettle that must not be watched, supper, the tyranny of the stomach, and a restless night under cover.
Chapter XI
A cold bathe, George’s shirt dropped in the river, Harris’s scrambled eggs, and the historical retrospect of June 1215.
Chapter XII
Lovers in a house, a night search for rooms at Datchet on an earlier trip, no mustard, the battle with the pineapple tin, Maidenhead, sailing, and three fishers cursing.

Chapters XIII–XVI Steam launches, a stew and the woman in the river

Marlow, Bisham and Medmenham supply history to retell; Montmorency squares up to a cat and thinks better of it. The three obstruct every steam launch they meet, make an Irish stew from everything left in the hampers, and remember past disasters with rafts, punts and sails. Above Reading a friend’s launch tows them, and J. finds launches delightful. Near Goring they find a drowned woman, and for a page the book stops joking.

Chapter XIII
Bisham Abbey, the Medmenham monks, Montmorency and the Tom cat, a procession out of Marlow, and useful methods for annoying steam launches.
Chapter XIV
Sonning, the Irish stew and Montmorency’s water-rat, the fight with the kettle, George’s banjo, and Harris and the swans.
Chapter XV
Love of work, the old river hand and what he says he has done, early boating memories, punting, and a first experience of sailing.
Chapter XVI
A tow from a steam launch, the irritating behaviour of small boats, the dead woman in the water, and Streatley and Goring.

Chapters XVII–XIX A plaster trout, Oxford and a toast in London

Two days at Streatley to get the washing done, and a digression on anglers that ends with the plaster trout. They pull past Wallingford, Dorchester and Abingdon to Oxford, where Montmorency finds dogs enough to fight. The rain begins on the way back down. At Pangbourne they leave the boat with the boatman on a pretext, take the train to Paddington, go to the Alhambra, and drink to being out of it.

Chapter XVII
Clothes ruined by George’s method, the art of angling, a conscientious fly-fisher, and the trout in the inn parlour.
Chapter XVIII
The photograph at Hampton Court lock, Wallingford, Dorchester and Abingdon, and the difficult water between Iffley and Oxford.
Chapter XIX
The hired up-river boat, the river in the rain, George’s banjo, the flight from Pangbourne and supper after the ballet.
