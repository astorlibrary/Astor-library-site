The Comedy of Errors · William Shakespeare

Themes

Identity

The play removes one proof at a time. A name fails, because two men answer to it. A face fails, because two men have it. Servants, wives, creditors and sworn witnesses all fail. Only the sight of both twins standing together settles anything, which makes the ending a visual event and not an argument.

Antipholus of Syracuse loses himself on purpose in 1.2; by 2.2 he wonders whether he is in earth, heaven or hell; in 5.1 the Duke has to ask outright which of two men is the natural one.

I to the world am like a drop of water That in the ocean seeks another drop, Who, failing there to find his fellow forth, Unseen, inquisitive, confounds himself.

Antipholus of Syracuse, 1.2.35

They say this town is full of cozenage, As nimble jugglers that deceive the eye, Dark-working sorcerers that change the mind, Soul-killing witches that deform the body

Antipholus of Syracuse, 1.2.97

Marriage, obedience and jealousy

Adriana and Luciana open the second act with a formal argument about whether a wife’s liberty should match her husband’s, and the play puts both positions to the proof. Adriana’s jealousy turns out to be justified, and she is still the one blamed for the household’s collapse by the woman who turns out to be her mother-in-law.

The couplet debate of 2.1 sets the terms; Adriana’s speech in 2.2 raises the stakes; Luciana advises hiding an affair in 3.2; and the Abbess delivers the verdict against Adriana in 5.1.

A man is master of his liberty; Time is their master, and when they see time, They'll go or come.

Luciana, 2.1.7

A wretched soul bruis'd with adversity, We bid be quiet when we hear it cry; But were we burd'ned with like weight of pain, As much, or more, we should ourselves complain

Adriana, 2.1.34

Service and the beaten servant

Both Dromios were bought as infants to attend the twins, and both spend the day being struck for errands they carried out correctly. The beatings are staged as farce and described by the victims as a working life, which is where the play’s comedy sits closest to something else.

The first blow falls in 1.2 and Dromio of Ephesus is threatened with another in 2.1; by 4.4 he can list what service has paid him, sorted by weather, from cold mornings to a lame beggar’s old age.

The clock hath strucken twelve upon the bell; My mistress made it one upon my cheek.

Dromio of Ephesus, 1.2.45

Am I so round with you, as you with me, That like a football you do spurn me thus? You spurn me hence, and he will spurn me hither.

Dromio of Ephesus, 2.1.80

Debt, bonds and the price of a life

Ephesus runs on credit. A man’s life costs a thousand marks, a chain costs two hundred ducats, and a goldsmith who cannot collect must have a customer arrested to keep his own name good. Every crisis in the middle acts is triggered by somebody needing to be paid on time.

The ransom is set in 1.1; the chain is ordered in 3.1 and misdelivered in 3.2; the arrest in 4.1 follows a creditor’s own deadline, and the ducats sent as bail go astray in 4.2.

Again, if any Syracusian born Come to the bay of Ephesus, he dies, His goods confiscate to the Duke's dispose, Unless a thousand marks be levied To quit the penalty and to ransom him.

Duke, 1.1.18

If the skin were parchment, and the blows you gave were ink, Your own handwriting would tell you what I think.

Dromio of Ephesus, 3.1.13

Time

Characters announce the hour constantly: two o’clock, five o’clock, dinner time, the dial pointing at five, sunset for the execution. The whole plot runs on people being late, and the servants build their best jokes out of Father Time’s bald head and time as a bankrupt who owes more than he is worth.

Dinner is already spoiling in 1.2, Adriana counts the hours in 2.1, Dromio runs a comic case on Time in 2.2 and 4.2, and the Merchant’s sighting of the dial at five in 5.1 brings the execution party on.

The clock hath strucken twelve upon the bell; My mistress made it one upon my cheek.

Dromio of Ephesus, 1.2.45

A man is master of his liberty; Time is their master, and when they see time, They'll go or come.

Luciana, 2.1.7

Being shut out

Dinner is the thing everybody is trying to get to and almost nobody eats. A house with a locked door and the wrong family inside it is the play’s central image, and it is staged so that the audience sees both sides of the same door.

The dinner is announced in 1.2, taken by the wrong guest in 2.2, denied to the right one in 3.1, and replaced at the end by an invitation into the abbey for a gossips’ feast.

A heavier task could not have been impos'd Than I to speak my griefs unspeakable

Egeon, 1.1.31

I to the world am like a drop of water That in the ocean seeks another drop, Who, failing there to find his fellow forth, Unseen, inquisitive, confounds himself.

Antipholus of Syracuse, 1.2.35

Witchcraft, possession and being called mad

Ephesus was known for sorcery long before this play, and the Syracusans reach for that explanation within an hour of landing. The Ephesians reach for the same one about them. Being declared mad has hard consequences here: a conjurer is hired, a man is tied up, and only sanctuary stops the treatment going further.

Antipholus of Syracuse names the town’s jugglers and witches in 1.2; by 4.3 he thinks the Courtesan is the devil; in 4.4 Pinch has the other twin bound, and the Abbess proposes syrups and prayers in 5.1.

They say this town is full of cozenage, As nimble jugglers that deceive the eye, Dark-working sorcerers that change the mind, Soul-killing witches that deform the body

Antipholus of Syracuse, 1.2.97

Am I in earth, in heaven, or in hell? Sleeping or waking, mad, or well-advis'd? Known unto these, and to myself disguis'd!

Antipholus of Syracuse, 2.2.202
