Identity

The play removes one proof at a time. A name fails, because two men answer to it. A face fails, because two men have it. Servants, wives, creditors and sworn witnesses all fail. Only the sight of both twins standing together settles anything, which makes the ending a visual event and not an argument.

Through the play Antipholus of Syracuse loses himself on purpose in 1.2; by 2.2 he wonders whether he is in earth, heaven or hell; in 5.1 the Duke has to ask outright which of two men is the natural one.

In the text

I to the world am like a drop of water That in the ocean seeks another drop, Who, failing there to find his fellow forth, Unseen, inquisitive, confounds himself.

Antipholus of Syracuse, 1.2.35

‘Confounds’ means defeats and also mingles, so the drop searching the sea is lost in the same motion that dissolves it. He states the play’s problem before any error has happened: a man looking for his double risks becoming indistinguishable from what he is looking for. Adriana will take the same image back off him in 2.2 and use it about marriage.

Am I in earth, in heaven, or in hell? Sleeping or waking, mad, or well-advis’d? Known unto these, and to myself disguis’d!

Antipholus of Syracuse, 2.2.202

‘Known unto these, and to myself disguis’d’ is the exact reverse of every other disguise plot on the Elizabethan stage. Nobody is hiding; their certainty hides him from himself. Three lines later he decides to go along with it, and that keeps the play running: the one man who could stop the errors chooses the dinner.

Marriage, obedience and jealousy

Adriana and Luciana open the second act with a formal argument about whether a wife’s liberty should match her husband’s, and the play puts both positions to the proof. Adriana’s jealousy turns out to be justified, and she is still the one blamed for the household’s collapse by the woman who turns out to be her mother-in-law.

Through the play The couplet debate of 2.1 sets the terms; Adriana’s speech in 2.2 raises the stakes; Luciana advises hiding an affair in 3.2; and the Abbess delivers the verdict against Adriana in 5.1.

In the text

A man is master of his liberty; Time is their master, and when they see time, They’ll go or come.

Luciana, 2.1.7

The advice is given by a woman with no husband, and it concedes the argument it means to close: if time is a man’s master, the wife waiting at home is mastered by him at one more remove. Adriana spots it immediately and asks the obvious question in the next line.

Teach sin the carriage of a holy saint, Be secret-false. What need she be acquainted? What simple thief brags of his own attaint?

Luciana, 3.2.14

The sister who spent the second act preaching obedience now coaches adultery, and her objection was never to the sin. ‘Attaint’ is a legal word for being convicted and corrupted, which makes her advice a plea to avoid the verdict and keep the offence.

Service and the beaten servant

Both Dromios were bought as infants to attend the twins, and both spend the day being struck for errands they carried out correctly. The beatings are staged as farce and described by the victims as a working life, which is where the play’s comedy sits closest to something else.

Through the play The first blow falls in 1.2 and Dromio of Ephesus is threatened with another in 2.1; by 4.4 he can list what service has paid him, sorted by weather, from cold mornings to a lame beggar’s old age.

In the text

Am I so round with you, as you with me, That like a football you do spurn me thus? You spurn me hence, and he will spurn me hither.

Dromio of Ephesus, 2.1.80

‘Round’ means plain-spoken and also spherical, and the pun holds the whole complaint: he is spoken to bluntly and kicked like a ball between two houses. In his next line he asks to be cased in leather, which is what a football was made of, so the joke ends with a servant requesting armour.

I am an ass indeed; you may prove it by my long ears. … When I am cold, he heats me with beating; when I am warm he cools me with beating.

Dromio of Ephesus, 4.4.25

The speech starts as a pun and turns into an account of a whole life, from the hour of his nativity to the day he expects to beg at doors with a limp his master gave him. Both Dromios call themselves asses repeatedly, and here the word starts to sound like a job description.

Debt, bonds and the price of a life

Ephesus runs on credit. A man’s life costs a thousand marks, a chain costs two hundred ducats, and a goldsmith who cannot collect must have a customer arrested to keep his own name good. Every crisis in the middle acts is triggered by somebody needing to be paid on time.

Through the play The ransom is set in 1.1; the chain is ordered in 3.1 and misdelivered in 3.2; the arrest in 4.1 follows a creditor’s own deadline, and the ducats sent as bail go astray in 4.2.

In the text

Again, if any Syracusian born Come to the bay of Ephesus, he dies, His goods confiscate to the Duke’s dispose, Unless a thousand marks be levied To quit the penalty and to ransom him.

Duke, 1.1.18

A comedy opens with a trade embargo read out in full. The law is reciprocal, since Syracuse does the same to Ephesians, so its cruelty is a matter of policy; what matters is the sum: a man’s life is quoted at a price, and the rest of the play moves smaller sums of money to the wrong people.

For slander lives upon succession, For ever hous’d where it gets possession.

Balthasar, 3.1.110

‘Hous’d’ and ‘possession’ are chosen with care by a man standing outside a house he cannot get into. Balthasar’s point is practical: force the door and the street will invent a reason. It is also the moment the play turns, because taking the advice sends Antipholus to a courtesan and puts the chain into the wrong hands.

Time

Characters announce the hour constantly: two o’clock, five o’clock, dinner time, the dial pointing at five, sunset for the execution. The whole plot runs on people being late, and the servants build their best jokes out of Father Time’s bald head and time as a bankrupt who owes more than he is worth.

Through the play Dinner is already spoiling in 1.2, Adriana counts the hours in 2.1, Dromio runs a comic case on Time in 2.2 and 4.2, and the Merchant’s sighting of the dial at five in 5.1 brings the execution party on.

In the text

The clock hath strucken twelve upon the bell; My mistress made it one upon my cheek.

Dromio of Ephesus, 1.2.45

Two strokes, two meanings, one word. The joke makes a household bell into a slap and gets a laugh, and it also tells the audience exactly what this servant’s day is like. Shakespeare keeps the pun running: the blows are marks, the marks are money, and the money is what everyone is shouting about.

Time is a very bankrupt, and owes more than he’s worth to season.

Dromio of Syracuse, 4.2.58

Everything in Ephesus can be put on account, including the hours. Dromio has just come from watching his master arrested for debt, and the joke is that the afternoon itself has run out of credit and is turning back to avoid the sergeant. It is nonsense, and it is also an exact description of a day in which nothing is paid for on time.

Being shut out

Dinner is the thing everybody is trying to get to and almost nobody eats. A house with a locked door and the wrong family inside it is the play’s central image, and it is staged so that the audience sees both sides of the same door.

Through the play The dinner is announced in 1.2, taken by the wrong guest in 2.2, denied to the right one in 3.1, and replaced at the end by an invitation into the abbey for a gossips’ feast.

In the text

A heavier task could not have been impos’d Than I to speak my griefs unspeakable

Egeon, 1.1.31

The line that launches a hundred lines of narrative says the story cannot be told. ‘Unspeakable’ is doing two jobs: too painful to say, and too large for speech. Nothing else in the play sounds like this, and the register only returns when Egeon does, in the last scene, to a son who does not know his voice.

Thirty-three years have I but gone in travail Of you, my sons, and till this present hour My heavy burden ne’er delivered.

Abbess, 5.1.399

‘Travail’ is both labour and hardship, and she takes the metaphor all the way: the day’s confusion has been a delivery, and it has taken thirty-three years. The number does not fit the rest of the play, which points to twenty-five or so, and editors have argued about it since the eighteenth century without agreeing on a fix.

Witchcraft, possession and being called mad

Ephesus was known for sorcery long before this play, and the Syracusans reach for that explanation within an hour of landing. The Ephesians reach for the same one about them. Being declared mad has hard consequences here: a conjurer is hired, a man is tied up, and only sanctuary stops the treatment going further.

Through the play Antipholus of Syracuse names the town’s jugglers and witches in 1.2; by 4.3 he thinks the Courtesan is the devil; in 4.4 Pinch has the other twin bound, and the Abbess proposes syrups and prayers in 5.1.

In the text

They say this town is full of cozenage, As nimble jugglers that deceive the eye, Dark-working sorcerers that change the mind, Soul-killing witches that deform the body

Antipholus of Syracuse, 1.2.97

Ephesus had this reputation in Acts 19, where converts burn their books of magic, and Shakespeare cashes it in early so that every later mistake has an explanation waiting. The three trades are graded by what they work on (eye, mind, body), and the play goes on to deceive the eye, change the mind and, in Pinch’s hands, get at the body.

The fellow is distract, and so am I, And here we wander in illusions. Some blessed power deliver us from hence!

Antipholus of Syracuse, 4.3.36

What unnerves him is kindness. He is not being robbed: he is greeted by name, shown silk cut to his measure and handed a purse of gold. A whole city has taken him for somebody, he cannot find out who, and the prayer is the point at which he stops trying to explain it.
