The Comedy of Errors · William Shakespeare

Characters

Antipholus of Syracuse

A traveller searching for his lost twin and mother; first appears 1.2

He left Syracuse at eighteen and has spent seven years looking for a brother he would not know by sight, and says so in the play’s most famous image: a drop of water gone into the sea to find another drop. Ephesus answers him with gold, dinner invitations and a wife, none of which he asked for, and he decides in 2.2 to take what is offered and see where it leads.

Antipholus of Ephesus

A merchant of Ephesus, married to Adriana; first appears 3.1

He is the twin with everything to lose: a standing in the city, a wife, a goldsmith who trusts him and a soldier’s claim on the Duke’s gratitude. In a single afternoon he is shut out of his house, refused his dinner, arrested for a chain he never touched, declared insane by a quack and tied up in his own cellar. He never once doubts who he is, which is why he is the angriest man on stage.

Dromio of Syracuse

Servant to Antipholus of Syracuse; first appears 1.2

He is the quicker of the two Dromios and does most of the joking, making a beating into an argument about Father Time’s bald head and a fat kitchen maid into a globe of the world. He decides early that Ephesus is fairy land, where the goblins will pinch them black and blue if they disobey.

Dromio of Ephesus

Servant to Antipholus of Ephesus; first appears 1.2

He is beaten by two masters and a mistress in a single day for carrying messages accurately. His complaints are the play’s running account of what service costs: sent between house and mart like a football, marked on the head instead of paid in marks, and beaten at last for buying the rope’s end his master told him to buy. He gets the last speech of the play, and uses it to take his brother’s hand.

Adriana

Wife to Antipholus of Ephesus; first appears 2.1

She is jealous, and she is right: her husband spends the afternoon at a courtesan’s table and promises his wife’s gold chain to the woman sitting across it. Shakespeare gives her the best speech in the play, an argument that a marriage cannot be divided any more than one drop of water can be taken back out of the sea, and then has her deliver it to a man she has never met.

Luciana

Adriana’s unmarried sister; first appears 2.1

She preaches wifely obedience with the confidence of someone who has never been married, telling her sister that beasts, fishes and fowls are all subject to their males. Courted by the wrong Antipholus, she gives him advice she cannot have meant to give: if you must be unfaithful, do it quietly and be kind at the table. Her own feelings are never stated: in the last scene the right Antipholus promises to make good what his brother said to her, and she says nothing at all.

Egeon

A merchant of Syracuse, condemned to die at sunset; first appears 1.1

He opens the play asking to be executed and spends a hundred lines telling the Duke why, in a speech of shipwreck and a mast cut in half that belongs to romance and not to farce. He then disappears for three acts. When he comes back, on his way to the block, his own son looks at him and says he has never seen him before. The Folio prints his name as Egeon; modern editions often set it as Aegeon.

Emilia

Abbess of the priory at Ephesus, and Egeon’s wife; first appears 5.1

She appears for the first time in the last scene, refuses to hand over the man who has taken sanctuary with her, and takes Adriana apart on the subject of nagging with a thoroughness nobody else in the play manages. Then she recognises her husband. In her closing speech the whole day becomes a birth she has been waiting thirty-three years to finish.

Solinus

Duke of Ephesus; first appears 1.1

He enforces a law he plainly dislikes, tells Egeon that his own crown and oath stop him from doing what he would prefer, and then gives him the day anyway. In the last scene he is handed two sworn accounts of the same afternoon that cannot both be true, and reaches for the only explanation available: they have all been drinking from Circe’s cup.

Angelo

A goldsmith of Ephesus; first appears 3.1

He makes the chain that ruins everybody. Owing money to a merchant who is about to sail, he needs payment for a necklace he handed to the wrong Antipholus in the street, and has the right one arrested when he denies taking it. He is neither a villain nor a fool: he is a tradesman with a debt due, and his credit is the only thing he has.
