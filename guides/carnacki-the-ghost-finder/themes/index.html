Method

Before anything supernatural is entertained, Carnacki eliminates people: tape across the doors, wax on the windows, photographs of every wall. The haunting only begins once fraud has been ruled out in front of the reader.

Through the book The method is set out in the first case, tested in the middle ones, and in the last it catches a hoax instead of a ghost, which proves the method was never rigged.

In the text

I got my tape measure then, and measured out a circle thirty-three feet

No. 2, The House Among the Laurels

Carnacki mentions a tape measure in a haunted house without any irony. The whole method is in this clause: the occult is being handled by somebody who would rather be precise than impressive.

the sound of a hoof in the dark billiard room we had just left

No. 4, The Horse of the Invisible

Hodgson’s timing is exact: the noise arrives in the sentence immediately after Carnacki has started listing commonplace possibilities. The detective story is allowed to nearly succeed before the ghost story takes it back.

Occult learning

The stories assume a profession: manuscripts to consult, monographs to read, rituals with numbered signs, and defences that can be built wrong. Carnacki’s authority comes from reading; he claims no gift.

Through the book Each case adds a scrap of the system (the Saaamaaa Ritual, the Unknown Last Line, the Incantation of Raaaee), and none of it is ever explained fully enough to be checked.

In the text

Theyre must noe lyght come from wythin the barryier.

No. 2, The House Among the Laurels

Hodgson invents a fourteenth-century sentence and uses it as a safety regulation. The spelling makes it old, the context makes it operational, and the pipes being confiscated makes it real.

there is some inscrutable Protective Force constantly intervening between the human soul

No. 4, The Horse of the Invisible

The nearest thing to consolation in Hodgson, and it comes as jargon at the end of a story. Inscrutable is the operative word: something is on our side and nobody knows what it is.

The Electric Pentacle

The Electric Pentacle is the book’s best idea: the mediaeval figure of protection, wired with vacuum tubes and run off accumulators. Edwardian electricity meets a fourteenth-century manuscript and neither is treated as absurd.

Through the book It is introduced as an improvement, relied on in every serious case, and its weakness (it can be broken by a hand reaching across it) makes the first story frightening.

In the text

the Electric Pentacle, which is a most marvelous ‘Defense’

No. 1, The Gateway of the Monster

Hodgson’s single best invention, and the reason these stories still feel unlike anything else: a mediaeval figure of protection rewired with vacuum tubes. The quotation marks around Defense are Carnacki’s, and they are the mark of a man using a technical term from an old book.

The storytelling evening

The card, the dinner, the refusal to be hurried, the story told without interruption, the dismissal. The frame is as fixed as the pentacle, and it does the same job: it holds a shape around something unruly.

Through the book Six stories, one unvarying evening, and the reader comes to want the closing words as much as the guests do.

In the text

In response to Carnacki’s usual card of invitation to have dinner and listen to a story, I arrived promptly at 427, Cheyne Walk

No. 1, The Gateway of the Monster

The important word is usual: before we have met anyone we know this has happened many times and will happen again. Hodgson establishes a routine so that the things inside it can be as irregular as he likes.

Out you go!

Every story

Three words, genially delivered, immediately after something that ought to keep four grown men talking all night. The formula is a door closing on the subject, and by the third story the reader is waiting for it.

A protective power

The one piece of comfort in Hodgson’s universe is stated in an aside: an inscrutable protective force intervenes between the human soul and what is outside. It is not called God and it does not always arrive early.

Through the book It saves Carnacki in the third case, is explained to Arkright in the same story, and is never claimed as certain.

In the text

that feeling of spiritual sickness, as if some delicate, beautiful, inward grace had suffered, which is felt only upon the too near approach of the ab-human

No. 1, The Gateway of the Monster

The damage is moral more than physical, and Carnacki says it is worse than pain. This sentence separates Hodgson’s monsters from the ghosts of the period: what they threaten is something the narrator can only call grace.

Admitting fear

Carnacki reports his own terror as part of the evidence: the sweat, the cowed feeling, the spiritual sickness that comes with the ab-human. In a genre of unflappable experts, that is the collection’s most modern quality.

Through the book He is frightened in the first story and still frightened in the sixth, and never pretends the experience gets easier.

In the text

The clungk clunck, clungk clunck, of the great hoof falls passed right between us

No. 4, The Horse of the Invisible

The noise is invented and repeated, and no creature is ever attached to it. The onomatopoeia is faintly ridiculous on the page and works completely in the ear, which is the risk Hodgson takes throughout and usually wins.

the Outer Monstrosities

No. 4, The Horse of the Invisible

Capitalised, plural, and never described. Hodgson’s habit of naming forces he will not show gave later writers a whole vocabulary, and the restraint is why the phrase still carries weight.
