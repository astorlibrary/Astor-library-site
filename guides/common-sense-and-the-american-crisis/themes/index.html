Independence

Common Sense makes independence a matter of plain fact: a continent should not be governed by an island three thousand miles away, a colony that has come of age cannot stay a ward, and every argument for reconciliation was answered by the shots of 19 April 1775. Paine insists that independence means only whether Americans shall make their own laws, and in the Crisis papers he defends the Declaration as the sense of the people given a legal form.

Through the book Now is the seed time of continental union; ’Tis time to part; a continent governed by an island; the independency is ours, not theirs; Our great title is AMERICANS.

In the text

The cause of America is in a great measure the cause of all mankind.

Common Sense, Introduction

A dispute over taxes and charters becomes a question for the whole world. The claim flatters American readers and lets anyone, from anywhere, claim a stake in the outcome. It also sets up the pamphlet’s later vision of America as an asylum for mankind.

Europe, and not England, is the parent country of America. This new world hath been the asylum for the persecuted lovers of civil and religious liberty from every part of Europe.

Common Sense, Thoughts on the Present State of American Affairs

By widening the family, Paine takes the emotional force out of the phrase mother country. Not one third of Pennsylvanians, he points out, are of English descent, and in his version the first settlers fled the cruelty of a monster.

Monarchy and hereditary succession

Monarchy is attacked from two directions, nature and scripture. Kings came into the world like some new species exalted above their equals; the Jews asked for one against God’s will; hereditary succession hands nations to fools and minors and has caused more civil wars than it has prevented. The English king has little to do but make war and give away places, and is paid eight hundred thousand pounds a year for it. In America, Paine says, the law is king.

Through the book Male and female are the distinctions of nature; an ass for a lion; a French bastard with an armed banditti; crowned ruffians; the Royal Brute of Britain; in America the law is king.

In the text

Male and female are the distinctions of nature, good and bad the distinctions of heaven; but how a race of men came into the world so exalted above the rest, and distinguished like some new species, is worth enquiring into,

Common Sense, Of Monarchy and Hereditary Succession

Two natural distinctions are granted so that a third can be questioned. Calling kings a new species makes monarchy look absurd, a biological oddity, and the mild phrase is worth enquiring into makes the attack sound like simple curiosity.

For as in absolute governments the King is law, so in free countries the law ought to be King; and there ought to be no other.

Common Sense, Thoughts on the Present State of American Affairs

Reversing two words, Paine reverses a whole order of government. He even allows a crown to be placed on the charter for the ceremony, then orders it demolished and scattered among the people whose right it is.

Society and government

The pamphlet opens with a distinction that became famous: society is produced by our wants and government by our wickedness, so that government at best is a necessary evil. Paine’s ideal is simple, representative and often elected, and he mocks the celebrated balance of the English constitution as a machine in which the crown, holding the key, always has its way. His plan for a continental charter, with a large and equal representation, is offered only as hints for something better.

Through the book Society is produced by our wants; the colony under a tree; the key given to the crown; a Continental Charter; virtue is not hereditary.

In the text

Society is produced by our wants, and government by our wickedness; the former promotes our happiness positively by uniting our affections, the latter negatively by restraining our vices.

Common Sense, Of the Origin and Design of Government

Everything in the sentence comes in pairs: society and government, wants and wickedness, positively and negatively. The balance makes a radical idea sound like common sense, since government is a remedy for our faults and nothing grander. A people can therefore change its government without losing its society.

the grave is nevertheless a perfect republic. Death is not the monarch of the dead, but of the dying.

Crisis V

In the middle of a satirical address to Howe, Paine finds a republican truth: in death all are equal. The second sentence adds a paradox, and then applies it to George III, who, like Death, loses a subject with every conquest and will war himself out of his dominions.

Nature and reason

Against custom and authority, Paine appeals to nature and common sense. Nature disapproves of hereditary right, has placed England and America in different systems, and has deserted the connection; the simple voice of nature and of reason will say what is right. The title itself claims that the argument needs no learning to follow.

Through the book Time makes more converts than reason; the simple voice of nature and of reason; nature disapproves it; Nature hath deserted the connexion.

In the text

a long habit of not thinking a thing wrong, gives it a superficial appearance of being right, and raises at first a formidable outcry in defense of custom. But the tumult soon subsides. Time makes more converts than reason.

Common Sense, Introduction

Before arguing anything, Paine explains why his readers may resist him: habit makes the familiar feel right. The paragraph gives the reader permission to change his mind, and its last sentence, a short epigram after a long one, predicts that time will do the rest.

In the following pages I offer nothing more than simple facts, plain arguments, and common sense;

Common Sense, Thoughts on the Present State of American Affairs

Method and title are named together in one modest phrase. The claim to plainness is itself a strategy: it sets the reader’s own judgement against the learned arguments of lawyers and ministers, and asks him to divest himself of prejudice before reading on.

Union

From the seed time of continental union in Common Sense to the last Supernumerary Crisis, Paine argues that American strength lies in unity. The thirteenth Crisis calls the union of the states the great hinge on which the whole machine turned and the most sacred thing in the constitution of America, and the final paper warns that the states, separated, are a medley of individual nothings.

Through the book It is not in numbers, but in unity, that our great strength lies; the union, that great palladium of our liberty and safety; Our great title is AMERICANS; a medley of individual nothings.

In the text

Now is the seed time of continental union, faith and honor. The least fracture now will be like a name engraved with the point of a pin on the tender rind of a young oak; the wound will enlarge with the tree, and posterity read it in full grown characters.

Common Sense, Thoughts on the Present State of American Affairs

Rural readers would know that a mark scratched on a sapling grows with it. The image makes a political choice a matter of timing: whatever is done now will be magnified for generations. It also makes posterity the judge of the present.

I consider the war of America against Britain as the country’s war, the public’s war, or the war of the people in their own behalf,

Crisis X

Paine repeats the word war to insist on whose it is. If the war belongs to the people, and not to Congress or the assemblies, then paying for it is every citizen’s obligation. It is a political argument for taxation made in the name of popular sovereignty.

Courage

The Crisis papers were written to keep an army and a public from giving up. Paine despises the summer soldier and the sunshine patriot, loves the man who can smile in trouble, and treats every setback, from the retreat across New Jersey to the loss of Charleston, as a test that will make the country firmer. What we obtain too cheap, he says, we esteem too lightly.

Through the book These are the times that try men’s souls; I love the man that can smile in trouble; Those who expect to reap the blessings of freedom; America ever is what she thinks herself to be.

In the text

These are the times that try men’s souls. The summer soldier and the sunshine patriot will, in this crisis, shrink from the service of their country; but he that stands it now, deserves the love and thanks of man and woman.

Crisis I

Alliteration does much of the work: summer soldier and sunshine patriot are volunteers who serve only in fair weather. The first sentence was short enough to become a watchword, and the next lines add that what we obtain too cheap, we esteem too lightly. According to tradition, Washington had the paper read to his troops before Trenton.

America ever is what she thinks herself to be.

Crisis IX

Morale, in this sentence, is destiny: governed by sentiment, America becomes, as she pleases, the victor or the victim. The idea runs through all the Crisis papers, which exist to change what the country thinks of itself.

Trade and peace

Paine’s America is a trading nation whose plan is commerce. Its corn will fetch its price in any market in Europe, the connection with Britain only drags it into European wars, and war can never be the interest of a trading nation. After the peace the same argument becomes a demand that the union be strong enough to protect American shipping.

Through the book Our plan is commerce; the make-weight in the scale of British politics; a bull-dog set upon a customer; Lord Sheffield and the carrying trade.

In the text

War never can be the interest of a trading nation, any more than quarrelling can be profitable to a man in business. But to make war with those who trade with us, is like setting a bull-dog upon a customer at the shop-door.

Crisis VII

The English are spoken to as shopkeepers, in the language of their own interest. The first sentence is a principle, the second a comic picture of it. America was Britain’s customer, and the war attacks the trade it was meant to secure.

even the light from heaven is only permitted to shine into their dwellings by paying eighteen pence sterling per window annually;

Crisis Extraordinary

After pages of figures, Paine gives the reader one image to remember: a tax on daylight. The window tax, like the duties on rum, coffee and small beer he also lists, makes the abstract sum of six millions a year into a daily burden in every house.

Scripture and conscience

Scripture supplies arguments against kings, from Gideon’s refusal of a crown to Samuel’s warning, and Paine calls monarchy the Popery of government. He asks government to protect all conscientious professors of religion and to do nothing more, likens the denominations to children of one family, and rebukes the Quaker leaders for mixing religion with politics.

Through the book Gideon and Samuel; monarchy is the Popery of government; children of the same family; Call not coldness of soul, religion.

In the text

I look on the various denominations among us, to be like children of the same family, differing only, in what is called, their Christian names.

Common Sense, Of the Present Ability of America

A pun carries the argument. Denominations differ as brothers’ Christian names differ, while they share the family name. Paine believes diversity of religious opinion is the will of the Almighty, a larger field for Christian kindness, and a continental government must protect it.

Call not coldness of soul, religion; nor put the Bigot in the place of the Christian.

Common Sense, To the Quakers

Paine, whose father was a Quaker, turns the Friends’ own principles against the Testimony’s authors. He accuses them of mistaking a lack of feeling for the sufferers in America for piety, and, a little later, of having mistaken party for conscience.

Posterity

Common Sense constantly measures the present against the future. The least fracture in the union will grow like a name cut into a young oak, a patched-up peace would leave the sword to our children, and a parent who wants peace in his own day wrongs his child. Paine asks his readers to take their children in their hand and look a few years further into life.

Through the book posterity read it in full grown characters; leaving the sword to our children; let it be in my day, that my child may have peace; an inheritance for posterity.

In the text

We have it in our power to begin the world over again. A situation, similar to the present, hath not happened since the days of Noah until now. The birthday of a new world is at hand,

Common Sense, Appendix

This is the pamphlet at its most visionary. The comparison with Noah makes the present moment a second beginning after a flood, and the birthday of a new world gives it the hope of a birth. In the next sentences Paine sets ‘a race of men, perhaps as numerous as all Europe contains’ against the ‘little, paltry cavellings’ of ‘a few weak or interested men’.

a generous parent should have said, “If there must be trouble, let it be in my day, that my child may have peace;”

Crisis I

Through a remembered scene, Paine answers a whole political attitude. The tavern-keeper wants the trouble postponed; Paine rewrites his words so that a parent takes the danger on himself to spare his child. This single reflection, he says, is enough to awaken every man to duty.

Whig and Tory

Paine writes as a partisan of the Whigs, who supported independence, against the Tories who stayed loyal to the Crown. Common Sense hopes that the two names will be extinct. The Crisis papers, written in a war in which loyalists fought beside the British, grow harsher, calling every Tory a coward and proposing tests of allegiance, taxes and exile for the disaffected.

Through the book Let the names of Whig and Tory be extinct; Every Tory is a coward; Here is the touchstone to try men by; the warm passions of a Whig and the cold malice of a Tory.

In the text

There is a dignity in the warm passions of a Whig, which is never to be found in the cold malice of a Tory.

Crisis VI

Warm and cold divide the two parties as clearly as virtue and vice. In the next sentences nature is only heated in the Whig and poisoned in the Tory, and the Whig forgives while the Tory can only revenge. Paine applies the distinction to England as well as America.

give up the murderer, and save your officer, as the first outset of a necessary reformation.

Supernumerary Crisis (To Sir Guy Carleton)

Paine reduces a painful case to a plain choice for Carleton: surrender the loyalist Lippincott, or be responsible for the death of an innocent British captain. The letter separates the villain from the victim, and puts the decision squarely on the British commander.
