Common Sense and The American Crisis · Thomas Paine

Themes

Independence

Common Sense makes independence a matter of plain fact: a continent should not be governed by an island three thousand miles away, a colony that has come of age cannot stay a ward, and every argument for reconciliation was answered by the shots of 19 April 1775. Paine insists that independence means only whether Americans shall make their own laws, and in the Crisis papers he defends the Declaration as the sense of the people given a legal form.

Now is the seed time of continental union; ’Tis time to part; a continent governed by an island; the independency is ours, not theirs; Our great title is AMERICANS.

The cause of America is in a great measure the cause of all mankind.

Common Sense, Introduction

Europe, and not England, is the parent country of America. This new world hath been the asylum for the persecuted lovers of civil and religious liberty from every part of Europe.

Common Sense, Thoughts on the Present State of American Affairs

Monarchy and hereditary succession

Monarchy is attacked from two directions, nature and scripture. Kings came into the world like some new species exalted above their equals; the Jews asked for one against God’s will; hereditary succession hands nations to fools and minors and has caused more civil wars than it has prevented. The English king has little to do but make war and give away places, and is paid eight hundred thousand pounds a year for it. In America, Paine says, the law is king.

Male and female are the distinctions of nature; an ass for a lion; a French bastard with an armed banditti; crowned ruffians; the Royal Brute of Britain; in America the law is king.

Government, like dress, is the badge of lost innocence; the palaces of kings are built on the ruins of the bowers of paradise.

Common Sense, Of the Origin and Design of Government

though we have been wise enough to shut and lock a door against absolute monarchy, we at the same time have been foolish enough to put the crown in possession of the key.

Common Sense, Of the Origin and Design of Government

Society and government

The pamphlet opens with a distinction that became famous: society is produced by our wants and government by our wickedness, so that government at best is a necessary evil. Paine’s ideal is simple, representative and often elected, and he mocks the celebrated balance of the English constitution as a machine in which the crown, holding the key, always has its way. His plan for a continental charter, with a large and equal representation, is offered only as hints for something better.

Society is produced by our wants; the colony under a tree; the key given to the crown; a Continental Charter; virtue is not hereditary.

Society is produced by our wants, and government by our wickedness; the former promotes our happiness positively by uniting our affections, the latter negatively by restraining our vices.

Common Sense, Of the Origin and Design of Government

Government, like dress, is the badge of lost innocence; the palaces of kings are built on the ruins of the bowers of paradise.

Common Sense, Of the Origin and Design of Government

Nature and reason

Against custom and authority, Paine appeals to nature and common sense. Nature disapproves of hereditary right, has placed England and America in different systems, and has deserted the connection; the simple voice of nature and of reason will say what is right. The title itself claims that the argument needs no learning to follow.

Time makes more converts than reason; the simple voice of nature and of reason; nature disapproves it; Nature hath deserted the connexion.

a long habit of not thinking a thing wrong, gives it a superficial appearance of being right, and raises at first a formidable outcry in defense of custom. But the tumult soon subsides. Time makes more converts than reason.

Common Sense, Introduction

The cause of America is in a great measure the cause of all mankind.

Common Sense, Introduction

Union

From the seed time of continental union in Common Sense to the last Supernumerary Crisis, Paine argues that American strength lies in unity. The thirteenth Crisis calls the union of the states the great hinge on which the whole machine turned and the most sacred thing in the constitution of America, and the final paper warns that the states, separated, are a medley of individual nothings.

It is not in numbers, but in unity, that our great strength lies; the union, that great palladium of our liberty and safety; Our great title is AMERICANS; a medley of individual nothings.

Now is the seed time of continental union, faith and honor. The least fracture now will be like a name engraved with the point of a pin on the tender rind of a young oak; the wound will enlarge with the tree, and posterity read it in full grown characters.

Common Sense, Thoughts on the Present State of American Affairs

I look on the various denominations among us, to be like children of the same family, differing only, in what is called, their Christian names.

Common Sense, Of the Present Ability of America

Courage

The Crisis papers were written to keep an army and a public from giving up. Paine despises the summer soldier and the sunshine patriot, loves the man who can smile in trouble, and treats every setback, from the retreat across New Jersey to the loss of Charleston, as a test that will make the country firmer. What we obtain too cheap, he says, we esteem too lightly.

These are the times that try men’s souls; I love the man that can smile in trouble; Those who expect to reap the blessings of freedom; America ever is what she thinks herself to be.

These are the times that try men's souls. The summer soldier and the sunshine patriot will, in this crisis, shrink from the service of their country; but he that stands it now, deserves the love and thanks of man and woman.

Crisis I

Your lordship, I find, has now commenced author, and published a proclamation; I have published a Crisis.

Crisis II

Trade and peace

Paine’s America is a trading nation whose plan is commerce. Its corn will fetch its price in any market in Europe, the connection with Britain only drags it into European wars, and war can never be the interest of a trading nation. After the peace the same argument becomes a demand that the union be strong enough to protect American shipping.

Our plan is commerce; the make-weight in the scale of British politics; a bull-dog set upon a customer; Lord Sheffield and the carrying trade.

War never can be the interest of a trading nation, any more than quarrelling can be profitable to a man in business. But to make war with those who trade with us, is like setting a bull-dog upon a customer at the shop-door.

Crisis VII

Like fire at a distance you heard not even the cry; you felt not the danger, you saw not the confusion. To you every thing has been foreign but the taxes to support it.

Crisis VIII

Scripture and conscience

Scripture supplies arguments against kings, from Gideon’s refusal of a crown to Samuel’s warning, and Paine calls monarchy the Popery of government. He asks government to protect all conscientious professors of religion and to do nothing more, likens the denominations to children of one family, and rebukes the Quaker leaders for mixing religion with politics.

Gideon and Samuel; monarchy is the Popery of government; children of the same family; Call not coldness of soul, religion.

Europe, and not England, is the parent country of America. This new world hath been the asylum for the persecuted lovers of civil and religious liberty from every part of Europe.

Common Sense, Thoughts on the Present State of American Affairs

I look on the various denominations among us, to be like children of the same family, differing only, in what is called, their Christian names.

Common Sense, Of the Present Ability of America

Posterity

Common Sense constantly measures the present against the future. The least fracture in the union will grow like a name cut into a young oak, a patched-up peace would leave the sword to our children, and a parent who wants peace in his own day wrongs his child. Paine asks his readers to take their children in their hand and look a few years further into life.

posterity read it in full grown characters; leaving the sword to our children; let it be in my day, that my child may have peace; an inheritance for posterity.

Now is the seed time of continental union, faith and honor. The least fracture now will be like a name engraved with the point of a pin on the tender rind of a young oak; the wound will enlarge with the tree, and posterity read it in full grown characters.

Common Sense, Thoughts on the Present State of American Affairs

We have it in our power to begin the world over again. A situation, similar to the present, hath not happened since the days of Noah until now. The birthday of a new world is at hand,

Common Sense, Appendix

Whig and Tory

Paine writes as a partisan of the Whigs, who supported independence, against the Tories who stayed loyal to the Crown. Common Sense hopes that the two names will be extinct. The Crisis papers, written in a war in which loyalists fought beside the British, grow harsher, calling every Tory a coward and proposing tests of allegiance, taxes and exile for the disaffected.

Let the names of Whig and Tory be extinct; Every Tory is a coward; Here is the touchstone to try men by; the warm passions of a Whig and the cold malice of a Tory.

Call not coldness of soul, religion; nor put the Bigot in the place of the Christian.

Common Sense, To the Quakers

a generous parent should have said, "If there must be trouble, let it be in my day, that my child may have peace;"
