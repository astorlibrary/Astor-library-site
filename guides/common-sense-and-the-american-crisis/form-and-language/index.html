Common Sense and The American Crisis · Thomas Paine

Form and language

Plain style

Short, direct sentences in everyday words, written for readers without learning.

Common Sense promises nothing more than simple facts, plain arguments and common sense, and Paine writes for tradesmen and farmers as much as for lawyers. His images come from ordinary life, last year’s almanac, an apprentice nearly out of his time, a man putting off unpleasant business, and his paragraphs often end on a blunt verdict: A mere absurdity! Reconciliation and ruin are nearly related.

a long habit of not thinking a thing wrong, gives it a superficial appearance of being right, and raises at first a formidable outcry in defense of custom. But the tumult soon subsides. Time makes more converts than reason.

Common Sense, Introduction

Direct address

A narrator or speaker turning outward to address the reader or the audience by name.

Readers and enemies alike are spoken to directly: ye that love mankind, ye that tell us of harmony and reconciliation, your lordship, sir. Most of the Crisis papers are open letters, to Lord Howe, Sir William Howe, the British commissioners, the people of England and Lord Shelburne, printed in American newspapers and sent, as Paine says, to England too.

In the following pages I offer nothing more than simple facts, plain arguments, and common sense;

Common Sense, Thoughts on the Present State of American Affairs

Antithesis

Opposed ideas set against each other in balanced phrases.

Pairs organise Paine’s thinking: society and government, wants and wickedness, a blessing and a necessary evil, the king as law and the law as king. The Crisis opens with the summer soldier and the sunshine patriot, and closes the war by turning the times that try men’s souls into the times that tried them.

Society is produced by our wants, and government by our wickedness; the former promotes our happiness positively by uniting our affections, the latter negatively by restraining our vices.

Common Sense, Of the Origin and Design of Government

Epigram

A short balanced sentence that sounds like settled wisdom and usually turns a received idea inside out.

Many of Paine’s sentences were built to be remembered and repeated: Time makes more converts than reason; the cause of America is in a great measure the cause of all mankind; These are the times that try men’s souls; America ever is what she thinks herself to be.

a long habit of not thinking a thing wrong, gives it a superficial appearance of being right, and raises at first a formidable outcry in defense of custom. But the tumult soon subsides. Time makes more converts than reason.

Common Sense, Introduction

Analogy

Reasoning from a familiar case to an unfamiliar one.

Homely comparisons carry many of the arguments: a child that has thrived on milk will still need meat, no satellite is larger than its planet, a guardian who has grown rich on his ward resents his coming of age, and making war on your customers is like setting a bull-dog upon one at the shop door.

Government, like dress, is the badge of lost innocence; the palaces of kings are built on the ruins of the bowers of paradise.

Common Sense, Of the Origin and Design of Government

Rhetorical questions

Questions asked for effect, where the answer is assumed.

Common Sense asks the reconciler: Hath your house been burnt? Hath your property been destroyed before your face? Anyone who can still shake hands with the murderers has the heart of a coward. The Crisis papers use questions to corner their addressees, asking Lord Howe what powers he means, when as a commissioner he has none.

Was America, then, the giant of the empire, and England only her dwarf in waiting!

Crisis XII

Objection and reply

Stating an opponent’s argument in order to answer it.

Much of Common Sense is a series of objections answered: But she has protected us, say some; But Britain is the parent country, say some; But where says some is the King of America? Paine gives the opposing case in its plainest form and disposes of it in the next sentence.

Europe, and not England, is the parent country of America. This new world hath been the asylum for the persecuted lovers of civil and religious liberty from every part of Europe.

Common Sense, Thoughts on the Present State of American Affairs

Argument from scripture

Appealing to the Bible as an authority in a political argument.

Writing for Bible readers, Paine turns scripture against kings. He retells Gideon’s refusal of a crown and Samuel’s warning of what a king would take, quoting the prophet at length, and calls on Pharaoh, Mammon and the Popery of government. The address to the Quakers meets their Testimony on its own religious ground.

Government, like dress, is the badge of lost innocence; the palaces of kings are built on the ruins of the bowers of paradise.

Common Sense, Of the Origin and Design of Government

Invective

Sustained personal abuse used as argument.

Paine can be ferocious: the Royal Brute of Britain, the sullen-tempered Pharaoh of England, a sottish, stupid, stubborn, worthless, brutish man. The fifth Crisis is an extended mock design for Sir William Howe’s monument. Paine’s defence, in the second Crisis, is that words were made for use, and the fault lies in deserving them.

One of the strongest natural proofs of the folly of hereditary right in kings, is, that nature disapproves it, otherwise she would not so frequently turn it into ridicule by giving mankind an ass for a lion.

Common Sense, Of Monarchy and Hereditary Succession

Figures and accounts

Arguing with numbers, tables and costs.

Arithmetic backs the rhetoric. The enlarged Common Sense adds a table of the cost of building warships of every rate to show that America could afford a navy, and the Crisis Extraordinary sets British taxes of about forty shillings a head against the thirteen shillings and fourpence a head that would defend and govern America.

even the light from heaven is only permitted to shine into their dwellings by paying eighteen pence sterling per window annually;

Crisis Extraordinary
