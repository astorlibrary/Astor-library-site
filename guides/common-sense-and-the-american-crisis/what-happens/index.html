Common Sense and The American Crisis · Thomas Paine

What happens

Common Sense: Introduction; Of the Origin and Design of Government Society, government and the English constitution

Paine opens by warning that a long habit of not thinking a thing wrong gives it a superficial appearance of being right, and declares that the cause of America is in a great measure the cause of all mankind. The first section separates society, produced by our wants, from government, produced by our wickedness, and imagines a few settlers meeting under a tree to make their first laws, then choosing representatives as their numbers grow. Against this simple model he sets the celebrated English constitution of king, lords and commons, which he calls the base remains of two ancient tyrannies mixed with some new republican materials, and in which the crown, as the giver of places and pensions, always has its way.

Common Sense: Of Monarchy and Hereditary Succession Kings and their heirs

Mankind were originally equals, Paine argues, and while differences of wealth can be explained, the distinction of kings and subjects has no natural or religious reason. He goes to scripture: for nearly three thousand years from the creation the Jews had no king, Gideon refused a crown, and Samuel warned the people what a king would take from them. Hereditary succession is worse still, an insult to posterity that nature mocks by giving mankind an ass for a lion. The first king of the present English line was a French bastard landing with an armed banditti, and far from preventing civil wars, succession has brought England eight civil wars and nineteen rebellions since the Conquest.

Common Sense: Thoughts on the Present State of American Affairs The case for separation

Offering nothing more than simple facts, plain arguments and common sense, Paine declares that the period of debate is closed: since the fighting of 19 April 1775, all the old plans for reconciliation are like the almanacs of last year. He answers the case for staying with Britain point by point. America would have flourished without her; Britain protected the colonies for the sake of her own trade; Europe, not England, is the parent country; the connection drags America into European wars; and it is absurd for a continent to be governed by an island. Reconciliation would leave the king a veto over every American law. Paine sketches a continental government, with a large Congress and a Continental Charter, and answers the question where the King of America is: in America the law is king.

Common Sense: Of the Present Ability of America; Appendix; To the Quakers Strength, the Appendix and the Quakers

The last section argues that America is strong enough now: its numbers suit its needs, it has no debt, and it has the timber, tar, iron and hemp to build a navy, whose cost the enlarged edition calculates ship by ship from British figures. Youth is the seed time of good habits, and the time to form a government is before interests divide. The Appendix answers the king’s speech, which reached Philadelphia on the day the pamphlet first appeared, and ends with the birthday of a new world. The address to the Quakers tells the authors of their Ancient Testimony that bearing arms in defence is less sinful than making war, and that in declaring for the king they have mixed religion with politics.

The American Crisis I–IV (December 1776 to September 1777) The retreat, Trenton and the Brandywine

Paine began the first Crisis at Newark during the retreat across New Jersey, and it appeared in the Pennsylvania Journal on 19 December 1776, a week before Washington crossed the Delaware to attack Trenton. It opens with “These are the times that try men’s souls”, describes the retreat from Fort Lee, praises Washington’s fortitude, and tells of a Tory tavern-keeper who wanted peace in his day. The second paper, addressed to Lord Howe in January 1777, mocks his proclamation offering pardons; the third, dated on the second anniversary of Lexington, reviews the whole quarrel and sets out the case for independence under four heads; the fourth was written the day after the defeat at the Brandywine.

The American Crisis V–VIII (March 1778 to March 1780) Howe, the commissioners and the people of England

Written at Lancaster in March 1778, with the British in Philadelphia, the fifth paper mocks Sir William Howe with a design for his monument, then turns to the inhabitants of America with a plan for filling the army. The sixth answers the peace commissioners of 1778, the Earl of Carlisle, General Clinton and William Eden, whose cargo of pardons will have no market and whose manifesto threatens that the whole contest is changed. The seventh and eighth are addressed to the people of England: war can never be the interest of a trading nation, and the English, safe on their island, have known nothing of the war but its taxes.

The American Crisis IX–XI, the Crisis Extraordinary and the first Supernumerary Crisis (June 1780 to May 1782) Charleston, taxes and Yorktown

The ninth paper, of June 1780, treats the loss of Charleston as a call to wake up: America ever is what she thinks herself to be. The Crisis Extraordinary, in October, sets the taxes of England against the sum needed to defend America and argues that conquest would multiply the burden. After the surrender of Cornwallis at Yorktown, the tenth paper answers the king’s speech and asks the states to pay the quotas Congress has set; the eleventh warns that Britain is trying to separate America from France; and a Supernumerary Crisis of May 1782 asks Sir Guy Carleton to give up the loyalist who hanged Captain Huddy of the New Jersey militia, so that Captain Asgill, the British officer chosen by lot for retaliation, need not die.

The American Crisis XII, XIII and the last Supernumerary Crisis (October 1782 to December 1783) Peace and the union

The twelfth paper answers the Earl of Shelburne, Britain’s first minister, who had said that the sun of England’s glory would set when Parliament acknowledged American independence. The thirteenth, published on 19 April 1783, the eighth anniversary of Lexington, opens with “The times that tried men’s souls are over” and turns to what peace demands: a good national character, which Paine rates as highly as independence, and above all the union of the states, since the world knows Americans by no other title. The last paper, sent from New York in December 1783, attacks Lord Sheffield’s plan to keep the West India carrying trade in British ships and argues that only a union able to act on commerce can answer it.
