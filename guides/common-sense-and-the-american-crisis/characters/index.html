Common Sense and The American Crisis · Thomas Paine

Characters

Thomas Paine

The author, who signed his papers Common Sense; first appears Common Sense, Introduction

An English staymaker and dismissed excise officer from Thetford, Paine arrived in Philadelphia at the end of 1774 with a letter from Benjamin Franklin, edited a magazine, and published Common Sense anonymously in January 1776. He served as a volunteer aide to General Nathanael Greene during the retreat of 1776, became secretary to the Congress’s Committee for Foreign Affairs, lost the post in the quarrel over Silas Deane, and went to France in 1781 to help raise money. He took no profit from Common Sense, and signed every Crisis paper with its title.

George III

King of Great Britain; first appears Common Sense, Introduction

Never named in Common Sense, the king is its real target: the hardened, sullen-tempered Pharaoh of England who can hear of his subjects’ slaughter and sleep, and the Royal Brute of Britain, whose speech to Parliament reached Philadelphia on the day the pamphlet first appeared. In the first Crisis he is the sottish, stupid, stubborn, worthless, brutish man to whom Paine will never swear allegiance, and his speech after Yorktown is answered in the tenth.

George Washington

Commander of the Continental Army; first appears Crisis I

In the first Crisis Paine, who marched with the troops from Fort Lee to the Delaware, compares him to King William, who never appeared to full advantage but in difficulties and in action, and credits him with a mind that can even flourish upon care. According to a tradition repeated by Conway, Washington had the paper read to his soldiers before Trenton. In January 1776 he had written of the sound doctrine and unanswerable reasoning of Common Sense.

Benjamin Franklin

Paine’s patron; first appears Crisis III

Paine knew Franklin in London and came to America with his letter of introduction. In a note to the third Crisis he recalls that in October 1775 Franklin offered him materials for a history of the troubles, and that he had already begun Common Sense and sent Franklin the first copy printed. The pamphlet was at first attributed to Franklin, who wrote in February 1776 that Paine was the reputed and, he thought, the real author.

Lord Howe

Peace commissioner, addressed in the second Crisis; first appears Crisis II

With his brother, General Howe, Lord Howe came to America empowered to offer pardons, and their joint proclamation of 30 November 1776 ordered all congresses and committees to disband. Paine addresses the second Crisis to him, sets his own paper against the proclamation, recalls that America had raised a monument to Howe’s dead brother, and tells him that no messenger was ever sent on a more foolish errand.

Sir William Howe

British commander-in-chief; first appears Crisis I

The general who drives Washington’s army across New Jersey in 1776 and takes Philadelphia in 1777 is Paine’s chief military adversary. Paine says in the first Crisis that Howe’s campaign is rather a ravage than a conquest. In the fifth, written at Lancaster in March 1778, he addresses Howe with extended mockery, looking for a suitable monument for the new knight of the windmill and post.

Nathanael Greene

General commanding at Fort Lee; first appears Crisis I

The Continental general who commanded the garrison at Fort Lee on 20 November 1776, when the British landed above it and the retreat began. Paine, his volunteer aide-de-camp, describes him (spelled Green in the text) ordering the men under arms and sending an express to Washington at Hackensack.

The Tories

Americans loyal to the Crown; first appears Common Sense, Thoughts on the Present State of American Affairs

For Paine the Tories are Americans who opposed independence and sided with Britain. Common Sense sorts the reconcilers into interested men, weak men, prejudiced men and moderate men, and ends by hoping that the names of Whig and Tory will be extinct. In the Crisis papers the tone hardens: every Tory is a coward, the tavern-keeper at Amboy wants peace in his own day, and Paine proposes tests, taxes and exile for the disaffected.

The Quaker leaders

Authors of the Ancient Testimony; first appears Common Sense, To the Quakers

Paine, whose father was a Quaker, addresses the representatives of the Society of Friends who published The Ancient Testimony early in 1776, which declared that the setting up and putting down of kings is God’s peculiar prerogative and then urged subordination to the king. He tells them they have mistaken party for conscience, and in the second Crisis attacks a further Quaker testimony, signed by John Pemberton in December 1776.

The Carlisle commissioners

British peace commissioners of 1778; first appears Crisis VI

The Earl of Carlisle, General Clinton and William Eden were sent in 1778, after the French alliance, with offers of pardon and the repeal of the offending acts. Paine’s sixth Crisis answers their last proclamation, which warned that if America joined France the whole contest was changed, and asks whether they consider themselves men or devils.

Silas Deane

American agent in France, attacked by Paine; first appears Crisis X

A former member of Congress sent to France to obtain supplies. Paine accused him in print of attempting a fraud on the public funds, a quarrel that cost Paine his post as secretary to the Committee for Foreign Affairs in January 1779. In the tenth Crisis Paine says that Deane rose like a rocket and fell like a stick, calls him a plodding, plotting, cringing mercenary who had turned to treason in France, and says his name, like Arnold’s, ought to be forgotten.

Lord Shelburne

Britain’s first minister in 1782; first appears Crisis XII

The Earl of Shelburne, head of the British ministry that made peace. The twelfth Crisis answers a speech in which he said that whenever Parliament acknowledged American independence the sun of England’s glory would set, and that he wished he could plead before Congress. Paine replies that independence is the only way out of the war and that America cannot be pleaded back into dependence.

Sir Guy Carleton

British commander at New York in 1782; first appears Supernumerary Crisis (To Sir Guy Carleton)

In May 1782 Paine addresses a Supernumerary Crisis to the new British commander in New York. Captain Huddy of the New Jersey militia had been taken from prison and hanged by loyalist refugees under a Captain Lippincott, and Captain Asgill of the Guards had been chosen by lot to die in retaliation. Paine asks Carleton to give up the murderer and save his own officer.

William the Conqueror

Paine’s example of a king’s origins; first appears Common Sense, Of Monarchy and Hereditary Succession

In Common Sense the Norman Conquest shows how the English crown began: a French bastard landing with an armed banditti and making himself king against the consent of the natives. Paine uses him to prove that the antiquity of English monarchy will not bear looking into, and turns him against the parent-country argument: if descent decided government, England ought to be governed by France.
