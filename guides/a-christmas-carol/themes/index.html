Whether a person can change

The book claims that a settled character can be remade in one night, a change most Victorian novels take hundreds of pages over. Dickens makes it believable by letting the evidence build up, and by ending with actions: the turkey, the raise, the dinner at Fred’s.

Through the book Scrooge argues with Marley, weeps with the first spirit, is rebuked by the second, and by the last is on his knees asking whether what he has seen is fixed. The last stave answers with what he does.

In the text

There’s more of gravy than of grave about you, whatever you are!

Scrooge, Stave I

Where Scrooge starts. He meets a ghost with a joke and an argument, so the change that follows cannot be put down to fright alone.

Are these the shadows of the things that Will be, or are they shadows of the things that May be only?

Scrooge, Stave IV

Scrooge puts the theme as a question: is the future fixed, or can it still be altered? The spirit gives no answer, so the answer has to come from what he does next.

Scrooge was better than his word. He did it all, and infinitely more; and to Tiny Tim, who did not die, he was a second father.

Stave V

The proof is given in deeds. The narrator reports what Scrooge did and what became of Tiny Tim, and says nothing of how Scrooge felt.

Poverty and responsibility

Dickens attacks a habit of thought more than wealth itself. Scrooge objects to charity because institutions already exist, and the book takes him to the places where the institutional answer runs out.

Through the book The collectors are refused in Stave I; the same language is turned round on Scrooge in Stave III; Ignorance and Want make the argument visible before Stave IV shows what the refusal costs.

In the text

“Are there no prisons?” asked Scrooge.

Scrooge, Stave I

Scrooge’s case in four words: the poor are already provided for, so nothing more can be asked of him.

“Are there no prisons?” said the Spirit, turning on him for the last time with his own words. “Are there no workhouses?”

The Ghost of Christmas Present, Stave III

The spirit puts the same question back to him while Scrooge is looking at two ragged children, and the answer he gave the collectors no longer serves.

Memory and time

The three spirits are three tenses, and the book’s structure is a grammar lesson. The past gives him evidence: Scrooge weeps at the schoolboy because he has been given the one point of view he has always avoided, his own.

Through the book Past explains, present accuses, future threatens; Stave V gathers all three into a single morning in which Scrooge says he will live in all of them.

In the text

“The school is not quite deserted,” said the Ghost. “A solitary child, neglected by his friends, is left there still.”

The Ghost of Christmas Past, Stave II

The past begins with Scrooge himself, a boy left at school over the holidays. He says he knows it, and sobs.

I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year. I will live in the Past, the Present, and the Future. The Spirits of all Three shall strive within me.

Scrooge, Stave IV

Scrooge promises to live in the past, the present and the future together, the three times the spirits have just taken him through.

Business and the meaning of a word

Marley’s correction of one noun is the turning point of the whole book. Scrooge and Marley have treated business as a trade; the ghost redefines it as everything a person is answerable for, and the rest of the story puts the new definition to work on a turkey, a coal-box and a salary.

Through the book Stated as doctrine by Marley, dramatised by Fezziwig as something an employer may actually do, and settled in Stave V when Scrooge raises a wage.

In the text

“Business!” cried the Ghost, wringing its hands again. “Mankind was my business. The common welfare was my business; charity, mercy, forbearance, and benevolence were, all, my business.

Marley’s Ghost, Stave I

Scrooge has just praised Marley as “a good man of business”. Marley takes the word and widens it until it covers everything one person owes to others.

He has the power to render us happy or unhappy; to make our service light or burdensome; a pleasure or a toil.

Scrooge, Stave II

Scrooge defends his old master and, without meaning to, describes the power he now holds over Bob Cratchit. A moment later he wishes he could say a word to his clerk.

Christmas and community

Christmas in the book is a set of open doors: dinners, parties, toasts, games, the ship’s crew and the lighthouse keepers. Its power over Scrooge is that it is happening whether or not he attends, and the spirits’ argument is largely just to show him the attendance.

Through the book Fred’s speech puts the case; Stave III proves it across an absurd range of places; Stave IV shows the alternative, a death nobody feels obliged to attend.

In the text

a good time; a kind, forgiving, charitable, pleasant time; the only time I know of, in the long calendar of the year, when men and women seem by one consent to open their shut-up hearts freely

Fred, Stave I

Fred’s case for Christmas is that it is the one time of year when people open themselves to each other. Scrooge ends the conversation by repeating “Good afternoon” until Fred leaves.

“God bless us every one!” said Tiny Tim, the last of all.

Tiny Tim, Stave III

The Cratchits’ blessing leaves nobody out, and Scrooge is standing unseen in the room when it is said.

And so, as Tiny Tim observed, “God Bless Us, Every One!”

Stave V

The book ends on the same words, this time in the narrator’s voice and with Scrooge among the people they cover.

Isolation

Scrooge’s loneliness is presented as something he has worked at, warning human sympathy to keep its distance. The cruellest stroke is that the future he is shown is simply more of the same, with no one missing him.

Through the book The solitary oyster of Stave I becomes the solitary child of Stave II and the unattended body of Stave IV; the pattern is one life shown three times.

In the text

Hard and sharp as flint, from which no steel had ever struck out generous fire; secret, and self-contained, and solitary as an oyster.

Stave I

The first portrait of Scrooge is a list of ways of being shut: secret, self-contained, solitary.

“It is required of every man,” the Ghost returned, “that the spirit within him should walk abroad among his fellow-men, and travel far and wide; and, if that spirit goes not forth in life, it is condemned to do so after death.

Marley’s Ghost, Stave I

Marley names the duty Scrooge has avoided, which is to go out among other people, and the penalty for avoiding it.
