A Christmas Carol · Charles Dickens

Themes

Whether a person can change

The book stakes everything on a claim most Victorian fiction makes slowly: that a settled character can be remade in one night. Dickens protects the claim by letting the evidence build up, and by ending with actions: the turkey, the raise, the dinner at Fred’s.

Scrooge argues with Marley, weeps with the first spirit, is rebuked by the second, and by the last is on his knees asking whether what he has seen is fixed. The last stave answers in errands.

Marley was dead, to begin with. There is no doubt whatever about that.

Stave I

There’s more of gravy than of grave about you, whatever you are!

Scrooge, Stave I

Poverty and responsibility

Dickens attacks a habit of thought more than wealth itself. Scrooge objects to charity because institutions already exist, and the book takes him to the places where the institutional answer runs out.

The collectors are refused in Stave I; the same language is turned round on Scrooge in Stave III; Ignorance and Want make the argument visible before Stave IV shows what the refusal costs.

“Are there no prisons?” asked Scrooge.

Scrooge, Stave I

“If they would rather die,” said Scrooge, “they had better do it, and decrease the surplus population.

Scrooge, Stave I

Memory and time

The three spirits are three tenses, and the book’s structure is a grammar lesson. The past gives him evidence: Scrooge weeps at the schoolboy because he has been given the one point of view he has always avoided, his own.

Past explains, present accuses, future threatens; Stave V gathers all three into a single morning in which Scrooge says he will live in all of them.

“The school is not quite deserted,” said the Ghost. “A solitary child, neglected by his friends, is left there still.”

The Ghost of Christmas Past, Stave II

“It matters little,” she said softly. “To you, very little. Another idol has displaced me; and, if it can cheer and comfort you in time to come as I would have tried to do, I have no just cause to grieve.”

Belle, Stave II

Business and the meaning of a word

Marley’s correction of one noun is the hinge of the whole book. Scrooge and Marley have treated business as a trade; the ghost redefines it as everything a person is answerable for, and the rest of the story puts the new definition to work on a turkey, a coal-box and a salary.

Stated as doctrine by Marley, dramatised by Fezziwig as something an employer may actually do, and settled in Stave V when Scrooge raises a wage.

He carried his own low temperature always about with him; he iced his office in the dog-days, and didn’t thaw it one degree at Christmas.

Stave I

a good time; a kind, forgiving, charitable, pleasant time; the only time I know of, in the long calendar of the year, when men and women seem by one consent to open their shut-up hearts freely

Fred, Stave I

Christmas and community

Christmas in the book is a set of open doors: dinners, parties, toasts, games, the ship’s crew and the lighthouse keepers. Its power over Scrooge is that it is happening whether or not he attends, and the spirits’ argument is largely just to show him the attendance.

Fred’s speech puts the case; Stave III proves it across an absurd range of places; Stave IV shows the alternative, a death nobody feels obliged to attend.

a good time; a kind, forgiving, charitable, pleasant time; the only time I know of, in the long calendar of the year, when men and women seem by one consent to open their shut-up hearts freely

Fred, Stave I

“Come in!” exclaimed the Ghost. “Come in! and know me better, man!”

The Ghost of Christmas Present, Stave III

Isolation

Scrooge’s loneliness is presented as something he has worked at, warning human sympathy to keep its distance. The cruellest stroke is that the future he is shown is simply more of the same, with no one missing him.

The solitary oyster of Stave I becomes the solitary child of Stave II and the unattended body of Stave IV; the pattern is one life shown three times.

Hard and sharp as flint, from which no steel had ever struck out generous fire; secret, and self-contained, and solitary as an oyster.
