A Christmas Carol · Charles Dickens

Context

Dates

1812
Dickens born in Portsmouth The family’s later collapse into debt gave him the clerk’s-eye view of London money that runs through the novella.
1834
Poor Law Amendment Act The workhouse system Scrooge names so confidently when he is asked for a donation.
1843
Dickens speaks in Manchester He addressed the Athenaeum there in October, on education and poverty, weeks before beginning the book.
1843
A Christmas Carol published Chapman and Hall issued it on 19 December as a gift book with gilt edges and John Leech’s illustrations; the production costs cut deeply into Dickens’s profit.
1844
Stage versions within weeks Edward Stirling’s adaptation opened at the Adelphi Theatre in February, one of several unauthorised dramatisations.
1844
Dickens publishes The Chimes Dickens follows the Carol with the second of five Christmas books, angrier and more directly political.
1853
Dickens begins reading the Carol in public The first readings were given in Birmingham at the end of the year, and he went on performing an abridged text for the rest of his life.
1870
Dickens dies at Gad’s Hill By then the public readings had made Scrooge a role as much as a character.
1901
R. W. Paul’s film Scrooge; or, Marley’s Ghost R. W. Paul’s short film, among the earliest screen versions of any Dickens work.
1951
Alastair Sim stars in the film Scrooge Brian Desmond Hurst’s British film became one of the standard screen reference points for the part.
1992
The Muppet Christmas Carol Brian Henson’s film with Michael Caine keeps much of Dickens’s narration by putting Gonzo on screen as the author.

Places

City of London
Scrooge’s counting house and the Exchange, where Stave IV opens among merchants discussing a funeral.
Cornhill
Bob Cratchit slides down the ice here twenty times on his way home on Christmas Eve.
Camden Town
The Cratchits’ house, and the address the prize turkey has to be sent to by cab.
Manchester
Dickens spoke at the Athenaeum here in October 1843, shortly before writing the book.
Portsmouth
Dickens’s birthplace.

Critics

William Makepeace Thackeray

“A Box of Novels”, in Fraser’s Magazine (1844)

Thackeray brushed aside the objections of other reviewers and called the Carol “a national benefit, and to every man or woman who reads it a personal kindness”.

Edmund Wilson

“Dickens: The Two Scrooges”, in The Wound and the Bow (1941)

Wilson argued that Dickens’s work is divided between generosity and a darker, aggressive side, and that Scrooge’s sudden conversion is not psychologically convincing. The “two Scrooges” of his title are the two sides of Dickens himself.

Paul Davis

The Lives and Times of Ebenezer Scrooge (1990)

Davis follows the Carol through its adaptations on stage, screen and in print, and calls it a “culture-text”: a story known mostly through retellings, which each generation changes to suit itself.
