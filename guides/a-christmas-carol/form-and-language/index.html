A Christmas Carol · Charles Dickens

Form and language

Direct address

A narrator who speaks openly to the reader, arguing, joking and insisting.

The book opens by haggling with the reader about a simile (what exactly is dead about a doornail) and only then explains that the point must be conceded or nothing wonderful can follow.

Marley was dead, to begin with. There is no doubt whatever about that.

Stave I

Simile

An explicit comparison using like or as.

Scrooge is hard as flint, solitary as an oyster and dead as a doornail by proxy; in the last stave the same device returns inverted, as a feather, an angel, a schoolboy and a drunken man in one breath.

Hard and sharp as flint, from which no steel had ever struck out generous fire; secret, and self-contained, and solitary as an oyster.

Stave I

Allegory

Abstractions given bodies and names so an argument can be seen.

Ignorance and Want are children with names on them, and Marley’s chain is a balance sheet made wearable; the technique lets Dickens put a social argument on stage without stopping the story.

“I wear the chain I forged in life,” replied the Ghost. “I made it link by link, and yard by yard; I girded it on of my own free will, and of my own free will I wore it.

Marley's Ghost, Stave I

Gothic machinery

Bells, darkness, apparitions, graves and locked rooms used to produce dread.

Every Gothic device here does moral work: the knocker, the clanking chain and the shrouded figure all produce evidence as well as atmosphere.

There’s more of gravy than of grave about you, whatever you are!

Scrooge, Stave I

Structural repetition

The same shape repeated with variations so that changes register.

Three spirits, three visitations, three refusals in Stave I answered by three acts of generosity in Stave V; the book is built so the reader can hear the rhyme.

“If they would rather die,” said Scrooge, “they had better do it, and decrease the surplus population.

Scrooge, Stave I

Cold imagery

Weather and temperature used to describe a person.

Scrooge carries his own low temperature about with him, ices his office in the dog-days and cannot be chilled by any wind. The metaphor is established before a single ghost appears.

Hard and sharp as flint, from which no steel had ever struck out generous fire; secret, and self-contained, and solitary as an oyster.

Stave I
