A Christmas Carol · Charles Dickens

Characters

Ebenezer Scrooge

Moneylender, surviving partner of Scrooge and Marley; first appears Stave I

A man whose coldness is described as a climate he carries about with him. Dickens gives him wit, arguing with his own hallucination about undigested beef, which is why his conversion can be believed: the intelligence was never missing, only the use he put it to.

Jacob Marley

Scrooge’s dead partner; first appears Stave I

Seven years dead and still doing the accounts. He is punished for a narrow idea of business more than for any cruelty, and his chain is made of the objects of his trade, so we see the moral before anyone states it.

Bob Cratchit

Scrooge’s clerk; first appears Stave I

Fifteen shillings a week, a white comforter instead of a greatcoat, and a coal-box he is not allowed to reach. Dickens is careful to show him sliding on the ice at Cornhill on the way home, so that his goodness reads as more than the patience of a man too tired to object.

Tiny Tim

Bob Cratchit’s youngest son; first appears Stave III

He has very few lines and one of them has escaped the book entirely. Dickens stops him being merely pathetic by giving him a thought of his own about being seen in church: he has decided how to use the fact that people notice him.

Fred

Scrooge’s nephew; first appears Stave I

He makes the case for Christmas in one long unbroken sentence and admits within it that the season has never earned him a penny, which is the strongest argument available. He keeps inviting his uncle to dinner every year and is refused every year, and goes on asking.

Belle

The woman Scrooge was engaged to; first appears Stave II

She releases him in the gentlest possible terms and the terms are final: they made their contract when they were both poor, he is a different man now, and she doubts he would choose her today. She is right, and he cannot answer.

Mr Fezziwig

The master Scrooge was apprenticed to; first appears Stave II

An employer who spends a few pounds on a party and buys an amount of happiness that Scrooge, watching, can only describe as a fortune. He is there to prove that there is another way of doing business, inside commerce itself.

The Ghost of Christmas Past

The first of the three spirits; first appears Stave II

A figure that is old and young together, with a jet of light from its head that Scrooge keeps trying to cover. It offers no interpretation of anything it shows, which is the method: the scenes are left to do their own arguing.

The Ghost of Christmas Present

The second of the three spirits; first appears Stave III

A giant among heaped food who greets Scrooge as a man to be known better, and then spends the stave withholding comfort. He gives Scrooge’s own words about the surplus population back to him unaltered, and finishes by opening his robe on two children he insists belong to mankind.

The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come

The third and last of the spirits; first appears Stave IV

A shrouded shape that never speaks and only points. Because it supplies no commentary, every conclusion in the stave has to be reached by Scrooge himself, which is why the stave frightens him more than the chained ghost did.
