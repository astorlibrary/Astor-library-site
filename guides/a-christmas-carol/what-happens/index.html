A Christmas Carol · Charles Dickens

What happens

Stave I Marley's Ghost

Scrooge is defined entirely by what he turns away: his nephew’s invitation, two charity collectors, his clerk’s fire. Then his dead partner arrives dragging the cash-boxes and ledgers he accumulated instead of a life, and promises three more visitors.

Counting house
Fred offers dinner and is refused; the charity collectors are told about prisons and the surplus population.
The knocker
Marley’s face appears in the door knocker, and Scrooge explains it away by arguing with his own digestion.
Marley’s chain
The ghost states the book’s thesis, that mankind should have been his business, and sets out the terms of the visitations.

Stave II The First of the Three Spirits

The Ghost of Christmas Past shows scenes Scrooge cannot alter and does not dispute: the boy left at school, Fezziwig’s ball, and Belle releasing him from an engagement he has already broken in everything but name. The spirit never argues; it simply declines to stop.

The schoolroom
A solitary child left behind at the holidays, and an old man crying at the sight of himself.
Fezziwig’s ball
An employer who spends a small sum and buys enormous happiness, which Scrooge himself points out.
Belle’s release
She names the golden idol that has taken her place, and wonders whether he would choose her now.

Stave III The Second of the Three Spirits

The present is shown as a series of doors opening: the Cratchits’ dinner in Camden Town, Fred’s party, miners, lighthouse keepers and sailors all keeping the day. The spirit quotes Scrooge’s own words about the surplus population back at him, and then lifts his robe on two starved children.

The Cratchits’ dinner
A small goose made to go round, and Tiny Tim’s blessing, immediately shadowed by the spirit’s answer about the empty seat.
Fred’s party
Scrooge watches himself become a parlour game and laughs before he can stop himself.
Ignorance and Want
Two children under the robe, named as Man’s and not the spirit’s, with a warning about which of them is the more dangerous.

Stave IV The Last of the Spirits

The final spirit says nothing at all, so Scrooge has to do the interpreting himself: businessmen bargaining over whether to attend a cheap funeral, a charwoman selling bed-curtains stripped from a corpse, an unwatched body, and a family relieved by a death. Only at the grave is the name supplied.

On ’Change
Merchants treat a death as a minor scheduling question, and Scrooge listens without recognising the subject.
Old Joe’s shop
The dead man’s curtains, shirt and blankets are haggled over by the people who were nearest him at the end.
The churchyard
Scrooge wants to know whether these are shadows of what will be or only of what may be, and reads his own name on the stone.

Stave V The End of It

The bed-curtains are still hanging, which is the proof Scrooge wants. The last stave is made of errands: a turkey bought, a donation whispered, a dinner attended, a salary raised. Dickens will not let the change stand as a private feeling.

Christmas morning
Scrooge laughs, sends the prize turkey to Camden Town anonymously, and pays for the cab.
The street
He finds the charity collector of the day before and names a sum the text never discloses to the reader.
The counting house
Bob Cratchit arrives eighteen and a half minutes late, is threatened with a raise, and is told to buy more coal.
