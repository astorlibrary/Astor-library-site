The Rime of the Ancient Mariner · Samuel Taylor Coleridge

Context

Dates

1726
George Shelvocke’s A Voyage Round the World published Its account of a rough passage round Cape Horn includes an officer shooting a black albatross in the hope of better weather. Wordsworth later recalled suggesting the incident to Coleridge.
1772
Samuel Taylor Coleridge born at Ottery St Mary, Devon The youngest child of the vicar and schoolmaster of the town; he was sent to school at Christ’s Hospital in London after his father’s death.
1773
Cook’s Resolution crosses the Antarctic Circle The expedition’s astronomer, William Wales, afterwards taught mathematics at Christ’s Hospital while Coleridge was a pupil there, a possible route by which descriptions of polar ice reached the poem.
1797
The poem is planned on a November walk in Somerset Coleridge, living at Nether Stowey, set out with William and Dorothy Wordsworth from Alfoxden towards the coast. A joint ballad was proposed to pay for the trip, and soon became Coleridge’s alone.
1798
Finished in March and published in Lyrical Ballads The anonymous volume, printed in Bristol, opened with ‘The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere’ in deliberately antique spelling, prefaced by a short prose Argument.
1800
Revised for the second edition of Lyrical Ballads The spelling was modernised, the title shortened, the poem moved from the front of the volume, and Wordsworth added a note listing what he took to be its defects alongside its merits.
1816
Coleridge moves to Highgate He went to live in the household of the surgeon James Gillman, seeking help with his dependence on opium, and stayed for the rest of his life.
1817
Sibylline Leaves adds the marginal gloss The poem appeared under Coleridge’s name for the first time, with a Latin epigraph from Thomas Burnet and a prose commentary in the margin. Biographia Literaria, with his retrospective account of the Lyrical Ballads plan, came out the same year.
1818
Frankenstein quotes the poem Mary Shelley’s novel gives Walton an allusion to the albatross and has Victor recite the stanza of the lonesome road, an early sign of the poem’s hold on the Gothic imagination.
1834
Final lifetime text; Coleridge dies at Highgate The last collected Poetical Works he oversaw prints the version now standard. He died on 25 July.
1876
Gustave Doré’s illustrated edition Doré’s large engravings of the ice, the becalmed ship and the spectre-bark fixed the poem’s look for generations of readers.

Places

Nether Stowey, Somerset
Coleridge’s cottage from the end of 1796, where the poem was written.
Alfoxden, near Holford
The house William and Dorothy Wordsworth rented in 1797 and 1798, a few miles’ walk from Stowey; the November walk began here.
Watchet harbour
The small Bristol Channel port on the route of the 1797 walk, by local tradition the model for the harbour with its kirk, hill and lighthouse. The poem itself names no place.
Bristol
Where Joseph Cottle had Lyrical Ballads printed in 1798.
Cape Horn
The poem gives no bearings, but a ship driven south to the ice that then runs north into an unvisited sea follows the Horn route of Shelvocke’s voyage, and the gloss calls the second sea the Pacific.
Ottery St Mary, Devon
Coleridge’s birthplace, where his father was vicar.
Highgate, London
Coleridge’s home from 1816 until his death in 1834, and where the later revisions of the poem were made.
