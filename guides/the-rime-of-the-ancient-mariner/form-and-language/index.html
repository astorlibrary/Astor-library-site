The Rime of the Ancient Mariner · Samuel Taylor Coleridge

Form and language

Frame narrative

A story told inside another story, so that every account reaches the reader through somebody with an interest in it.

The voyage is enclosed in an encounter on a road outside a wedding. The frame breaks in whenever the Wedding-Guest can bear no more, and it supplies the ending: the last two stanzas end on him. After 1817 a second frame, the prose gloss in the margin, tells the story again in a confident scholarly voice that knows more, or claims to, than the verse does.

It is an ancient Mariner, And he stoppeth one of three.

Part I

Ballad stanza

A four-line stanza alternating four-stress and three-stress lines, rhymed on the second and fourth, inherited from traditional sung narrative.

Coleridge keeps the old measure for speed and plainness and then breaks it for effect. When the storm drives the ship, when the spectre-bark closes in, when the fiend treads behind on the lonesome road, the stanza swells to five or six lines, so that the reader waits longer than expected for the rhyme to close.

It is an ancient Mariner, And he stoppeth one of three.

Part I

Internal rhyme

A rhyme between the middle and the end of a single line.

Many of the long lines chime at the half-way point: guests met and feast set, the ice that did split with a thunder-fit, the first that ever burst. The effect is a gallop in the passages of motion and, in the becalmed passages, a kind of locked echo in which the line seems unable to get away from itself.

For all averred, I had killed the bird That made the breeze to blow.

Part II

Refrain

A phrase brought back at intervals, so that its meaning shifts with what has happened since.

Repetition works at every scale, from doubled words within a line to whole stanzas that return altered. The sun that came up upon the left in Part I rises upon the right in Part II, which is how the poem says the ship has rounded the ice; the lighthouse, hill and kirk of the departure come back in reverse order at the homecoming; and the word ‘alone’ is struck four times in two lines.

For all averred, I had killed the bird That made the breeze to blow.

Part II

Archaism

The deliberate use of words and forms already old-fashioned when the work was written.

Words such as eftsoons, swound, gramercy, clomb and I wist give the poem the air of something found more than written, and the 1798 text went further with antique spelling throughout. Coleridge pared much of this away in revision, but kept enough that the tale seems to come from no particular century, which suits a teller who is himself out of time.

It is an ancient Mariner, And he stoppeth one of three.

Part I

Simile

A comparison made explicit with “like” or “as”, so that the two things are held side by side.

The similes repeatedly reach away from the sea towards the land the Mariner has lost: a hidden brook in the leafy month of June, a meadow-gale of spring, April hoar-frost. In others the ship becomes a picture or a prison, as with the painted ship and the sun peering through a dungeon-grate. The most famous of them, the man on the lonesome road, describes fear by refusing to look at its cause.

He holds him with his glittering eye-- The Wedding-Guest stood still, And listens like a three years child: The Mariner hath his will.

Part I

Ambiguous symbols

An object placed at the centre of a work whose meaning is never settled by it.

The albatross is hailed as a soul, credited with the wind, blamed for the fog, hung in the place of a cross and mourned by a spirit, all by characters with reasons of their own. The verse itself never says what the bird means, only what is done with it. Readings that make it Christ, nature, the imagination or an innocent stranger are all continuing the crew’s habit of deciding after the fact.

As if it had been a Christian soul, We hailed it in God's name.
