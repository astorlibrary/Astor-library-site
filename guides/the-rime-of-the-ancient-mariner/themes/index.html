The Rime of the Ancient Mariner · Samuel Taylor Coleridge

Themes

The crime

The shooting is given one clause and no reason, and the poem never goes back to supply one. That omission is the centre of the work: guilt here attaches to a man with no examined intention behind it, and has to be carried, first as a dead bird and then as a story. The crew’s guilt is different in kind, a matter of approving after the event, and is punished more severely.

Stated in a line at the end of Part I, made visible as a burden at the end of Part II, lifted from the neck in Part IV, and shown in Part VII to have moved inwards, where it returns at uncertain hours.

"God save thee, ancient Mariner! From the fiends, that plague thee thus!-- Why look'st thou so?"--With my cross-bow I shot the ALBATROSS.

Part I

For all averred, I had killed the bird That made the breeze to blow.

Part II

Nature

The same sea creatures appear twice. In Part II they are slimy things crawling on a rotting sea, and in Part IV they are water-snakes in blue, green and velvet black, shedding light as they rear. Nothing about them has altered except the way they are looked at, and the poem locates the Mariner’s release in that change of looking. The sun in this poem tends to preside over suffering and the moon over relief, a pattern too consistent to be accidental and too loose to be a code.

From the bird treated as a guest and then as a target in Part I, through disgust in Part II and unwilled love in Part IV, to the closing claim that prayer and the love of creatures are one act.

As if it had been a Christian soul, We hailed it in God's name.

Part I

We were the first that ever burst Into that silent sea.

Part II

Isolation

The Mariner’s punishment is solitude in company: two hundred bodies on deck, every one with its eyes open, and nobody to speak to. The poem treats loneliness as a spiritual condition as well as a physical one, since at its worst he cannot pray either. His later longing is touchingly modest. He does not ask for the wedding feast, only to walk to church in a crowd.

Introduced when the ship breaks into a sea nobody has entered, brought to its extreme in Part IV, and carried home in Part VII, where the man who praises company passes like night from land to land and belongs nowhere.

We were the first that ever burst Into that silent sea.

Part II

Day after day, day after day, We stuck, nor breath nor motion; As idle as a painted ship Upon a painted ocean.

Part II

Penance

The poem borrows the vocabulary of confession, with its shrieving and penance and a hermit to hear it, and then declines to complete the sacrament. The albatross falls off, the curse is snapped and the ship comes home, yet a voice has already said that more penance is to come, and the Hermit gives a question instead of absolution. Release in this poem is real but partial, and it is never declared finished.

First relief in Part IV; the verdict of the second voice at the end of Part V; the hope of being shrieved at the end of Part VI; and in Part VII the discovery that telling the tale frees him only until the next time.

O happy living things! no tongue Their beauty might declare: A spring of love gushed from my heart, And I blessed them unaware

Part IV

The self same moment I could pray; And from my neck so free The Albatross fell off, and sank Like lead into the sea.

Part IV

Storytelling

The poem is about being told a story as much as about the story. It opens on a listener being stopped and closes on what listening has done to him, and in between the teller explains that he does not choose his moments or his audience. Storytelling is therefore shown from both ends as something undergone. It is a penance for the speaker and, the last stanza suggests, an injury as well as an education for the hearer.

The frame is set in the first stanza, interrupts the voyage at its most frightening points in Parts I, IV and V, and takes over completely in Part VII, where the origin and mechanism of the compulsion are finally given.

It is an ancient Mariner, And he stoppeth one of three.

Part I

He holds him with his glittering eye-- The Wedding-Guest stood still, And listens like a three years child: The Mariner hath his will.

Part I

Prayer and providence

The Mariner’s world is thick with Christian reference, from vespers and the Virgin to the holy rood and guardian saints, and he reads his rescue as grace. But the poem also shows his fate settled by a throw of dice between two spectres, and a sea so lonely that God himself scarcely seemed to be there. It holds both pictures without choosing, which is why it has been read as an orthodox parable and as a nightmare with a parable pinned to it.

The bird is greeted in God’s name in Part I; the dice fall in Part III; prayer fails and then succeeds in Part IV; and Part VII ends with a creed whose comfort the listener’s condition does not quite confirm.

As if it had been a Christian soul, We hailed it in God's name.

Part I

Instead of the cross, the Albatross About my neck was hung.
