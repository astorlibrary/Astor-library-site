The crime

The shooting is given one clause and no reason, and the poem never goes back to supply one. That omission is the centre of the work: guilt here attaches to a man with no examined intention behind it, and has to be carried, first as a dead bird and then as a story. The crew’s guilt is different in kind, a matter of approving after the event, and is punished more severely.

Through the poem Stated in a line at the end of Part I, made visible as a burden at the end of Part II, lifted from the neck in Part IV, and shown in Part VII to have moved inwards, where it returns at uncertain hours.

In the text

“God save thee, ancient Mariner! From the fiends, that plague thee thus!— Why look’st thou so?”—With my cross-bow I shot the ALBATROSS.

Part I

The confession answers a question about his expression, and it answers ‘why’ with ‘what’. The instrument comes first and the verb is the plainest available. There is no ‘because’, then or afterwards. Coleridge places the act at the very end of the part, after a stanza of moonshine and vespers, so it comes as abruptly as the shot, and the capitals let the word ring.

For all averred, I had killed the bird That made the breeze to blow.

Part II

‘Averred’ is a word for sworn testimony, and the crew use it twice, a stanza apart, for opposite findings: that he killed the bird that brought the breeze, and that he killed the bird that brought the fog. Their morality is a weather report. The poem treats the second verdict as the fatal one, since approving the act afterwards is the only thing the two hundred ever do to deserve what follows.

Nature

The same sea creatures appear twice. In Part II they are slimy things crawling on a rotting sea, and in Part IV they are water-snakes in blue, green and velvet black, shedding light as they rear. Nothing about them has altered except the way they are looked at, and the poem locates the Mariner’s release in that change of looking. The sun in this poem tends to preside over suffering and the moon over relief, a pattern too consistent to be accidental and too loose to be a code.

Through the poem From the bird treated as a guest and then as a target in Part I, through disgust in Part II and unwilled love in Part IV, to the closing claim that prayer and the love of creatures are one act.

In the text

As if it had been a Christian soul, We hailed it in God’s name.

Part I

The sailors greet the first creature they have met in the ice as one greets a person, and the ‘as if’ keeps the comparison honest: nobody claims the bird is a soul. What the line establishes is hospitality. The bird is received as a guest, fed, and called to, so that the shooting nine evenings later is a breach of welcome before it is anything more mysterious.

Water, water, every where, And all the boards did shrink; Water, water, every where, Nor any drop to drink.

Part II

The line most people know is usually misquoted, and the true second line is the more exact observation: salt water and sun are shrinking the planks of the ship itself, so the sea surrounds them and is also pulling their shelter apart. The stanza is a riddle of abundance as deprivation. It prepares for Part IV, where the Mariner is surrounded by life and cannot be part of it until he sees it differently.

Isolation

The Mariner’s punishment is solitude in company: two hundred bodies on deck, every one with its eyes open, and nobody to speak to. The poem treats loneliness as a spiritual condition as well as a physical one, since at its worst he cannot pray either. His later longing is touchingly modest. He does not ask for the wedding feast, only to walk to church in a crowd.

Through the poem Introduced when the ship breaks into a sea nobody has entered, brought to its extreme in Part IV, and carried home in Part VII, where the man who praises company passes like night from land to land and belongs nowhere.

In the text

Alone, alone, all, all alone, Alone on a wide wide sea! And never a saint took pity on My soul in agony.

Part IV

The first two lines contain almost nothing but one word and its near-rhymes: ‘alone’, ‘all’, ‘a’. Long open vowels and the doubled ‘wide wide’ stretch the line out to the horizon it describes. The loneliness is then made vertical as well as horizontal, since no saint looks down either. He is not literally alone, as the next stanza cruelly points out; he has two hundred companions on deck.

I pass, like night, from land to land; I have strange power of speech

Part VII

He compares himself to night, the thing that fell at one stride when the crew died, and the comparison makes him a natural force that arrives everywhere and stays nowhere. ‘Strange power of speech’ is as close as the poem comes to describing itself. Coleridge, a famous talker who held rooms for hours, gives the gift of eloquence to a man for whom it is a symptom.

Penance

The poem borrows the vocabulary of confession, with its shrieving and penance and a hermit to hear it, and then declines to complete the sacrament. The albatross falls off, the curse is snapped and the ship comes home, yet a voice has already said that more penance is to come, and the Hermit gives a question instead of absolution. Release in this poem is real but partial, and it is never declared finished.

Through the poem First relief in Part IV; the verdict of the second voice at the end of Part V; the hope of being shrieved at the end of Part VI; and in Part VII the discovery that telling the tale frees him only until the next time.

In the text

The self same moment I could pray; And from my neck so free The Albatross fell off, and sank Like lead into the sea.

Part IV

Prayer is a sign of the release and does not cause it: the blessing came first and was not a prayer at all. The bird, which flew and perched and came to a call, leaves the poem as dead weight, and ‘like lead’ will be used once more, of the ship itself going down in Part VII. Both burdens sink, and neither sinking ends the matter, which is why this stanza closes a part and the poem goes on.

Quoth he, “The man hath penance done, And penance more will do.”

Part V

The sentence is passed in his hearing but not to his face, by speakers who are never identified, and it is the most exact statement of his condition in the poem: penance in the past tense and the future tense, with no present in which it is complete. The soft voice, as soft as honey-dew, delivers the harder news. Everything in Parts VI and VII should be read under this line.

Storytelling

The poem is about being told a story as much as about the story. It opens on a listener being stopped and closes on what listening has done to him, and in between the teller explains that he does not choose his moments or his audience. Storytelling is therefore shown from both ends as something undergone. It is a penance for the speaker and, the last stanza suggests, an injury as well as an education for the hearer.

Through the poem The frame is set in the first stanza, interrupts the voyage at its most frightening points in Parts I, IV and V, and takes over completely in Part VII, where the origin and mechanism of the compulsion are finally given.

In the text

It is an ancient Mariner, And he stoppeth one of three.

Part I

‘It is’, not ‘there was’: the present tense puts the Mariner in front of us as something encountered, almost as a thing, and the same tense will return at the end when we learn he is still doing this. ‘One of three’ is never explained. Two men walk on into the wedding, and the poem’s first act is a selection without a stated reason, which prepares for a story whose central act also has no stated reason.

Since then, at an uncertain hour, That agony returns; And till my ghastly tale is told, This heart within me burns.

Part VII

‘Uncertain’ removes the last comfort of routine: the penance has no calendar. The burning has moved from the sky and the sea of Part II into his chest, so the tropics are now internal. ‘Ghastly’ is the word he used of the dead crew at the ropes, and he applies it to his own story. Telling brings relief, which means every listener is being used as medicine, and the Wedding-Guest is only the latest dose.

Prayer and providence

The Mariner’s world is thick with Christian reference, from vespers and the Virgin to the holy rood and guardian saints, and he reads his rescue as grace. But the poem also shows his fate settled by a throw of dice between two spectres, and a sea so lonely that God himself scarcely seemed to be there. It holds both pictures without choosing, which is why it has been read as an orthodox parable and as a nightmare with a parable pinned to it.

Through the poem The bird is greeted in God’s name in Part I; the dice fall in Part III; prayer fails and then succeeds in Part IV; and Part VII ends with a creed whose comfort the listener’s condition does not quite confirm.

In the text

Her lips were red, her looks were free, Her locks were yellow as gold: Her skin was as white as leprosy, The Night-Mare LIFE-IN-DEATH was she, Who thicks man’s blood with cold.

Part III

The first two lines are the stock portrait of a ballad heroine, and the third spoils it with a single noun. Red, gold and white are the colours of beauty, and white is also the colour of the disease, so the portrait turns on its own palette. The stanza runs to five lines because the name needs a line to itself. ‘Thicks’ is used as a verb, an old usage that makes the cold seem to act inside the listener’s own veins.

He prayeth best, who loveth best All things both great and small; For the dear God who loveth us He made and loveth all.

Part VII

The rhythm goes nursery-simple and the diction could be a hymn for children, which is either the hard-won plainness of a man who has been to the bottom or a formula too small for what it follows. The poem gives grounds for both. The creed says God loves all; the narrative has shown two hundred men killed for a change of opinion. The stanza may comfort the speaker more than the listener.
