The Rime of the Ancient Mariner · Samuel Taylor Coleridge

Characters

The Ancient Mariner

Teller of the tale and its only survivor; first appears Part I

He has no name, no rank and no stated reason for the one act that defines him, and the poem is careful to keep it that way. What he has instead is an eye that fixes listeners, a body that frightens everybody who sees it, and a story he cannot choose not to tell. He is often read as a penitent who has learned his lesson; the verse also shows a man whose punishment has simply changed its form, from thirst to narration.

The Wedding-Guest

The listener, next of kin to the bridegroom; first appears Part I

He is the reader’s stand-in and the poem’s control experiment: a cheerful man with somewhere to be, who protests, beats his breast at the sound of the bassoon, and still cannot get up. His interruptions mark the moments at which the tale becomes hardest to bear, and his last state is the only evidence the poem offers of what the story does to a person who hears it.

The Albatross

The bird that comes through the fog; first appears Part I

In the verse it does very little: it arrives, eats what the sailors give it, circles the ship and perches on the rigging for nine evenings. Everything else is put on it by other people. The crew hail it as if it were a Christian soul, then decide it brought the wind, then decide it brought the fog; a spirit is said to have loved it; and it ends as a weight hung where a cross should be.

The crew

Two hundred shipmates, living and then dead; first appears Part I

They judge the killing by its results, condemning it while the luck holds and praising it when the sun comes out, and the poem makes that change of mind their share in the crime. Their deaths are nonetheless out of all proportion, which is one of the difficulties the poem leaves standing. After death they are its strangest image: bodies that work the ropes in silence, among them the Mariner’s own nephew, and whose open eyes go on cursing him.

Life-in-Death

The woman on the spectre-bark; first appears Part III

She is painted in the colours of a ballad beauty, red lips and yellow hair, with one detail wrong: skin as white as leprosy. Her name, which Coleridge added in revision, describes the sentence she wins for the Mariner, which is not to die. She speaks one line, of triumph at a throw of the dice, and that line is the nearest thing the poem offers to an account of how its justice is decided.

Death

Her companion on the skeleton ship; first appears Part III

He is hardly described at all in the late text, which cut the grisly portrait that the 1798 version gave him, so that he survives mainly as a question the Mariner asks on seeing the ship. He loses the throw for the Mariner and, by implication, takes the rest: two hundred men, who go down with a thump while the poem looks the other way at the rising moon.

The Polar Spirit

The spirit from the land of mist and snow; first appears Part II

The verse never names him; the title comes from the prose gloss of 1817. He is known only at second hand, through the crew’s dreams, which place him nine fathoms under the keel, and through two voices who say he loved the bird. He both avenges the albatross and, under orders the poem does not explain, carries the ship north as far as the equator, where his reach ends and the ship jolts free.

The Hermit

The holy man of the wood by the harbour; first appears Part VI

He is the poem’s picture of religion lived at ease in nature: he sings hymns of his own making, kneels on a mossy oak-stump and likes talking to sailors. The Mariner expects him to wash the blood away. What the Hermit actually does is cross himself and ask a question, and the answer to that question is the first telling of the tale, so the confessor, far from ending the compulsion, starts it.

The Pilot

The harbour pilot who rows out to the ship; first appears Part VI

An ordinary working man brought to the edge of the supernatural, he serves to measure how the Mariner now looks to the world he has come back to. He thinks the ship fiendish before he reaches it, and when the drowned-looking body in his boat moves its lips he shrieks and falls down in a fit.

The Pilot's boy

The lad in the rescue boat; first appears Part VI

He has the poem’s most disturbing line after the shooting itself. Watching the Mariner take the oars, he laughs long and loud with his eyes going to and fro, and says what the adults are too frightened or too pious to say. The Mariner adds, in passing and in the present tense, that the boy is mad now, which is what the homecoming has cost somebody who only came out in a boat.
