The Moonstone · Wilkie Collins

Characters

Gabriel Betteredge

House steward at Lady Verinder’s, and first narrator; first appears First Period, Chapter I

Seventy years old, in service since he was a footman, and entirely convinced that a household runs on precedence and that Robinson Crusoe will answer any question put to it. His prejudices are visible on every page, and that makes his evidence usable: the reader can see the shape of what he is leaving out.

Rachel Verinder

The owner of the diamond, on her eighteenth birthday; first appears First Period, Chapter IX

She is the only person in the book who knows a central fact from the first morning and refuses to give it up, and her silence is read by everyone as guilt, temper or debt. Collins gives her no narrative of her own, so she is constructed entirely out of other people’s accounts of a woman they have decided is behaving badly.

Franklin Blake

Lady Verinder’s nephew, and the compiler of the record; first appears First Period, Chapter I

He brings the diamond from London, commissions every narrative in the book, and turns out to be the man who took it. Educated in Germany, France and Italy, he argues with himself in three national manners, and his greatest discovery is that his own memory holds nothing at all of the most important night of his life.

Rosanna Spearman

Second housemaid, formerly a thief; first appears First Period, Chapter IV

Taken from a reformatory, deformed in one shoulder, and in love with a gentleman who does not notice she exists. She hides the paint-stained nightgown to protect him, is suspected because of her record, and walks into the Shivering Sand. Her letter, read fifty chapters later, is the most painful document in the book.

Sergeant Cuff

A detective officer from Scotland Yard; first appears First Period, Chapter XII

Grizzled, softly spoken and interested chiefly in roses, he reads a smear of paint faster than anybody and misreads a young woman completely. Collins makes him formidable and also wrong, which is unusual in the genre he helped invent: the great detective leaves the case with three predictions, two of which come true for reasons he never understood.

Drusilla Clack

A poor cousin, and the second period’s first narrator; first appears Second Period, First Narrative, Chapter I

She lives on a small income in Brittany, distributes tracts with titles like ‘Satan in the Hair Brush’, and writes her narrative for the fee. Everything she reports is accurate and every judgement she attaches to it is wrong, which makes her the clearest demonstration of Collins’s method: evidence and interpretation arriving in the same sentence and needing to be separated by the reader.

Godfrey Ablewhite

A barrister and celebrated charitable speaker; first appears First Period, Chapter X

The favourite of the Ladies’ committees, a large fair man who is kind to everybody and is living two lives at the expense of a trust he was supposed to hold. Collins makes him the one figure in the book whose public character is entirely manufactured, and the novel’s method (many witnesses, each partial) is what it takes to catch him.

Ezra Jennings

Mr. Candy’s assistant, and the designer of the experiment; first appears Second Period, Third Narrative, Chapter IX

Of mixed birth, piebald hair, dying of an incurable disease and ruined years ago by an accusation he will not describe, he is the only person who will try an idea out before asserting it. He reconstructs the birthday night as a controlled trial and insists that Rachel’s consent be freely given, and he asks for nothing except to be remembered.

Mathew Bruff

Solicitor, of Gray’s Inn Square; first appears Second Period, Second Narrative, Chapter I

He drew the simplest will of his career for Sir John and has kept the family’s secrets ever since, and his narrative supplies the legal machinery the plot turns on. He is also the proof that competence is no protection against prejudice: he explains Rachel’s troubles shrewdly and states in the same breath that hardly one woman in a thousand could manage a trust.

The three Indians

Brahmin guardians of the Moonstone, travelling as jugglers; first appears First Period, Chapter III

They appear at the gate, at a London bank and finally at Somnauth, and they are the only characters with a claim to the diamond that predates every English one. Collins filters them through Victorian conventions of mystery and disguise, and still gives them the last word: the stone goes home and the English lose sight of it for ever.

Mr. Murthwaite

A traveller in India, and the last witness; first appears First Period, Chapter X

He is the only Englishman in the book who can speak to the three Indians in their own language and the only one who states plainly what they will do to recover the stone. His statement closes the novel from Kattiawar, which puts the final act of interpretation outside the English record entirely.

Lady Julia Verinder

Rachel’s mother, mistress of the house; first appears First Period, Chapter II

She will not have her daughter questioned, sends Cuff away before he can go further, and dies before the case is reopened. Her judgement is the one the household runs on, and it is exercised in the teeth of the professional advice she is paying for.
