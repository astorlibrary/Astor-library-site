Testimony

Every narrator is instructed to write only what they saw, and every one of them breaks the rule by supplying motive. The novel is built so that the truth exists nowhere in a single document: it has to be assembled by a reader setting Betteredge’s prejudice against Clack’s piety against Bruff’s professional certainty.

Through the book Betteredge establishes the method and its weakness; Clack pushes unreliability to comedy; Jennings’s journal is the one narrative with nothing to defend, which is why it can solve the case.

In the text

In the first part of Robinson Crusoe, at page one hundred and twenty-nine, you will find it thus written:

First Period, Chapter I

A detective novel begins by citing another book by page number, and the citation is exact where everything else in Betteredge’s testimony will be approximate. Collins is announcing his method in the first line: this story will arrive as a document that quotes its sources.

he will refrain from mentioning any personal objection which he may entertain to this species of literature

Second Period, First Narrative, Chapter VI

The editor of the record appears inside the record, and the reader watches the dossier being assembled and trimmed. Blake’s courtesy is lethal (he declines to mention his personal objection while mentioning it), and the exchange tells us, in passing, that every narrative in the book has passed through one man’s hands.

Empire and possession

The stone is stolen twice (from Somnauth in the eleventh century and from Seringapatam in 1799) before it becomes an English birthday present. Collins attaches the adjective “Indian” to it in every drawing room and bank vault, so English law can hold the diamond but cannot naturalise it.

Through the book A private letter of accusation in the Prologue, a legacy designed as a weapon in the First Period, and a restitution in the Epilogue that the English narrators can record but not control.

In the text

the Mohammedan conqueror, Mahmoud of Ghizni, crossed India; seized on the holy city of Somnauth; and stripped of its treasures the famous temple, which had stood for centuries⁠—the shrine of Hindu pilgrimage, and the wonder of the Eastern world.

Prologue

The theft the English commit in 1799 is not the first: Collins puts an eleventh-century sack at the head of the book so that the stone arrives with a history of plunder already attached. The Epilogue returns to this same city, so the novel ends where the stone’s history began.

here was our quiet English house suddenly invaded by a devilish Indian Diamond⁠—bringing after it a conspiracy of living rogues, set loose on us by the vengeance of a dead man.

First Period, Chapter V

The sentence sets ‘quiet English house’ against ‘devilish Indian Diamond’ as though the two belonged to different worlds, which is the assumption the whole novel dismantles. The conspiracy of living rogues turns out to include an English barrister, and the vengeance of a dead man to be an English colonel’s.

Detection

Cuff reads objects brilliantly and people badly, and in the end the case is opened by an experiment more than by inference. Collins shows that clues belong to several disciplines (policing, medicine, law, household routine) and that a detective who owns only one of them will get it wrong.

Through the book Local incompetence, then professional inference, then a collapse of the professional theory, and finally a reconstruction under laboratory conditions.

In the text

One of these days (please God) I shall retire from catching thieves, and try my hand at growing roses.

First Period, Chapter XII

Collins gives his detective a private life before he gives him a theory, and the roses measure something: Cuff is at his most attentive and most accurate when the subject is not human. The same man will shortly read Rachel Verinder as a young woman in debt.

I looked back again at the letters. My own name. Plainly confronting me

Second Period, Third Narrative, Chapter III

Collins breaks the paragraph into fragments so that the sentence has to be read twice, which is what Blake is doing on the page. The buried evidence solves the mystery and destroys the investigator in the same instant, and the novel still has three hundred pages to run.

Class, service and suspicion

Rosanna is suspected because she was a thief; the servants are examined first; Betteredge measures every visitor by the manner in which the household should receive them. The book is unusually clear that a criminal record and a crooked shoulder are treated as evidence, while a reputation for charity is treated as an alibi.

Through the book The servants’ rooms are searched in the First Period; the man nobody suspects is exposed in the Sixth Narrative, by which time he has been dead for chapters.

In the text

trembling in a manner most remarkable to see, and which has given to it, among the people in our parts, the name of the Shivering Sand.

First Period, Chapter IV

The name comes from the local people, so the place is real but slightly outside ordinary jurisdiction. The Shivering Sand takes the place a Gothic castle once had: it swallows a woman, hides the only physical evidence, and gives it back a year later intact.

His gipsy-complexion, his fleshless cheeks, his gaunt facial bones, his dreamy eyes, his extraordinary parti-coloured hair, the puzzling contradiction between his face and figure which made him look old and young both together

Second Period, Third Narrative, Chapter IX

Blake catalogues the features that have made a whole town refuse to employ Jennings and admits that his own first impression is unfavourable. The doubleness (old and young, black hair and white) is the book’s picture of a man read wrongly by everybody, which is the condition Rachel and Rosanna are in too.

Memory and medicine

The solution requires a state in which a man acts with purpose and retains nothing. Collins builds it out of contemporary argument about opium, nervous disease and the conditions under which an experiment can be repeated, and he is careful that the drug proves nothing on its own: Jennings has to design, observe and interpret.

Through the book A dose given as a joke at the birthday dinner, a year of contradiction, and a second dose given deliberately with witnesses, consent and a written protocol.

In the text

The one effectual palliative in my case, is⁠—opium. To that all-potent and all-merciful drug I am indebted for a respite of many years from my sentence of death.

Second Period, Third Narrative, Chapter IX

The drug that lost Blake a night of his life is the same drug keeping Jennings alive, and the man who explains it is speaking from inside the experiment he is about to run. Collins, who took laudanum himself for years, presents opium as neither a poison nor a miracle: it is a treatment with a stated limit.

It leaves her entirely mistress of her own decision. If she consents to assist the experiment, she consents of her own free will

Second Period, Fourth Narrative

Consent is written into the protocol before the laudanum is measured, by the one character the town has decided is disreputable. Set it beside the birthday dinner, where the first dose was administered to Blake as a practical joke without his knowledge, and the novel’s moral arrangement is complete.

Respectability

The novel’s two most public characters (a platform philanthropist and a tract-distributing spinster) are the ones least able to see themselves. Collins sets professed virtue against conduct repeatedly, and reserves his admiration for a reformed thief, a dying outcast and an old servant who admits he reads one book for comfort.

Through the book Godfrey’s charity funds itself from a trust; Clack writes her pious narrative for pay; Jennings, whom nobody will speak to, asks for consent before he acts.

In the text

having always a few tracts in my bag, I selected one which proved to be quite providentially applicable to the person who answered the door.

Second Period, First Narrative, Chapter I

The pamphlet is chosen for the servant who opened the door, which means a stranger has been diagnosed in the time it takes to cross a hall. Clack’s own word, ‘providentially’, disguises a judgement as a piece of divine good fortune, and she uses the same manoeuvre on every person in her narrative.

to be also capable of properly administering a trust (which, in my experience of the fair sex, not one in a thousand of them is competent to do)

Second Period, Second Narrative, Chapter I

Everything is in the parenthesis: Bruff praises Lady Verinder’s competence and generalises about her sex in the same sentence, without noticing the contradiction. Collins gives his most reliable witness a prejudice he cannot see, which is the exact defect the novel’s many-narrator structure exists to expose.
