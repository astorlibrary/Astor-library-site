The Moonstone · Wilkie Collins

Themes

Testimony

Every narrator is instructed to write only what they saw, and every one of them breaks the rule by supplying motive. The novel is built so that the truth exists nowhere in a single document: it has to be assembled by a reader setting Betteredge’s prejudice against Clack’s piety against Bruff’s professional certainty.

Betteredge establishes the method and its weakness; Clack pushes unreliability to comedy; Jennings’s journal is the one narrative with nothing to defend, which is why it can solve the case.

Empire and possession

The stone is stolen twice (from Somnauth in the eleventh century and from Seringapatam in 1799) before it becomes an English birthday present. Collins attaches the adjective “Indian” to it in every drawing room and bank vault, so English law can hold the diamond but cannot naturalise it.

A private letter of accusation in the Prologue, a legacy designed as a weapon in the First Period, and a restitution in the Epilogue that the English narrators can record but not control.

the Mohammedan conqueror, Mahmoud of Ghizni, crossed India; seized on the holy city of Somnauth; and stripped of its treasures the famous temple, which had stood for centuries⁠—the shrine of Hindu pilgrimage, and the wonder of the Eastern world.

Detection

Cuff reads objects brilliantly and people badly, and in the end the case is opened by an experiment more than by inference. Collins shows that clues belong to several disciplines (policing, medicine, law, household routine) and that a detective who owns only one of them will get it wrong.

Local incompetence, then professional inference, then a collapse of the professional theory, and finally a reconstruction under laboratory conditions.

First Period, Chapter X

Class, service and suspicion

Rosanna is suspected because she was a thief; the servants are examined first; Betteredge measures every visitor by the manner in which the household should receive them. The book is unusually clear that a criminal record and a crooked shoulder are treated as evidence, while a reputation for charity is treated as an alibi.

The servants’ rooms are searched in the First Period; the man nobody suspects is exposed in the Sixth Narrative, by which time he has been dead for chapters.

First Period, Chapter XV

Memory and medicine

The solution requires a state in which a man acts with purpose and retains nothing. Collins builds it out of contemporary argument about opium, nervous disease and the conditions under which an experiment can be repeated, and he is careful that the drug proves nothing on its own: Jennings has to design, observe and interpret.

A dose given as a joke at the birthday dinner, a year of contradiction, and a second dose given deliberately with witnesses, consent and a written protocol.

Second Period, Third Narrative, Chapter III

Respectability

The novel’s two most public characters (a platform philanthropist and a tract-distributing spinster) are the ones least able to see themselves. Collins sets professed virtue against conduct repeatedly, and reserves his admiration for a reformed thief, a dying outcast and an old servant who admits he reads one book for comfort.

Godfrey’s charity funds itself from a trust; Clack writes her pious narrative for pay; Jennings, whom nobody will speak to, asks for consent before he acts.

here was our quiet English house suddenly invaded by a devilish Indian Diamond⁠—bringing after it a conspiracy of living rogues, set loose on us by the vengeance of a dead man.
