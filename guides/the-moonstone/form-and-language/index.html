The Moonstone · Wilkie Collins

Form and language

Multiple narration

A story assembled from separate first-person accounts, each limited to what the writer witnessed.

Eleven narrators write in turn to Franklin Blake’s commission, and none of them can revise another’s pages, so the contradictions stay on the record.

The documentary frame

A novel presented as a dossier of letters, journals, statements and reports.

Blake’s commission is explained in Chapter I and the correspondence about what may be included is printed inside the narrative it belongs to.

the Mohammedan conqueror, Mahmoud of Ghizni, crossed India; seized on the holy city of Somnauth; and stripped of its treasures the famous temple, which had stood for centuries⁠—the shrine of Hindu pilgrimage, and the wonder of the Eastern world.

The unreliable narrator

A witness whose facts can be trusted and whose judgements cannot.

Miss Clack reports a proposal, a will and a death accurately while describing all three as tributes to her own Christian usefulness.

having always a few tracts in my bag, I selected one which proved to be quite providentially applicable to the person who answered the door.

Second Period, First Narrative, Chapter I

The material clue

A physical trace that carries the solution before anybody can read it.

A smear in wet paint fixes the time, a buried nightgown fixes the name, and both are in the reader’s hands long before they are understood.

“Nobody has stolen the Diamond,” answered Sergeant Cuff.

First Period, Chapter XII

Serial suspense

Structure shaped by weekly publication: withheld information, instalment endings, deliberate recurrence.

Thirty-two weeks meant readers had to carry a detail for months, so Collins brings back the quicksand, the paint and the Indians at intervals, since readers could not be relied on to remember.

trembling in a manner most remarkable to see, and which has given to it, among the people in our parts, the name of the Shivering Sand.

First Period, Chapter IV

Idiolect

Characterisation carried by the habits of a narrator’s prose.

Betteredge digresses and quotes Defoe, Clack italicises her own virtues, Bruff numbers his points, and Jennings writes in dated fragments between doses.
