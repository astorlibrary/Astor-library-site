The Moonstone · Wilkie Collins

What happens

Prologue The Storming of Seringapatam (1799)

An unnamed cousin writes to his family explaining why he will never speak to John Herncastle again. In the confusion of the sack he finds Herncastle with a torch and a bloody dagger and two dead Indians at his feet, and hears the third man curse the stone as he dies. Nothing is proved, and the letter is deliberately left as a private accusation.

Prologue
The legend of the yellow diamond, its removal from Somnauth, and a death in a doorway that the writer will not report to his colonel.

First Period The Loss of the Diamond (1848), told by Gabriel Betteredge

The house steward writes twenty-three chapters of the birthday, the theft and the investigation, in a voice built out of Robinson Crusoe, household hierarchy and an unshakeable belief that women are the cause of most trouble. Sergeant Cuff arrives, reads the smeared paint on a door correctly and the people entirely wrongly, Rosanna Spearman drowns herself in the quicksand, and Rachel leaves in a fury nobody can explain.

First Period, Chapters I–IV
Betteredge is commissioned to write, opens Robinson Crusoe for guidance, and introduces the household, Lady Verinder and the Shivering Sand.
First Period, Chapters V–IX
Franklin Blake brings the legacy from the wicked Colonel; three Indian jugglers appear at the gate; Rachel is given the stone on her eighteenth birthday.
First Period, Chapters X–XIII
The dinner party, Murthwaite’s warning, the missing diamond, the local superintendent’s failure, and the sending for Sergeant Cuff.
First Period, Chapters XIV–XIX
The smeared door, the missing nightgown, Cuff’s theory of Rachel’s debts, and Rosanna’s journey to Frizinghall and to the sand.
First Period, Chapters XX–XXIII
Rosanna drowns, Rachel refuses to be questioned, Cuff is dismissed with three predictions, and Franklin Blake leaves England.

Second Period, First Narrative Contributed by Miss Clack

A poor relation with a bag of religious tracts takes the story to London, and reveals far more than she intends. Miss Clack is present at Godfrey Ablewhite’s proposal, at Rachel’s refusal and at Lady Verinder’s death, and reports all three through a haze of self-congratulation. Collins prints Franklin Blake’s letters declining her extracts inside her own narrative, so the reader watches her being edited.

Second Period, First Narrative, Chapters I–III
Clack distributes improving literature, hides behind a curtain, and hears Godfrey Ablewhite propose to Rachel.
Second Period, First Narrative, Chapters IV–VI
Lady Verinder’s illness and will; Clack’s campaign of anonymous extracts; the correspondence in which Blake refuses to print them.
Second Period, First Narrative, Chapters VII–VIII
Rachel breaks with Godfrey on Mr Bruff’s advice, and Clack records his composure as saintliness.

Second Period, Second Narrative Contributed by Mathew Bruff, Solicitor

The family lawyer supplies what only a lawyer would have: the terms of Lady Verinder’s will, the reason Godfrey’s interest in Rachel cooled, and an interview with an Indian client who wanted to know the law on pledged property. Bruff is shrewd, useful and quite sure that women cannot administer a trust, which is the kind of blind spot Collins gives to every witness.

Second Period, Second Narrative, Chapter I
Rachel has only a life interest, which removes Godfrey’s motive for marrying her and supplies Bruff’s explanation of his conduct.
Second Period, Second Narrative, Chapters II–III
A question about the Indian law of pledges, and Murthwaite’s reading of the conspiracy at a London dinner table.

Second Period, Third Narrative Contributed by Franklin Blake

Blake returns, commissions the whole record, and follows Rosanna’s posthumous letter to a tin box buried under the Shivering Sand. In it is a paint-stained nightgown marked with his own name. He then hears from Rachel that she watched him take the diamond with her own eyes, and from then on the novel’s central problem is a contradiction: he has no memory of doing what she saw.

Second Period, Third Narrative, Chapters I–III
Betteredge, the quicksand, the chain, and a nightgown that carries the wrong name: his own.
Second Period, Third Narrative, Chapters IV–VII
Rosanna’s account of her silence and her love; Rachel, confronted at last, says she saw him do it.
Second Period, Third Narrative, Chapters VIII–X
Mr. Candy’s wandering memory, and an assistant nobody in the town will speak to, who has the means of reconstructing what was said at the birthday dinner.

Second Period, Fourth Narrative Extracted from the Journal of Ezra Jennings

A dying man with a disgraced past and an opium habit designs the experiment that solves the case: reproduce the conditions of the birthday night, give the same dose, and see what Blake does. The journal is the most intimate document in the book, and it is kept by the only narrator who has nothing to gain and no reputation to protect.

Second Period, Fourth Narrative
Consent obtained from Rachel and Mr. Bruff, the house restored to its 1848 state, the laudanum given, and Blake watched through the night.

Second Period, Fifth to Eighth Narratives Blake resumed, Cuff, Mr. Candy and Betteredge

The experiment proves what Blake did and not where the diamond went, so the last movement follows the stone to a London bank, a disguise and a murder in a cheap tavern. Sergeant Cuff returns to write out the case he got wrong, naming a man whose reputation for charity had been paid for out of a trust he had robbed. Betteredge closes the book with Robinson Crusoe, as he opened it.

Second Period, Fifth Narrative
The pledged diamond, the watch on the bank, and a bearded man followed to a tavern in Shore Lane.
Second Period, Sixth Narrative
Cuff’s written account of Godfrey Ablewhite’s two lives, the trust he had emptied, and the debt the diamond was raised to pay.
Second Period, Seventh Narrative
The doctor supplies the last medical detail and the news of Ezra Jennings’s death.
Second Period, Eighth Narrative
The steward is given the last word, and uses it on a marriage, a baby and his own favourite book.

Epilogue The Finding of the Diamond

Three short statements move the story out of English hands. Cuff’s man and a ship’s captain trace the three Indians as far as Bombay, and then Murthwaite, travelling in Kattiawar, watches a night ceremony at Somnauth and sees the yellow diamond back in the forehead of the god. The English narrators complete their record and lose the object it was about.

Epilogue, I
Three Hindus board a steamer bound for Rotterdam and slip ashore at Gravesend.
Epilogue, II
Three passengers go overboard off the Indian coast and swim for the shore.
Epilogue, III
A pilgrimage at Somnauth, three penitents parting for ever, and the Moonstone restored to the god of the Moon.
