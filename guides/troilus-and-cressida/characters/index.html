Troilus and Cressida · William Shakespeare

Characters

Troilus

Priam’s youngest son, in love with Cressida; first appears 1.1

He argues in the Trojan council that a thing is worth exactly what men are willing to pay for it, and cannot hold that position for a single night once it is applied to him. His love is real and almost entirely self-addressed: he tells Cressida what her constancy will mean to future poets, and when he sees her with Diomedes his first move is to deny that the woman in front of him is Cressida.

Cressida

Calchas’s daughter, left in Troy when her father defected; first appears 1.2

She is the quickest talker in the play and the least free person in it. Alone at the end of her first scene she says she will hold off because men value what they have not yet got, and everything afterwards shows how little that leaves her. She is traded for a prisoner in a conversation she is not present at, kissed in turn by the Greek command on arrival, and watched by three hidden men on the night she gives the sleeve away.

Pandarus

Cressida’s uncle, and the go-between; first appears 1.1

He praises each lover to the other for a whole afternoon, brings them together, makes jokes at the bedroom door and asks for nothing except to be in the room. He stands over their vows and invites the world to call every future go-between by his name. By the last act he has a cough, a rheum in his eyes and an ache in his bones, and Troilus dismisses him in a line and a half.

Hector

Troy’s best soldier, and Priam’s eldest son; first appears 2.2

He makes the strongest case in the play for giving Helen back, on the law of nations and the arithmetic of Trojan dead, and then says he will keep her because Troy’s dignity is bound up in it. The same split kills him: he lets beaten Greeks get up again, goes to the field on a day his whole family has knelt to prevent, chases a man for his armour, and unbuckles his own in an open field.

Ulysses

A Greek commander, and the camp’s strategist; first appears 1.3

He gives the play its great speech on degree, and then spends four acts managing two proud men with a rigged lottery and a staged snub. He is right about nearly everything and almost nothing he arranges works: Ajax fights nobody, Achilles comes back for a dead friend, and the war is settled by an ambush. He is also the man who walks Troilus to the tent where he will watch Cressida hand over the sleeve.

Achilles

The Greeks’ best fighter, who will not fight; first appears 2.1

He lies in his tent with Patroclus, watching impressions of the generals and living on his own fame and an oath sworn to a Trojan princess. Ulysses tells him that reputation goes stale unless it is kept up daily, and it does not move him; the death of Patroclus does. He then surrounds an unarmed Hector with twenty Myrmidons, has them do the killing, and orders the field to be told that Achilles killed Hector.

Thersites

A scurrilous Greek, kept about the camp as a fool; first appears 2.1

He is beaten by Ajax in his first scene, taken up by Achilles in his second, and licensed to say whatever he likes. His account of the war, that it is a quarrel over an adulteress and a cuckold which will end in disease, is coarser than the play and is never contradicted by it. He gets off the battlefield alive in the last act by telling Hector, accurately, that he is a rascal and a knave.

Diomedes

A Greek commander, sent to fetch Cressida; first appears 1.3

He is given the job of bringing Cressida out of Troy and tells Troilus to his face that he will take his instructions from nobody. In the camp he presses her for a token, refuses to be teased or delayed, and wears the sleeve on his helm the next morning. He is also the one man who says out loud what Helen has cost, counting a Greek life against every false drop in her veins.

Ajax

A Greek lord, half Trojan by blood; first appears 2.1

Alexander describes him in the second scene as a creature assembled from other animals, and the camp treats him as a tool: Ulysses rigs the lottery so that he draws the lot to fight Hector and Achilles is humiliated by the choice. Praised into a stupor, he stops recognising the people he speaks to. The duel he has been built up for ends in an embrace, because his mother was Priam’s sister.

Cassandra

Priam’s daughter, a prophet nobody acts on; first appears 2.2

She breaks into the Trojan council to say that Troy must fall unless Helen goes, and Troilus answers that a mad sister’s raptures cannot spoil a good quarrel. In the last act she kneels with Andromache and Priam to keep Hector indoors, and describes his wounds, his pale eye and the city’s screaming while he is standing in front of her, unhurt. She is right on both occasions and obeyed on neither.
