Troilus and Cressida · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Double plot

A second story running beside the first, repeating its problem in another key and commenting on it.

The love plot and the war plot ask the same question about worth and exchange. Helen is kept because the Trojans have already paid too much for her; Cressida is handed over because Antenor is worth more. The two plots meet in 4.5, where a woman is passed round a camp in the scene that also stages the war’s one formal duel.

Overhearing

A scene built in layers, with each watcher watched by another, so the audience sees the reaction before the event has finished.

Act V Scene 2 puts Cressida and Diomedes at the front, Troilus and Ulysses concealed behind them, and Thersites behind those two supplying obscene commentary. Every word Cressida says reaches the audience through three interested men, which is why the scene never settles what she is doing.

There’s language in her eye, her cheek, her lip, Nay, her foot speaks; her wanton spirits look out At every joint and motive of her body.…Set them down For sluttish spoils of opportunity, And daughters of the game.

Ulysses, 4.5.59

Epithets

The fixed descriptive tags attached to famous names by the tradition a story comes from.

The characters walk on already labelled by Homer and the Troy books: great Hector, the great Achilles, wise Ulysses. The play sets the label beside the man in the tent, so that in one speech Achilles is introduced as the sinew and forehand of the host and then pictured lolling on his bed, laughing at impressions.

One touch of nature makes the whole world kin— That all with one consent praise new-born gauds, Though they are made and moulded of things past

Ulysses, 3.3.173

Foils

A character constructed to throw another’s choices into relief by making different ones.

Ajax and Achilles are set against each other deliberately, by Ulysses, whose plan Nestor sums up as two curs taming each other with pride to set them on. Hector and Troilus split the argument about Helen between an old code and a new one, and Cressida and Helen are the two women the war is fought over and about.

But value dwells not in particular will: It holds his estimate and dignity As well wherein ’tis precious of itself As in the prizer.

Hector, 2.2.54

Catalogue

A list allowed to run on past the point of usefulness, so that the accumulation itself becomes the effect.

Thersites curses Patroclus with a list of ailments that runs from guts-griping and ruptures through raw eyes and wheezing lungs to incurable bone-ache and the tetter, and does not stop where a joke would. The same device builds Ulysses’ picture of the universe in 1.3, where the list of things degree holds up is longer than the argument needs.

Take but degree away, untune that string, And, hark, what discord follows! each thing meets In mere oppugnancy:

Ulysses, 1.3.110

Prophecy

A statement about the future that shapes the action by being believed, whether or not it is true.

Cassandra tells the council Troy will burn and is called brain-sick; she then describes Hector’s death in detail to his face. The lovers do the same thing to themselves in 3.2, swearing by the proverbs their names will become, and the audience already knows that all three forecasts are correct.

If I be false, or swerve a hair from truth, When time is old and hath forgot itself, When waterdrops have worn the stones of Troy…‘As false as Cressid.’

Cressida, 3.2.164

Epilogue

A direct address to the audience after the story has closed.

Pandarus is left alone after Troilus has walked off, and turns to the house to complain about his trade, recite a scrap of verse about the humble-bee, and promise to leave the audience his diseases in two months’ time. It answers the armed Prologue, which also spoke straight out and also promised nothing.

And hither am I come A prologue arm’d, but not in confidence Of author’s pen or actor’s voice, but suited In like conditions as our argument

Prologue, Prologue.22
