Price and value

The Trojan council spends most of a scene on a question of pricing: is Helen worth what she has cost. Troilus says worth is whatever a buyer will pay, Hector says a thing has its own estimate apart from the wanting, and the play then hands the argument to the lovers, where Cressida is exchanged for a prisoner and a sleeve becomes a trophy.

Through the play Troilus makes his merchant’s case in 2.2 and Hector answers it; by 4.3 a woman is being swapped for Antenor, and by 5.5 Diomedes is sending Troilus’s captured horse to her as a gift.

In the text

Fools on both sides! Helen must needs be fair, When with your blood you daily paint her thus.

Troilus, 1.1.85

Painting a woman’s face was a byword for cosmetic fraud, and Troilus makes the paint blood: Helen’s beauty is manufactured daily by the men dying for it. It is the sharpest thing said about the war, and it is said by the man who will stand up in council an act later and argue that she must be kept.

Let Helen go. Since the first sword was drawn about this question, Every tithe soul ’mongst many thousand dismes Hath been as dear as Helen—I mean, of ours.

Hector, 2.2.17

A disme is a tenth, the tithe taken by the church, and Hector uses it to count the dead: one in ten of many thousands, and each of them worth as much as she is. The arithmetic is unanswerable and he supplies the qualification himself (‘of ours’), which concedes that the calculation is tribal before it is moral.

Honour

Everybody appeals to honour and almost nobody can say what it requires. It makes Hector keep a woman he has just proved should be sent home, keeps Achilles in his tent, brings him out of it, calls off a duel between cousins, and finally licenses a ring of Myrmidons to kill one man who is unarmed.

Through the play Hector’s challenge in 1.3 offers the old single-combat version; 4.5 breaks it off on grounds of kinship; 5.7 and 5.8 replace it with a ring of Myrmidons.

In the text

Take but degree away, untune that string, And, hark, what discord follows! each thing meets In mere oppugnancy:

Ulysses, 1.3.110

The image is a lute string: loosen one and the whole instrument is out of tune. It is the most orderly statement of hierarchy in Shakespeare, and it is a diagnosis with a plan attached, delivered by a man who is about to rig a lottery. Nothing in the speech is untrue, and nothing in the play is run by it.

Come here about me, you my Myrmidons; Mark what I say. Attend me where I wheel; Strike not a stroke, but keep yourselves in breath…It is decreed Hector the great must die.

Achilles, 5.7.1

Eight lines of tactical instruction: stand round me, save your wind, and when I find him make a fence of your weapons. Decreed sounds like fate and means his own order. The single combat the whole play has been arranging, with heralds, a lottery and a trumpet, is replaced here by a squad briefing, and nobody in the camp objects afterwards.

Reputation

These names arrive on stage already famous, and the play looks hard at what fame is made of. Ulysses tells Achilles that good deeds fall into a bag on time’s back and that only the newest is looked at, and the Greek camp then proves it by transferring its worship to Ajax inside a day.

Through the play Ulysses lectures Achilles in 3.3; Ajax is praised into stupidity in 2.3 and by 3.3 cannot recognise Thersites; Achilles buys his fame back with an ambush in 5.8.

In the text

Hector’s opinion Is this, in way of truth. Yet, ne’ertheless, My spritely brethren, I propend to you In resolution to keep Helen still

Hector, 2.2.189

The play turns on two words. He has established the truth, in way of truth, and then leans the other way because the dignity of the family is engaged. Nothing has been refuted. The council simply prefers its own reputation to its own reasoning, and Troy is destroyed by a man who could see the answer perfectly well.

Time hath, my lord, a wallet at his back, Wherein he puts alms for oblivion, A great-siz’d monster of ingratitudes.

Ulysses, 3.3.143

A wallet here is a beggar’s satchel, worn behind so the owner cannot see into it, and what goes in are the good deeds already done. Ulysses is telling a soldier that his past is charity thrown to a monster who will not remember it. It is the most persuasive speech in the play and it fails: Achilles moves only when Patroclus is killed.

Vows and their keeping

Three people in an orchard swear an oath and, in the same breath, name the proverbs their names will become if it is broken. The play then breaks it. Troilus asks Cressida to be true four times in one parting, which is already a forecast, and she gives the sleeve away on her first night in the camp.

Through the play The oaths are sworn in 3.2, repeated as anxious instructions in 4.4, and cashed in 5.2, where Troilus watches the sleeve change hands and tries to argue that the woman is somebody else.

In the text

If I be false, or swerve a hair from truth, When time is old and hath forgot itself, When waterdrops have worn the stones of Troy…‘As false as Cressid.’

Cressida, 3.2.164

She invents her own curse and gets it exactly right, which is the strangest thing in the play. The condition she sets is geological (Troy worn away by water, cities swallowed by oblivion), and the punishment is that one phrase outlives all of it. By 1602 the phrase already existed, so the audience is watching a woman coin a proverb she is standing inside.

I speak not ‘Be thou true’ as fearing thee, For I will throw my glove to Death himself That there’s no maculation in thy heart

Troilus, 4.4.59

He says ‘be true’ in one form or another five times in seventeen lines, and each denial that he is afraid makes the fear plainer. ‘Maculation’ is a stain, a word from theology that does not belong in love talk. Cressida’s exasperated reply, that she will be true, is the last promise she makes in Troy, and she keeps it for about a day.

Appetite

Wanting is the engine of both plots, and again and again the play puts it in the language of eating and infection. Ulysses’ speech on order arrives at appetite as a universal wolf that devours everything and at last itself, Troilus says the will is infinite and the act a slave to limit, and Thersites reduces the whole war to lechery.

Through the play 1.3 gives the political version, 3.2 the erotic one, and Act V the physical bill: Thersites on disease, Pandarus coughing, and the last lines of the play made out of his ailments.

In the text

And appetite, an universal wolf, So doubly seconded with will and power, Must make perforce an universal prey, And last eat up himself.

Ulysses, 1.3.122

The chain is deliberate: power, will, appetite, and then a wolf that runs out of everything else and starts on itself. Ulysses means it politically, but the rest of the play reaches for the same image again and again, in Thersites’s lechery, in Troilus’s boundless desire, and in Achilles feeding his eyes on Hector’s body before he kills him.

This is the monstruosity in love, lady, that the will is infinite, and the execution confin’d; that the desire is boundless, and the act a slave to limit.

Troilus, 3.2.69

He thinks he is paying love a compliment and he is describing the machinery that will break them both. Will and desire are limitless, performance is not, and the gap between them is where every vow in the play goes. Cressida has just made the same point as a joke about lions and hares, and she is the one who will be blamed for proving it.

Degree and command

The Greek army has a general who cannot get his best soldier out of bed. Ulysses explains the failure as the collapse of rank into emulation, where each man envies the one above him, and then treats the collapse as a resource, running the camp by flattery, rigged chance and manufactured slights instead of orders.

Through the play The diagnosis comes in 1.3 and the remedy immediately after it; 2.3 and 3.3 show the remedy in operation; Act V shows what it produced, which is a victory nobody in the camp planned.

In the text

Take but degree away, untune that string, And, hark, what discord follows! each thing meets In mere oppugnancy:

Ulysses, 1.3.110

The image is a lute string: loosen one and the whole instrument is out of tune. It is the most orderly statement of hierarchy in Shakespeare, and it is a diagnosis with a plan attached, delivered by a man who is about to rig a lottery. Nothing in the speech is untrue, and nothing in the play is run by it.

Time

The play is soaked in the future tense. The lovers swear by what people will say of them centuries later, Cassandra describes a death that has not happened yet, Ulysses pictures time as a host hustling his guests out of the door, and Hector answers a prophecy of Troy’s fall by saying the end will crown everything.

Through the play Cassandra’s warning in 2.2 goes unheeded; the lovers name their own afterlife in 3.2; Ulysses makes time an argument for action in 3.3; and 4.5 leaves the outcome to time as arbitrator.

In the text

One touch of nature makes the whole world kin— That all with one consent praise new-born gauds, Though they are made and moulded of things past

Ulysses, 3.3.173

The first line is quoted at funerals as a statement about common feeling. In place it means the opposite: the trait all humanity shares is a weakness for the newest shiny thing, and the gauds are second-hand at that. Ulysses is flattering a man by telling him the world is fickle, which is itself the technique he is describing.

The end crowns all; And that old common arbitrator, Time, Will one day end it.

Hector, 4.5.230

Hector is being courteous and is also refusing the prophecy: he will not argue about the future, he will leave it to time. The audience knows Troy falls, so the calm is hard to watch, however wise it sounds. He has three scenes to live, and the end that crowns him is an unarmed man surrounded by Achilles’s Myrmidons.
