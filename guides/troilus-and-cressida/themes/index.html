Troilus and Cressida · William Shakespeare

Themes

Price and value

The Trojan council spends most of a scene on a question of pricing: is Helen worth what she has cost. Troilus says worth is whatever a buyer will pay, Hector says a thing has its own estimate apart from the wanting, and the play then hands the argument to the lovers, where Cressida is exchanged for a prisoner and a sleeve becomes a trophy.

Troilus makes his merchant’s case in 2.2 and Hector answers it; by 4.3 a woman is being swapped for Antenor, and by 5.5 Diomedes is sending Troilus’s captured horse to her as a gift.

Fools on both sides! Helen must needs be fair, When with your blood you daily paint her thus.

Troilus, 1.1.85

Women are angels, wooing: Things won are done; joy’s soul lies in the doing.…Men prize the thing ungain’d more than it is.

Cressida, 1.2.252

Honour

Everybody appeals to honour and almost nobody can say what it requires. It makes Hector keep a woman he has just proved should be sent home, keeps Achilles in his tent, brings him out of it, calls off a duel between cousins, and finally licenses a ring of Myrmidons to kill one man who is unarmed.

Hector’s challenge in 1.3 offers the old single-combat version; 4.5 breaks it off on grounds of kinship; 5.7 and 5.8 replace it with a ring of Myrmidons.

And hither am I come A prologue arm’d, but not in confidence Of author’s pen or actor’s voice, but suited In like conditions as our argument

Prologue, Prologue.22

Fools on both sides! Helen must needs be fair, When with your blood you daily paint her thus.

Troilus, 1.1.85

Reputation

These names arrive on stage already famous, and the play looks hard at what fame is made of. Ulysses tells Achilles that good deeds fall into a bag on time’s back and that only the newest is looked at, and the Greek camp then proves it by transferring its worship to Ajax inside a day.

Ulysses lectures Achilles in 3.3; Ajax is praised into stupidity in 2.3 and by 3.3 cannot recognise Thersites; Achilles buys his fame back with an ambush in 5.8.

And hither am I come A prologue arm’d, but not in confidence Of author’s pen or actor’s voice, but suited In like conditions as our argument

Prologue, Prologue.22

Hector’s opinion Is this, in way of truth. Yet, ne’ertheless, My spritely brethren, I propend to you In resolution to keep Helen still

Hector, 2.2.189

Vows and their keeping

Three people in an orchard swear an oath and, in the same breath, name the proverbs their names will become if it is broken. The play then breaks it. Troilus asks Cressida to be true four times in one parting, which is already a forecast, and she gives the sleeve away on her first night in the camp.

The oaths are sworn in 3.2, repeated as anxious instructions in 4.4, and cashed in 5.2, where Troilus watches the sleeve change hands and tries to argue that the woman is somebody else.

This is the monstruosity in love, lady, that the will is infinite, and the execution confin’d; that the desire is boundless, and the act a slave to limit.

Troilus, 3.2.69

If I be false, or swerve a hair from truth, When time is old and hath forgot itself, When waterdrops have worn the stones of Troy…‘As false as Cressid.’

Cressida, 3.2.164

Appetite

Wanting is the engine of both plots, and again and again the play puts it in the language of eating and infection. Ulysses’ speech on order arrives at appetite as a universal wolf that devours everything and at last itself, Troilus says the will is infinite and the act a slave to limit, and Thersites reduces the whole war to lechery.

1.3 gives the political version, 3.2 the erotic one, and Act V the physical bill: Thersites on disease, Pandarus coughing, and the last lines of the play made out of his ailments.

Women are angels, wooing: Things won are done; joy’s soul lies in the doing.…Men prize the thing ungain’d more than it is.

Cressida, 1.2.252

And appetite, an universal wolf, So doubly seconded with will and power, Must make perforce an universal prey, And last eat up himself.

Ulysses, 1.3.122

Degree and command

The Greek army has a general who cannot get his best soldier out of bed. Ulysses explains the failure as the collapse of rank into emulation, where each man envies the one above him, and then treats the collapse as a resource, running the camp by flattery, rigged chance and manufactured slights instead of orders.

The diagnosis comes in 1.3 and the remedy immediately after it; 2.3 and 3.3 show the remedy in operation; Act V shows what it produced, which is a victory nobody in the camp planned.

Take but degree away, untune that string, And, hark, what discord follows! each thing meets In mere oppugnancy:

Ulysses, 1.3.110

And appetite, an universal wolf, So doubly seconded with will and power, Must make perforce an universal prey, And last eat up himself.

Ulysses, 1.3.122

Time

The play is soaked in the future tense. The lovers swear by what people will say of them centuries later, Cassandra describes a death that has not happened yet, Ulysses pictures time as a host hustling his guests out of the door, and Hector answers a prophecy of Troy’s fall by saying the end will crown everything.

Cassandra’s warning in 2.2 goes unheeded; the lovers name their own afterlife in 3.2; Ulysses makes time an argument for action in 3.3; and 4.5 leaves the outcome to time as arbitrator.

If I be false, or swerve a hair from truth, When time is old and hath forgot itself, When waterdrops have worn the stones of Troy…‘As false as Cressid.’

Cressida, 3.2.164

Time hath, my lord, a wallet at his back, Wherein he puts alms for oblivion, A great-siz’d monster of ingratitudes.

Ulysses, 3.3.143
