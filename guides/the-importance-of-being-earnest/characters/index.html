The Importance of Being Earnest · Oscar Wilde

Characters

John Worthing, J.P.

A country landowner and magistrate, Cecily’s guardian; first appears Act I

He maintains a wicked younger brother in town so that he can be respectable in Hertfordshire, and takes the high moral tone in the country because he has invented somewhere else to be immoral. He is the only character who wants a settled identity, and the plot hands him one he has been using by accident for years.

Algernon Moncrieff

Lady Bracknell’s nephew, of Half-Moon Street; first appears Act I

He eats continuously, owes money everywhere, and has invented a permanently ill friend whose relapses can be timed to avoid any dinner he does not want. He is the play’s theorist, supplying most of its paradoxes, and the only person in it who is entirely unembarrassed by his own behaviour.

Hon. Gwendolen Fairfax

Lady Bracknell’s daughter; first appears Act I

She speaks in her mother’s cadences about entirely different things, and she has settled the question of whom she will marry before meeting anyone: it must be a man called Ernest. Her certainty is unshakeable and her reasoning is decorative, which makes her a comic character and also, in the tea scene, a formidable one.

Cecily Cardew

Jack’s ward, at the Manor House; first appears Act II

She has been conducting an engagement to a man she has never met for three months in a diary, including the letters he wrote her and the week they were broken off. Wilde gives the country ingenue the most thoroughly fictional inner life in the play, and it is entirely in writing.

Lady Bracknell

Gwendolen’s mother, Algernon’s aunt; first appears Act I

She conducts marriage as a matter of lists, incomes and addresses, and delivers her most outrageous positions in the tone of settled common sense. She was herself without fortune when she married, which she mentions once, and that single admission is the crack in an otherwise seamless piece of masonry.

Miss Prism

Cecily’s governess; first appears Act II

She wrote a three-volume novel of more than usually revolting sentimentality, mislaid it in a perambulator and deposited the baby in a hand-bag at Victoria Station. The play’s entire machinery of discovery rests on a woman who has spent two acts recommending moral fiction and disapproving of everybody.

Rev. Canon Chasuble, D.D.

Rector of the parish; first appears Act II

He has a sermon adaptable to almost any occasion, joyful or distressing, and is prepared to christen two grown men in one afternoon. His courtship of Miss Prism proceeds entirely in metaphors about bees and education, and neither party ever says anything directly until the last minute of the play.

Lane

Algernon’s manservant; first appears Act I

He drinks the champagne, keeps the accounts of the cucumber sandwiches and declines to have opinions in a way that is itself an opinion. His handful of lines are placed where they will deflate his employer’s, and they do it without ever ceasing to be perfectly correct.
