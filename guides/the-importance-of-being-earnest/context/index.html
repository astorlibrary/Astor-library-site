The Importance of Being Earnest · Oscar Wilde

Context

Dates

1854
Oscar Wilde born in Dublin Educated at Trinity College Dublin and Magdalen College, Oxford.
1891
The Picture of Dorian Gray published in book form Expanded from the magazine version of the previous year, with a preface answering its critics.
1892
Lady Windermere’s Fan opens at the St James’s Theatre The first of the society comedies, and the play the title page of the 1899 Earnest uses to identify its author.
1893
A Woman of No Importance opens The second society comedy, at the Haymarket.
1894
Earnest written, largely at Worthing Wilde took a house on the Sussex coast with his family for the late summer; the hero’s surname comes from the town.
1895
An Ideal Husband opens in January For a few weeks in early 1895 Wilde had two plays running in the West End at the same time.
1895
First performance, 14 February At the St James’s Theatre, with George Alexander as Jack; the run ended within months when Wilde was tried and imprisoned.
1897
Wilde released from prison He left England for France and never returned.
1899
First book edition, Leonard Smithers The three-act text corrected by Wilde, in an edition of a thousand copies, with a hundred large-paper copies signed by him.
1900
Wilde dies in Paris Three years after his release, and before the play had returned to the London stage under his name.
1952
Anthony Asquith’s film released With Edith Evans as Lady Bracknell, whose reading of the hand-bag line has shaped every performance since.

Places

Half-Moon Street, Mayfair
Algernon’s flat, and the setting of the whole first act.
St James's Theatre, King Street
Where the play opened on 14 February 1895 under George Alexander’s management; the building was demolished in 1957.
Victoria Station
The cloakroom where the hand-bag was deposited, on the Brighton line.
Worthing, West Sussex
Where Wilde wrote the play in the late summer of 1894, and where the hero got his surname.
Dublin
Wilde’s birthplace and the city of his education before Oxford.

Critics

William Archer

The World (1895)

Reviewing the first production, Archer found the play delightful and almost impossible to criticise, because it raised no principle of art or morals and made its own rules: it was, he said, the wilful expression of an irrepressibly witty personality.

George Bernard Shaw

The Saturday Review (1895)

Shaw admitted that he laughed, but called it a mechanical laughter. He thought the play an old-fashioned farce of lies and mistaken identities dressed up in Wilde’s wit, and missed the feeling of his earlier comedies; he later called it Wilde’s first really heartless play.
