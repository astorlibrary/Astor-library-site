The Importance of Being Earnest · Oscar Wilde

Form and language

Epigram

A short, polished statement that sounds like settled wisdom.

Wilde gives the epigrams to the characters least entitled to them, so that a manservant, an idler and a snob all speak with the authority of a proverb.

The truth is rarely pure and never simple.

Algernon, Act I

Inversion

Reversing a received sentiment so that the expected term ends up in the wrong place.

Divorces made in heaven, three being company in marriage, style mattering more than sincerity: the form is always a maxim and the content is always its opposite.

The truth is rarely pure and never simple.

Algernon, Act I

Bathos

A sentence that descends abruptly from the elevated to the trivial.

Lady Bracknell moves from the excesses of the French Revolution to a cloakroom at a railway station, and the drop is the joke.

The fact is constantly mentioned in the more expensive monthly magazines, and has reached the provincial pulpits, I am told; and my ideal has always been to love someone of the name of Ernest. There is something in that name that inspires absolute confidence.

Gwendolen, Act I

Farce structure

Symmetrical plotting in which two of everything collide.

Two invented people, two men called Ernest, two engagements, two christenings booked with one clergyman for the same afternoon, and one hand-bag to sort it all out.

You have invented a very useful younger brother called Ernest, in order that you may be able to come up to town as often as you like. I have invented an invaluable permanent invalid called Bunbury, in order that I may be able to go down into the country whenever I choose.

Algernon, Act I

The stage property

An object that does the work of the plot; it is not there for decoration.

The inscribed cigarette case exposes Jack, the diaries authorise both engagements, and the hand-bag delivers the recognition scene.

A handbag?

Lady Bracknell, Act I

The comic servant

A subordinate whose brief, correct replies undercut the speaker above them.

Lane’s refusal to have listened to the piano, and his account of married households and champagne, set the play’s tone before Jack has entered.

I didn’t think it polite to listen, sir.

Lane, Act I
