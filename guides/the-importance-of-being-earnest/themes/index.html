Earnestness and triviality

The play takes the highest Victorian virtue and reduces it to a name that can be acquired at a christening booked for half-past five. Nobody in it is sincere and nobody suffers for that; the characters who talk most about duty, morality and improving literature are the ones caught out, and the ones who admit to inventing their lives get what they want.

Through the play Jack adopts a high moral tone in Act I because he has somewhere else to misbehave; by the last line the word earnest has been converted into a proper noun and a joke.

In the text

I didn’t think it polite to listen, sir.

Lane, Act I

The servant declines to have heard something everyone in the theatre has just heard, and his grounds are manners. Within four lines Wilde has established the rule the whole play runs on: politeness is a way of not knowing things.

This is no time for wearing the shallow mask of manners. When I see a spade I call it a spade.

Cecily, Act II

The country girl claims plain speaking as a virtue, in a sentence that is itself a cliche about not using cliches. Within two lines Gwendolen’s reply, that she is glad to say she has never seen a spade, has made the claim a statement about class.

Names and identity

Two women insist on marrying a name, and two men arrange to be rechristened to supply it. Jack’s search for his parents is, in the end, a search for an entry in a printed list, and the play’s happy ending is a fact looked up in the Army Lists.

Through the play A name invented in Act I, promised to two women in Act II, and finally discovered to have been legally correct from the start in Act III.

In the text

The fact is constantly mentioned in the more expensive monthly magazines, and has reached the provincial pulpits, I am told; and my ideal has always been to love someone of the name of Ernest. There is something in that name that inspires absolute confidence.

Gwendolen, Act I

What she idealises is a set of syllables, and she supports it with the monthly magazines and the provincial pulpits, as though it were a matter of public opinion. Everything Jack has invented to make himself free has turned out to be the one condition he cannot meet.

You must not laugh at me, darling, but it had always been a girlish dream of mine to love someone whose name was Ernest.

Cecily, Act II

The second woman arrives at the identical requirement independently, and Wilde makes no attempt to explain the coincidence because the play is not interested in psychology. A private preference is presented as something more like a law of nature.

Marriage and money

Lady Bracknell conducts courtship as due diligence: income, investments, town address, politics, parentage. Wilde’s joke is that she says out loud how it works, and her objections to Cecily disappear the instant a number is mentioned.

Through the play A list of eligible young men in Act I, a valuation of a Hertfordshire estate, and an about-turn in Act III on the strength of a figure in the Funds.

In the text

Divorces are made in Heaven

Algernon, Act I

One word is swapped in a proverb and the sentiment is reversed without a syllable of argument. The line is also interrupted mid-breath by Algernon stopping Jack taking a sandwich, so that the epigram and the appetite share a sentence.

I am glad to say that I have never seen a spade. It is obvious that our social spheres have been widely different.

Gwendolen, Act II

She takes a dead metaphor literally and makes it evidence of rank, the fastest piece of social violence in the play. The second sentence is delivered as a neutral observation, and the flatness cuts.

The double life

Bunburying is the play’s word for the arrangement that lets a respectable life continue: a place where one is not, and a person one is not, held in reserve. The comedy’s geography is moral, and the two halves have to be kept from meeting, which is what Act II arranges.

Through the play Two invented people in Act I, both physically present in Act II, and both killed off by the last act.

In the text

You have invented a very useful younger brother called Ernest, in order that you may be able to come up to town as often as you like. I have invented an invaluable permanent invalid called Bunbury, in order that I may be able to go down into the country whenever I choose.

Algernon, Act I

The two fictions are laid out in parallel clauses, and the symmetry is the play’s architecture in advance: town and country, brother and invalid, each man needing somewhere he is not. Both inventions are described as useful, the only virtue either man claims for them.

I hope you have not been leading a double life, pretending to be wicked and being really good all the time. That would be hypocrisy.

Cecily, Act II

Hypocrisy is redefined as the pretence of wickedness, which inverts the standard Victorian charge exactly. Cecily is right about the double life and wrong about which way round it runs, and the audience is the only party in the garden that knows both halves.

Diaries, letters and the written record

Nearly every fact in the play is documented before it happens. Cecily’s engagement exists in her diary, Gwendolen carries hers for the sensational reading, Miss Prism’s novel is lost in a perambulator and Jack’s name is confirmed by the Army Lists. Writing here creates events before it records them.

Through the play An inscription inside a cigarette case in Act I, two diaries in conflict in Act II, and a shelf of Army Lists settling the plot in Act III.

In the text

Cecily, you will read your Political Economy in my absence. The chapter on the Fall of the Rupee you may omit. It is somewhat too sensational. Even these metallic problems have their melodramatic side.

Miss Prism, Act II

The collapse of the rupee had ruined real fortunes and salaries across the Empire, which is why it is too sensational for a schoolroom. Wilde’s governess protects her pupil from the one subject in the curriculum that describes the world she actually lives in.

The good ended happily, and the bad unhappily. That is what Fiction means.

Miss Prism, Act II

A whole theory of the improving Victorian novel in thirteen words, delivered by the woman whose own carelessness with a perambulator will supply this play’s ending. The capital letter on Fiction shows that she is defining a genre.

Servants, food and appetite

Cucumber sandwiches, bread and butter, muffins, tea and sugar are the currency of every quarrel, and the servants keep the accounts. Emotional crises in this play are conducted over plates, and Wilde uses eating as the only honest appetite anybody displays.

Through the play Sandwiches ordered for an aunt and eaten before she arrives in Act I, sugar and cake used as weapons in Act II, and muffins consumed during the collapse of both engagements.

In the text

I hate people who are not serious about meals. It is so shallow of them.

Algernon, Act I

Seriousness, the quality the whole play is named for, is transferred to dinner, and shallowness becomes a charge against people who are not hungry. The joke works because Algernon means it entirely: his appetite is the one sincere thing in the comedy.
