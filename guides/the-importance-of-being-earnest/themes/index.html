The Importance of Being Earnest · Oscar Wilde

Themes

Earnestness and triviality

The play takes the highest Victorian virtue and reduces it to a name that can be acquired at a christening booked for half-past five. Nobody in it is sincere and nobody suffers for that; the characters who talk most about duty, morality and improving literature are the ones caught out, and the ones who admit to inventing their lives get what they want.

Jack adopts a high moral tone in Act I because he has somewhere else to misbehave; by the last line the word earnest has been converted into a proper noun and a joke.

I didn’t think it polite to listen, sir.

Lane, Act I

The truth is rarely pure and never simple.

Algernon, Act I

Names and identity

Two women insist on marrying a name, and two men arrange to be rechristened to supply it. Jack’s search for his parents is, in the end, a search for an entry in a printed list, and the play’s happy ending is a fact looked up in the Army Lists.

A name invented in Act I, promised to two women in Act II, and finally discovered to have been legally correct from the start in Act III.

You have invented a very useful younger brother called Ernest, in order that you may be able to come up to town as often as you like. I have invented an invaluable permanent invalid called Bunbury, in order that I may be able to go down into the country whenever I choose.

Algernon, Act I

The fact is constantly mentioned in the more expensive monthly magazines, and has reached the provincial pulpits, I am told; and my ideal has always been to love someone of the name of Ernest. There is something in that name that inspires absolute confidence.

Gwendolen, Act I

Marriage and money

Lady Bracknell conducts courtship as due diligence: income, investments, town address, politics, parentage. Wilde’s joke is that she says out loud how it works, and her objections to Cecily disappear the instant a number is mentioned.

A list of eligible young men in Act I, a valuation of a Hertfordshire estate, and an about-turn in Act III on the strength of a figure in the Funds.

Divorces are made in Heaven

Algernon, Act I

You don’t seem to realise, that in married life three is company and two is none.

Algernon, Act I

The double life

Bunburying is the play’s word for the arrangement that lets a respectable life continue: a place where one is not, and a person one is not, held in reserve. The comedy’s geography is moral, and the two halves have to be kept from meeting, which is exactly what Act II arranges.

Two invented people in Act I, both physically present in Act II, and both killed off by the last act.

You have invented a very useful younger brother called Ernest, in order that you may be able to come up to town as often as you like. I have invented an invaluable permanent invalid called Bunbury, in order that I may be able to go down into the country whenever I choose.

Algernon, Act I

You don’t seem to realise, that in married life three is company and two is none.

Algernon, Act I

Diaries, letters and the written record

Nearly every fact in the play is documented before it happens. Cecily’s engagement exists in her diary, Gwendolen carries hers for the sensational reading, Miss Prism’s novel is lost in a perambulator and Jack’s name is confirmed by the Army Lists. Writing here creates events before it records them.

An inscription inside a cigarette case in Act I, two diaries in conflict in Act II, and a shelf of Army Lists settling the plot in Act III.

Cecily, you will read your Political Economy in my absence. The chapter on the Fall of the Rupee you may omit. It is somewhat too sensational. Even these metallic problems have their melodramatic side.

Miss Prism, Act II

The good ended happily, and the bad unhappily. That is what Fiction means.

Miss Prism, Act II

Servants, food and appetite

Cucumber sandwiches, bread and butter, muffins, tea and sugar are the currency of every quarrel, and the servants keep the accounts. Emotional crises in this play are conducted over plates, and Wilde uses eating as the only honest appetite anybody displays.

Sandwiches ordered for an aunt and eaten before she arrives in Act I, sugar and cake used as weapons in Act II, and muffins consumed during the collapse of both engagements.

I didn’t think it polite to listen, sir.

Lane, Act I
