Jane Eyre · Charlotte Brontë

Themes

Independence and equality

What Jane demands is standing, more than love. The orchard speech puts it in terms of souls at God’s feet, and the last movement of the novel puts it in terms of money: she cannot accept Rochester until she has her own income, her own relatives and no obligation to be grateful to him.

She stands up to Mrs Reed at ten, turns down the south of France at nineteen and St John’s India at twenty, and arrives at Ferndean able to say she is her own mistress.

I resisted all the way: a new thing for me, and a circumstance which greatly strengthened the bad opinion Bessie and Miss Abbot were disposed to entertain of me.

Chapter 2

“Unjust!⁠—unjust!” said my reason, forced by the agonising stimulus into precocious though transitory power

Chapter 2

Religion

Brontë sets out three versions of Christian conduct and tests each against Jane. Brocklehurst preaches mortification he does not practise; Helen Burns practises forbearance Jane cannot manage; St John offers a self-abolishing usefulness that Jane recognises as a slower death. What she keeps is the law she says was given by God and sanctioned by man.

Each is embodied in a house: Lowood’s hypocrisy, Helen’s deathbed, Moor House’s vocation. Jane refuses all three arrangements and none of the belief.

It is far better to endure patiently a smart which nobody feels but yourself, than to commit a hasty action whose evil consequences will extend to all connected with you

Helen Burns, Chapter 6

Life appears to me too short to be spent in nursing animosity or registering wrongs.

Helen Burns, Chapter 6

Passion and self-command

The novel’s own imagery is fire and ice, and its position is that both can destroy. Jane’s feeling for Rochester is described in terms as strong as anything in Victorian fiction, and the argument of Chapter 27 is that laws and principles exist for exactly the moment when feeling makes them inconvenient.

The red-room’s cold rage, the burning bed, the split chestnut tree, and then the deliberate iciness of Moor House: the book moves between temperatures as a way of measuring self-control.

It is far better to endure patiently a smart which nobody feels but yourself, than to commit a hasty action whose evil consequences will extend to all connected with you

Helen Burns, Chapter 6

if others don’t love me I would rather die than live⁠—I cannot bear to be solitary and hated, Helen.

Jane Eyre, Chapter 8

Rooms, locks and confinement

Jane is shut in the red-room at ten and taken to the third storey at nineteen, and the book invites the comparison: a woman put out of sight because she is inconvenient. The difference is that Jane can argue and be let out.

Gateshead’s locked door, Lowood’s walls, the Thornfield attic, and Jane’s own habit of pacing corridors because there is nowhere further to walk.

There was no possibility of taking a walk that day.

Class, money and the governess

A governess is educated enough to teach a gentleman’s children and poor enough to be a servant, which places her nowhere. Jane feels the difference in rank in every scene with Blanche Ingram, and the novel settles it with an inheritance from an uncle in Madeira.

From a dependent child at Gateshead to a salaried governess at Thornfield to a woman with twenty thousand pounds who gives three-quarters of it away.

my mission is to mortify in these girls the lusts of the flesh; to teach them to clothe themselves with shamefacedness and sobriety, not with braided hair and costly apparel

Mr Brocklehurst, Chapter 7

Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little, I am soulless and heartless? You think wrong!⁠—I have as much soul as you⁠—and full as much heart!

Jane Eyre, Chapter 23

Empire

The two turns of Jane’s fortune both come from outside England: Rochester’s marriage was arranged in Spanish Town for thirty thousand pounds, and Jane’s inheritance comes from an uncle in the wine trade at Funchal. The novel’s English settlement is paid for in colonies it barely describes.

Bertha arrives as a rumour, then a register, then a body; John Eyre’s letter arrives at Gateshead and is hidden for years before it finally frees Jane.

the laugh was as tragic, as preternatural a laugh as any I ever heard

Chapter 11

“My bride is here,” he said, again drawing me to him, “because my equal is here, and my likeness. Jane, will you marry me?”

Mr Rochester, Chapter 23
