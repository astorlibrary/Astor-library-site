Jane Eyre · Charlotte Brontë

Characters

Jane Eyre

Orphan, pupil, governess, heiress, narrator; first appears Chapter 1

Small, plain, penniless and entirely unwilling to be grateful. Her defining habit is argument: she wins the red-room scene, the orchard scene and the Moor House scene by reasoning aloud, and the novel is shaped by the things she refuses more than by her attachments.

Edward Fairfax Rochester

Master of Thornfield Hall; first appears Chapter 12

A man who tells Jane an enormous amount and withholds the one thing that matters, and who conducts his courtship partly by dressing up as a fortune-teller. The novel likes him and does not excuse him: his account of Jamaica is the most sympathetic passage in the book and the least trustworthy.

Bertha Mason

Rochester’s wife, kept on the third storey; first appears Chapter 26

Everything the reader learns about her arrives through people with reasons of their own: her husband, her brother, her keeper. She has no dialogue. What she does have is a series of actions (the fire in the bed, the torn veil, the attack on Mason, the burning of the house) that read as purpose when they are not being described as madness.

St John Rivers

Clergyman at Morton; Jane’s cousin; first appears Chapter 28

He is the novel’s hardest test, because he is no villain. He gives Jane a home, a school and a fortune, and then asks for her whole life on the grounds that she is “formed for labour, not for love”. He is in love with someone else and has decided that this disqualifies him from wanting her.

Helen Burns

Jane’s friend at Lowood; first appears Chapter 5

She offers the doctrine of endurance in its most attractive form: forgive, look past the body, live in calm looking to the end. Jane finds it magnificent and unusable. Helen’s death is the novel’s argument that patience is a real answer and not, for Jane, the available one.

Mrs Reed

Jane’s aunt by marriage, of Gateshead; first appears Chapter 1

She promised her dying husband to bring the child up and has kept the letter of it for ten years while hating the child throughout. Her deathbed is remarkable for what it does not contain: she confesses to hiding a letter from Jane’s uncle and still will not be reconciled.

Mr Brocklehurst

Treasurer and manager of Lowood School; first appears Chapter 4

He preaches mortification of the flesh to eighty half-starved girls and orders their hair cut off while his own daughters wait in velvet and ostrich plumes. Brontë gives him a particular kind of hypocrisy: he believes his doctrine, and has never imagined it applying to himself.

Miss Temple

Superintendent of Lowood; first appears Chapter 5

The one adult in the first half of the book who behaves decently and has the authority to act on it: she orders bread and cheese for the girls, writes to the apothecary Mr Lloyd to clear Jane’s name, and tells the truth about it publicly. Her marriage and departure are what finally make Jane restless enough to leave.

Adèle Varens

Rochester’s ward, Jane’s pupil; first appears Chapter 11

A French child fond of dresses and performance whom Rochester describes with a coolness the novel does not endorse. Jane’s treatment of her is one of the quiet tests in the book: she teaches her, defends her against the charge of being merely frivolous, and looks after her placement at the end.

Grace Poole

The servant who keeps the third storey; first appears Chapter 11

Paid five times a housemaid’s wages to sit with a woman nobody names, and blamed for everything that happens on her watch. She is the household’s explanation for the unexplainable noises, and the fact that everyone accepts the explanation says more about the house than about her.
