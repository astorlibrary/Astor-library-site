Jane Eyre · Charlotte Brontë

Context

Dates

1816
Charlotte Brontë born at Thornton The family moved to Haworth parsonage four years later.
1824
Charlotte is sent to school at Cowan Bridge Charlotte and three sisters were sent to the Clergy Daughters’ School; the conditions there are generally taken to lie behind Lowood.
1825
Maria and Elizabeth Brontë die Both fell ill at school and died at home within weeks of each other; Helen Burns is usually read as a memorial to Maria.
1846
Poems by Currer, Ellis and Acton Bell The three sisters’ first published book, which sold almost no copies and established the pseudonyms.
1847
Jane Eyre published Smith, Elder and Co. brought it out in October in three volumes, as an autobiography edited by Currer Bell.
1847
Wuthering Heights and Agnes Grey appear Emily’s and Anne’s novels were published in December by Newby, partly on the strength of Jane Eyre’s success.
1848
Second edition, with a preface and a dedication to Thackeray Charlotte answered the charge of coarseness and dedicated the edition to Thackeray, not knowing about his own wife’s confinement.
1848
Note to the third edition Written to deny the rumour that all three Bells were one author.
1855
Charlotte Brontë dies at Haworth She was the last of the six Brontë children, and died a few months after marrying Arthur Bell Nicholls.
1857
Gaskell’s Life of Charlotte Brontë The biography that fixed the Brontë legend, and shaped how Jane Eyre was read for a century.
1943
Robert Stevenson’s film With Joan Fontaine and Orson Welles; one of the versions that fixed Thornfield’s visual style for later adaptations.
1966
Wide Sargasso Sea Jean Rhys’s novel gives Bertha Mason a childhood, a name of her own and a first-person voice, and has changed how Chapter 26 is read.

Places

Haworth
The parsonage where the novel was written, over the churchyard on the edge of the moor.
Cowan Bridge
The Clergy Daughters’ School the Brontë sisters attended, generally taken as the original of Lowood.
Hathersage, Derbyshire
Charlotte stayed here in 1845; the Eyre family monuments in the church and North Lees Hall nearby are long associated with the novel.
Spanish Town, Jamaica
Where Rochester’s marriage to Bertha Mason was registered, and the document that stops the wedding.
Funchal, Madeira
John Eyre’s wine business, and the source of the inheritance that makes Jane independent.
Cornhill, London
The offices of Smith, Elder and Co., who published the novel and where Charlotte revealed herself as Currer Bell.

Critics

Elizabeth Rigby

The Quarterly Review (1848)

In an unsigned review Rigby called the novel “pre-eminently an anti-Christian composition” and attacked Jane’s rebelliousness and discontent with her station. The review is often quoted as an example of the alarm the book caused.

Virginia Woolf

A Room of One’s Own (1929)

Woolf quotes Jane’s thoughts on the roof at Thornfield about women’s confined lives, and points to the break when Grace Poole’s laugh interrupts them. She argues that Charlotte Brontë’s anger at her own position pulls the novel out of shape at such moments.

Sandra M. Gilbert and Susan Gubar

The Madwoman in the Attic (1979)

Gilbert and Gubar read Bertha Mason as Jane’s “truest and darkest double”: the rage that Jane must suppress in order to be accepted, acted out by the woman locked in the attic. Their title comes from this reading.

Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak

“Three Women’s Texts and a Critique of Imperialism”, in Critical Inquiry (1985)

Spivak argues that Jane’s rise as an independent woman depends on the dehumanising of Bertha, a Creole woman from Jamaica, and so on the empire. She reads the novel beside Jean Rhys’s Wide Sargasso Sea (1966), which tells Bertha’s story.
