Jane Eyre · Charlotte Brontë

Form and language

Address to the reader

The narrator speaking straight out of the page to whoever is holding the book.

Used at the hinges of the story, never for decoration: ‘Reader, I married him’ announces the marriage as Jane’s action, with her as the subject of the verb and Rochester as its object.

I resisted all the way: a new thing for me, and a circumstance which greatly strengthened the bad opinion Bessie and Miss Abbot were disposed to entertain of me.

Chapter 2

Gothic inheritance

Great houses, locked doors, night noises, interrupted weddings and secrets on an upper floor.

Brontë uses the machinery and then insists on explanations: the Gytrash in the lane is a dog, the laugh is a person, and the terror on the third storey is a marriage settlement.

I remembered certain of Bessie’s tales, wherein figured a North-of-England spirit called a “Gytrash,” which, in the form of horse, mule, or large dog, haunted solitary ways

Chapter 12

Fire and ice imagery

A pair of opposed images used consistently to measure feeling.

Rochester’s world burns (the bed, the house) and St John’s freezes; Jane has to find a temperature she can live at, and the novel tracks it in hearths, frost and weather.

“After a youth and manhood passed half in unutterable misery and half in dreary solitude, I have for the first time found what I can truly love⁠—I have found you. You are my sympathy⁠—my better self⁠—my good angel.

Mr Rochester, Chapter 27

Objects and omens

Things in the narrative that carry a meaning the characters half-register.

The chestnut tree split by lightning on the night of the engagement, the torn wedding veil and the red-room itself all comment on the plot before the plot arrives.

I remembered certain of Bessie’s tales, wherein figured a North-of-England spirit called a “Gytrash,” which, in the form of horse, mule, or large dog, haunted solitary ways

Chapter 12

Houses

Structuring a narrative by the places a character lives in.

Gateshead, Lowood, Thornfield, Moor House and Ferndean each impose a different arrangement on Jane, and the novel’s shape is the series of refusals that move her between them.

There was no possibility of taking a walk that day.

Chapter 1

Dreams and second sight

Visions, presentiments and voices treated as evidence within a realist narrative.

Jane dreams of an infant before the summons back to Gateshead and again before the wedding, and hears her name called across the moor at the moment St John is about to prevail; when Rochester later describes calling her at that hour, she finds the coincidence too awful and inexplicable to discuss.

“Jane! Jane! Jane!”⁠—nothing more.

Chapter 35
