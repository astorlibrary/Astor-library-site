Jane Eyre · Charlotte Brontë

What happens

Chapters 1–4 Gateshead and the red-room

A ten-year-old dependent is struck by her cousin, blamed for it, and locked in the room where her uncle died. The opening is a lesson in how a household decides who counts, and Jane’s first act of rebellion is verbal: she tells her aunt the truth about herself and feels, for the first time, free.

Chapter 1
Hidden behind a window curtain with Bewick’s birds; John Reed throws the book at her head.
Chapter 2
The red-room: cold, grand, unused since her uncle’s death, and the place where her reason first says the word unjust.
Chapter 4
Brocklehurst interviews her about hell; she tells Mrs Reed exactly what she thinks of her and is sent away.

Chapters 5–10 Lowood, Helen Burns and eight years

A charity school run on cold water, burnt porridge and public humiliation, where Jane meets two answers to injustice: Miss Temple’s practical decency and Helen Burns’s doctrine of endurance. A typhus epidemic puts more than half the school to bed and shames the management into reform.

Chapter 6
Helen, beaten for untidiness, explains why she does not resent it; Jane says she could not do the same.
Chapter 7
Brocklehurst orders the girls’ hair cut off while his wife and daughters stand by in velvet, furs and curls.
Chapter 9
Typhus empties the classrooms; Helen dies of consumption in Jane’s arms, and fifteen years pass before her grave has a stone.
Chapter 10
Eight years on, Jane advertises for a situation and receives one reply, from Thornfield.

Chapters 11–20 Thornfield, and the laugh on the third storey

Governess to a French child in a comfortable house with one door nobody opens. Jane’s restlessness on the leads produces the novel’s most direct statement about what women are permitted; then a man falls off a horse in a lane, a bed catches fire at night, and a guest is stabbed in the dark.

Chapter 12
Pacing the third-storey corridor, Jane argues that women feel as men feel; a strange laugh interrupts her.
Chapter 12
In Hay Lane, a dog and a horse out of Bessie’s ghost stories turn out to be Pilot and Mr Rochester.
Chapter 15
Jane wakes to smoke, puts out a fire in Rochester’s bed and is told to tell nobody.
Chapter 20
Mason is attacked upstairs; Jane holds a sponge and a candle for hours and is told nothing.

Chapters 21–27 The proposal, the interruption and the flight

Mrs Reed dies confessing that she hid a letter from Jane’s uncle. Rochester proposes in the orchard and the chestnut tree is split by lightning that night. At the altar a solicitor reads out proof of a marriage in Spanish Town fifteen years before, and Jane is taken upstairs to be shown what is in the attic.

Chapter 23
The orchard: Jane’s speech about being poor, obscure, plain and little, and about spirits standing equal.
Chapter 26
The wedding is stopped; Rochester takes the whole party up to see Bertha Mason.
Chapter 27
Rochester’s account of Jamaica and his offer of the south of France; Jane leaves before dawn with a parcel and twenty shillings.

Chapters 28–35 Moor House, money and St John Rivers

Jane begs on the moor, nearly dies at a door, and is taken in by the three people who turn out to be her cousins. An inheritance makes her independent; she divides it four ways. Then St John asks her to marry him and go to India, because she would be useful, and not because he loves her.

Chapter 30
German lessons with Diana, St John’s offer of the village school at Morton, and news that an uncle’s fortune has gone elsewhere.
Chapter 33
The inheritance from John Eyre of Madeira, and the discovery that the Rivers family are her cousins.
Chapter 34
St John tells her she is ‘formed for labour, not for love’, and claims her for his Sovereign’s service.
Chapter 35
She is close to consenting when she hears a voice calling her name across the moor.

Chapters 36–38 Ferndean

Thornfield is a blackened shell: Bertha set it alight and jumped from the roof, and Rochester lost a hand and his sight getting the servants out. Jane finds him in a damp house in a wood, and this time she has money, family and the right to choose. The last chapter reports ten years of marriage in the first person plural.

Chapter 36
The innkeeper’s account of the fire, the woman on the roof and the pavement below.
Chapter 37
Jane arrives with a tray of candles and water, and declines to explain herself quickly.
Chapter 38
Reader, I married him: a quiet wedding, St John’s last letter from India, and ten years of being somebody’s life.
