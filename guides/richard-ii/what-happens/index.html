Richard II · William Shakespeare

What happens

Act I The quarrel, and the banishments

Bolingbroke accuses Mowbray of treason and of Gloucester’s murder, a charge that leads back towards Richard himself. Richard first orders trial by combat and then stops it mid-ceremony, banishing both men. The interruption saves him from a verdict and costs him the only two men who could have given one.

1.1
Bolingbroke and Mowbray throw down their gages before the king, who fails to reconcile them.
1.2
The Duchess asks Gaunt for revenge; he answers that the quarrel is God’s, because the king ordered the murder.
1.3
Richard halts the combat at the trumpet and banishes Mowbray for life and Bolingbroke for ten years, then six.
1.4
Alone with his favourites, Richard mocks Bolingbroke’s popularity and decides to lease out England to fund the Irish wars.

Act II An inheritance seized, and an army landed

Gaunt dies telling Richard that a king who rents out his country is a landlord. Richard confiscates the Lancastrian estate on the spot, which converts a private grievance into a constitutional one: he has broken the law of inheritance that his own title rests on. Before he reaches Ireland, Bolingbroke has landed at Ravenspurgh.

2.1
Gaunt’s dying speech on England; Richard seizes his lands, and York warns him what that means.
2.2
The Queen feels a grief with no cause yet; news arrives that Bolingbroke has landed with a growing following.
2.3
Bolingbroke gathers support and tells York he has come only for his own inheritance.
2.4
Salisbury watches the Welsh troops leave on a rumour that the king is dead.

Act III Richard’s return, and Flint Castle

Richard lands from Ireland, greets the soil of his kingdom, and then loses the country in three successive pieces of bad news. At Flint Castle he comes down from the walls to meet Bolingbroke in the base court, a piece of staging that settles the question before either man says what he wants. In the garden scene the collapse becomes an argument about husbandry.

3.1
Bolingbroke sends Bushy and Green to execution, itemising their offences first.
3.2
Richard argues that no sea can wash the balm from a king, and then hears the reports that knock the claim down.
3.3
Northumberland delivers Bolingbroke’s terms; Richard comes down to the base court and is taken to London.
3.4
A gardener and his man read the state of England off a neglected garden, and the Queen overhears the news of the deposition.

Act IV The deposition in Westminster Hall

One scene, and the whole political argument is inside it. Carlisle predicts civil war if a subject crowns himself, and is arrested for saying so. Richard is made to hand over the crown in public, and makes the ceremony into a performance of his own fall, calling for a mirror and breaking it.

4.1
Gages fly over Gloucester’s death, Carlisle prophesies, Richard resigns the crown and smashes the glass.

Act V Richard’s imprisonment and murder

Richard is parted from the Queen and sent north. York uncovers his own son in a plot and denounces him, which shows what loyalty now costs a family. In prison Richard thinks his way through the only kingdom he has left, and Exton kills him on the strength of a question Bolingbroke asked aloud and never answered.

5.1
Richard and the Queen say goodbye on the way to the Tower; she is sent to France.
5.2
York finds a treasonous bond on his son and rides to betray him to the new king.
5.3
Father and mother plead on opposite sides; Henry pardons Aumerle and hunts the rest.
5.4
Exton repeats the king’s question about a living fear and decides it was an order.
5.5
Richard compares his cell to the world, hears music out of time, and is killed.
5.6
Henry receives the rebels’ heads and then Richard’s coffin, banishes Exton and vows a crusade.
