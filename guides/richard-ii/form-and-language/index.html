Richard II · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Unbroken verse

A play written entirely in metrical lines, with no prose scenes for servants, clowns or low life.

Gardeners, grooms and murderers all speak verse here, so there is no linguistic ground below the court to stand on and judge it from; the Henry IV plays open that ground immediately.

Extended conceit

A single comparison developed at length until it has been pushed as far as it will go.

The crown as a well with two buckets and the prisoner as a clock are both worked out line by line, which is how Richard fills the time he can no longer command.

How long a time lies in one little word! Four lagging winters and four wanton springs End in a word: such is the breath of kings.

Bolingbroke, 1.3.214

Allegory

A scene in which the surface action stands throughout for something else.

The garden in 3.4 is a working garden and a diagram of the realm, and the gardener never has to explain the second meaning.

The caterpillars of the commonwealth, Which I have sworn to weed and pluck away.

Bolingbroke, 2.3.169

Ritual inversion

Taking the form of a ceremony and running it backwards.

The deposition in 4.1 uses the shape of a coronation (crown, sceptre, oath, anointing) with every element handed back instead of received.

For God's sake, let us sit upon the ground And tell sad stories of the death of kings; How some have been deposed; some slain in war, Some haunted by the ghosts they have deposed;

King Richard II, 3.2.155

Prophecy

A speech that predicts consequences the audience can check against history.

Gaunt foretells Richard’s burn-out and Carlisle foretells civil war; an Elizabethan audience knew the second prophecy had nearly a century of fulfilment behind it.

Methinks I am a prophet new inspired And thus expiring do foretell of him: His rash fierce blaze of riot cannot last, For violent fires soon burn out themselves;

John of Gaunt, 2.1.31

Stage property

An object handled on stage that carries the argument of a scene.

Gages thrown on the floor, a crown held between two men, and a mirror shattered in public do the work that a soliloquy would do elsewhere.

Down, down I come; like glistering Phaethon, Wanting the manage of unruly jades. In the base court? Base court, where kings grow base, To come at traitors' calls and do them grace.

King Richard II, 3.3.180
