Richard II · William Shakespeare

Themes

Divine right and the anointed body

Richard’s whole defence is that kingship is a physical fact, done to him with holy oil, and no office held on conditions. The play does not refute him outright: Bolingbroke wins, but the crown he takes never stops being described as stolen, and the history plays that follow pay for it.

Asserted at full length in 3.2, tested against a landing army in 3.3, formally undone in 4.1, and mourned by the man who broke it in 5.6.

How long a time lies in one little word! Four lagging winters and four wanton springs End in a word: such is the breath of kings.

Bolingbroke, 1.3.214

Methinks I am a prophet new inspired And thus expiring do foretell of him: His rash fierce blaze of riot cannot last, For violent fires soon burn out themselves;

John of Gaunt, 2.1.31

Inheritance and the law of succession

Seizing Gaunt’s estate is the hinge of the plot because Richard’s own title comes from exactly the rule he breaks. York says so to his face: take away the right of an heir to succeed, and you have taken away the argument for being king. The play makes constitutional law a matter of personal cash flow.

Richard leases the realm in 1.4, confiscates Lancaster in 2.1, and by 2.3 Bolingbroke is back claiming only what the law says is his.

This royal throne of kings, this scepter'd isle, This earth of majesty, this seat of Mars, This other Eden, demi-paradise, This fortress built by Nature for herself Against infection and the hand of war,

John of Gaunt, 2.1.40

Landlord of England art thou now, not king: Thy state of law is bondslave to the law; And thou--

John of Gaunt, 2.1.114

Words and names

Banishment is measured in breath, a name is a thing that can be taken off, and the deposition is conducted as a reading aloud. Richard discovers that a word from a king used to be an action and is now only a word, and he becomes a poet at the exact moment the sentence stops working.

Bolingbroke counts four years cut from his exile in 1.3; Richard resigns his name in 4.1; at Pomfret in 5.5 he sets one biblical word against another and can settle nothing.

How long a time lies in one little word! Four lagging winters and four wanton springs End in a word: such is the breath of kings.

Bolingbroke, 1.3.214

The language I have learn'd these forty years, My native English, now I must forego: And now my tongue's use is to me no more Than an unstringed viol or a harp,

Mowbray, 1.3.159

England

The country is never an abstraction here. It is soil Richard greets by hand, a lease signed on rotten parchment, a sea-walled garden full of weeds, and a plot whose caterpillars Bolingbroke promises to pluck away. The metaphor lets servants pass judgement on kings without leaving their own subject.

Gaunt’s blessed plot in 2.1 is answered by the gardener’s choked-up beds in 3.4, and by Carlisle’s ground manured with English blood in 4.1.

Time

Richard is repeatedly late: he hopes to reach the dying Gaunt too late, comes back from Ireland a day too late, and is late to fight. In prison he works the image out in full, describing himself as a clock whose hands are his own fingers and whose chime is his groans, and concludes that the keeper of time has become time’s object.

He wastes it in Acts I and II, hears music played out of time in 5.5, and admits the reversal in a single line before the keepers arrive.

Methinks I am a prophet new inspired And thus expiring do foretell of him: His rash fierce blaze of riot cannot last, For violent fires soon burn out themselves;

John of Gaunt, 2.1.31

The ripest fruit first falls, and so doth he; His time is spent, our pilgrimage must be. So much for that. Now for our Irish wars:

King Richard II, 2.1.155

Ceremony and performance

Nearly every public event in the play is staged by someone: a trial by combat stopped at the trumpet, a descent from a castle wall, a resignation carried out with a crown held between two pairs of hands. Richard is the better performer and the worse politician, and the play does not say whether those are the same thing.

Richard controls the ritual in 1.3, loses control of the staging at Flint in 3.3, and in 4.1 seizes the only role left by directing his own deposition.

Teach thy necessity to reason thus; There is no virtue like necessity.

John of Gaunt, 1.3.278

For God's sake, let us sit upon the ground And tell sad stories of the death of kings; How some have been deposed; some slain in war, Some haunted by the ghosts they have deposed;

King Richard II, 3.2.155
