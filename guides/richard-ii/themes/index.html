Divine right and the anointed body

Richard’s whole defence is that kingship is a physical fact, done to him with holy oil, and no office held on conditions. The play does not refute him outright: Bolingbroke wins, but the crown he takes never stops being described as stolen, and the history plays that follow pay for it.

Through the play Asserted at full length in 3.2, tested against a landing army in 3.3, formally undone in 4.1, and mourned by the man who broke it in 5.6.

In the text

Not all the water in the rough rude sea Can wash the balm off from an anointed king; The breath of worldly men cannot depose The deputy elected by the Lord:

King Richard II, 3.2.54

The claim is made in the language of chemistry, oil that water cannot lift, and within fifty lines it is answered by a series of reports, with no argument at all. The sentence concedes more than it means to: if worldly breath cannot depose a king, the play then shows a king deposed by worldly hands instead.

Now is this golden crown like a deep well That owes two buckets, filling one another, The emptier ever dancing in the air, The other down, unseen and full of water:

King Richard II, 4.1.185

Two men gripping a single object become a mechanism in which neither can rise without the other falling. The conceit also insults the winner: the bucket that goes up is the empty one, ‘dancing in the air’, while the full one sits unseen at the bottom.

Inheritance and the law of succession

Seizing Gaunt’s estate is the turning point of the plot because Richard’s own title comes from the very rule he breaks. York says so to his face: take away the right of an heir to succeed, and you have taken away the argument for being king. The play makes constitutional law a matter of personal cash flow.

Through the play Richard leases the realm in 1.4, confiscates Lancaster in 2.1, and by 2.3 Bolingbroke is back claiming only what the law says is his.

In the text

Landlord of England art thou now, not king: Thy state of law is bondslave to the law; And thou—

John of Gaunt, 2.1.114

Gaunt swaps one title for another and the whole indictment is done. A landlord holds property for income; a king holds a country in trust, and the difference is the lease Richard has just signed. The dash marks the sentence Richard interrupts, and it is the last time anyone at court speaks to him like this.

When our sea-walled garden, the whole land, Is full of weeds, her fairest flowers choked up, Her fruit trees all unpruned, her hedges ruined, Her knots disordered, and her wholesome herbs Swarming with caterpillars?

A gardener’s man, 3.4.42

A servant asks why he should tend one garden when the larger one is ruined, and in doing so repeats Gaunt’s sea-wall and Bolingbroke’s caterpillars from a position with nothing to gain. The play’s political analysis is given to people with no rank at all, and delivered as a question about work.

Words and names

Banishment is measured in breath, a name is a thing that can be taken off, and the deposition is conducted as a reading aloud. Richard discovers that a word from a king used to be an action and is now only a word, and he becomes a poet at the exact moment the sentence stops working.

Through the play Bolingbroke counts four years cut from his exile in 1.3; Richard resigns his name in 4.1; at Pomfret in 5.5 he sets one biblical word against another and can settle nothing.

In the text

How long a time lies in one little word! Four lagging winters and four wanton springs End in a word: such is the breath of kings.

Bolingbroke, 1.3.214

Four years are counted in winters and springs, so the mercy is felt as weather before it is worked out as arithmetic. The couplet ends with ‘the breath of kings’, which makes royal authority a matter of moving air; Bolingbroke will spend the play showing what armies can do against it.

Ay, no; no, ay; for I must nothing be; Therefore no no, for I resign to thee. Now mark me, how I will undo myself;

King Richard II, 4.1.202

The line will not settle into an answer: ‘Ay’ and ‘I’ sounded alike, so ‘Ay, no’ is also ‘I, no’, a man agreeing and cancelling himself in the same breath. It is the shortest statement in the play of what deposition does to a person whose identity was an office.

England

The country is never an abstraction here. It is soil Richard greets by hand, a lease signed on rotten parchment, a sea-walled garden full of weeds, and a plot whose caterpillars Bolingbroke promises to pluck away. The metaphor lets servants pass judgement on kings without leaving their own subject.

Through the play Gaunt’s blessed plot in 2.1 is answered by the gardener’s choked-up beds in 3.4, and by Carlisle’s ground manured with English blood in 4.1.

In the text

This royal throne of kings, this scepter’d isle, This earth of majesty, this seat of Mars, This other Eden, demi-paradise, This fortress built by Nature for herself Against infection and the hand of war,

John of Gaunt, 2.1.40

The famous passage is a single sentence that does not reach its main verb until its twentieth line, and when it arrives the verb is ‘Is now leased out’. Quoted on its own the speech sounds like praise; read to the end of its grammar it is a complaint about a property transaction, and the delay is deliberate.

And if you crown him, let me prophesy: The blood of English shall manure the ground, And future ages groan for this foul act; Peace shall go sleep with Turks and infidels,

Bishop of Carlisle, 4.1.137

‘Manure’ is a farming word set in a cathedral register, and it carries the garden imagery into the political climax. The prophecy is unusual in Shakespeare for being entirely accurate and entirely useless: the audience already knows the Wars of the Roses happened, and the men on stage crown Bolingbroke anyway.

Time

Richard is repeatedly late: he hopes to reach the dying Gaunt too late, comes back from Ireland a day too late, and is late to fight. In prison he works the image out in full, describing himself as a clock whose hands are his own fingers and whose chime is his groans, and concludes that the keeper of time has become time’s object.

Through the play He wastes it in Acts I and II, hears music played out of time in 5.5, and admits the reversal in a single line before the keepers arrive.

In the text

Methinks I am a prophet new inspired And thus expiring do foretell of him: His rash fierce blaze of riot cannot last, For violent fires soon burn out themselves;

John of Gaunt, 2.1.31

‘Expiring’ means dying and also breathing out, so the prophecy and the last breath are the same event. Gaunt speaks with the authority of a dying man, the only position from which anyone in this play tells Richard the truth to his face.

I wasted time, and now doth time waste me; For now hath time made me his numbering clock: My thoughts are minutes; and with sighs they jar Their watches on unto mine eyes, the outward watch,

King Richard II, 5.5.49

The chiasmus swaps the two words round, and with them who is doing what, which is the play’s plot in ten syllables. What follows is a description of machinery: he lists the parts of his own body as the parts of a clock, down to the finger wiping tears off a dial.

Ceremony and performance

Nearly every public event in the play is staged by someone: a trial by combat stopped at the trumpet, a descent from a castle wall, a resignation carried out with a crown held between two pairs of hands. Richard is the better performer and the worse politician, and the play does not say whether those are the same thing.

Through the play Richard controls the ritual in 1.3, loses control of the staging at Flint in 3.3, and in 4.1 seizes the only role left by directing his own deposition.

In the text

What must the king do now? must he submit? The king shall do it: must he be deposed? The king shall be contented: must he lose The name of king? o’ God’s name, let it go:

King Richard II, 3.3.145

Richard refers to himself in the third person throughout, as though reading the part of a king from a script someone else is writing. The questions are rhetorical but the answers are real concessions, so the speech gives away the kingdom in the grammar while the surface sounds like defiance.

God save the king! Will no man say amen? Am I both priest and clerk? well then, amen.

King Richard II, 4.1.173

He offers the loyal shout and then has to supply the response himself, playing both halves of a church service to an audience that will not join in. The line measures the deposition better than any argument in the scene: the form still exists, and nobody is willing to take part in it.
