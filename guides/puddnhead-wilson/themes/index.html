The Tragedy of Pudd’nhead Wilson · Mark Twain

Themes

Race

Roxy is white to look at and a slave in law; her son is thirty-one parts white and salable. The novel states the arithmetic flatly and then spends twenty-one chapters showing that nobody in the town can see the difference it supposedly marks.

The first statement is an authorial aside in chapter two; by the courtroom it has become the sentence that disposes of two grown men, and the last page prices one of them.

Chapter II

The switched babies

Everything the town treats as character (bearing, speech, courage, gentility) is handed out in chapter three along with the ruffled muslin. The experiment runs for twenty-three years and neither the family nor the reader can point to the moment a nature asserted itself.

The exchange is undone in a courtroom, but the training cannot be: Chambers is given his name and his money and can use neither.

To all intents and purposes Roxy was as white as anybody, but the one sixteenth of her which was black outvoted the other fifteen parts and made her a negro.

Chapter III

Nature and nurture

Twain keeps two explanations in play and does not choose between them. Tom is indulged from birth, terrified of exposure from twenty-three, and the narration occasionally floats a third suggestion about blood, then gives the evidence against it.

Roxy blames the fraction in him; Tom blames his birth; the book shows a household that would have produced a bully out of anybody.

If you pick up a starving dog and make him prosperous, he will not bite you. This is the principal difference between a dog and a man.

Conclusion

Law, evidence and property

The trial is a triumph of method: a new kind of proof, presented by the one man in town who has been doing science instead of gossip. It convicts a murderer, corrects a twenty-three-year deception, and delivers one of them to the market.

Wilson’s finger-prints answer the question of who did it and cannot touch the question the book actually raises, which is who these people were allowed to be.

You ought to be sold down the river.

Chapter II

The Virginia code of honour

Dawson’s Landing runs on a Virginian honour system: duels, wills torn up and rewritten, an insult settled with pistols on a stretch of vacant ground. It is treated with perfect seriousness by everyone in the town and as a farce by the man telling the story.

The refused duel disgraces Tom before the town knows anything worse about him, and the Judge’s willingness to fight leaves him alone in the house with his heir.

Debt and reputation

Money runs under every plot turn: a gambling debt sets the thefts going, an allowance gives Roxy her hold, and an estate’s creditors decide the ending. Reputation is the other currency, and Wilson spends twenty years without any.

The town’s verdict on a man (fool, gentleman, foreigner) is shown to be as arbitrary as the cradle, and as binding.

Adam was but human—this explains it all. He did not want the apple for the apple’s sake, he wanted it only because it was forbidden.

Wilson’s reputation

One remark about a dog is taken literally and costs Wilson his profession. The town’s certainty that he is a fool is the same faculty that lets it believe Tom is a Driscoll, and the novel lets the two errors correct together.

His calendar mocks the town from the head of every chapter; the town elects him mayor the moment he does something it can understand.

I wish I owned half of that dog.
