The Tragedy of Pudd’nhead Wilson · Mark Twain

Form and language

The calendar mottoes

An aphorism at the head of each chapter, attributed to a character inside the book.

Wilson’s calendar is the novel’s second voice: dry, bleak and usually about human self-deception. The entries are funny on their own and pointed in place: the April Fool motto stands over the chapter in which the trial ends.

Adam was but human—this explains it all. He did not want the apple for the apple’s sake, he wanted it only because it was forbidden.

Chapter II, calendar

Speech as evidence

Writing characters’ pronunciation and idiom as they are heard.

Roxy’s speech and Chambers’s are the same dialect, and one of them is the son of the house. The novel makes that audible before it makes it explicit, so the reader hears the training that the town mistakes for nature.

Dey sha’n’t, oh, dey

Chapter III

Irony

Reporting something outrageous in the tone used for the ordinary.

Percy Driscoll congratulates himself on a merciful day’s work after threatening to sell four people down the river, and the narration records the self-congratulation without comment. The technique is Twain’s whole method here.

I wish I owned half of that dog.

Chapter I

Doubles

Two figures set up as a matched pair so the differences between them can be examined.

Two babies, two young men, two Italian twins, and a lawyer’s hobby that exists to tell identical things apart. The twins are what survived when Twain cut a farce about conjoined brothers out of the manuscript.

The courtroom demonstration

An extended public scene in which the plot is resolved in front of an audience.

Wilson lectures a packed court on the natal autograph, pauses until the room goes quiet, and then names the murderer. The scene is a magic act, and Twain gives it every beat of one.

These marks are his signature, his physiological autograph, so to speak, and this autograph can not be counterfeited

Chapter XXI

Foreshadowing

A line planted early that the plot later carries out.

Being sold down the river is a threat in chapter two, a terror in chapter three, an act in chapter sixteen and the last five words of the book.

You ought to be sold down the river.

Chapter II

Symbolic objects

Physical things that carry the plot and the argument together.

The knife passes from the twins to Tom and back as evidence; the glass strips hold twenty years of the town’s fingers. Both are objects that can say who a person is when no one in the town can.

Upon this haft stands the assassin’s natal autograph, written in the blood of that helpless and unoffending old man who loved you and whom you all loved.
