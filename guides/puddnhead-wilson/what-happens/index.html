The Tragedy of Pudd’nhead Wilson · Mark Twain

What happens

Chapters I-II Dawson’s Landing, and Wilson’s joke

Twain builds the town before he builds the plot: rose-vines, cats on window-sills, the first families of Virginia, and the slave market a steamboat ride downstream. A young lawyer arrives, makes a remark about half a dog, and is written off for twenty years. In the second chapter a theft among the house slaves is punished with a threat that sets the whole novel moving.

Chapter I
David Wilson says he would kill his half of a barking dog. The town decides he is a fool, and his legal career is over before it starts.
Chapter I
Judge Driscoll, Pembroke Howard and Colonel Essex: the Virginian code of honour, transplanted to a river town and taken entirely seriously.
Chapter II
Percy Driscoll threatens to sell the thieves down the river, then contents himself with selling them locally and congratulates himself on his mercy.
Chapter II
Roxy, white to look at and a slave in law, is nursing her master’s son and her own, born on the same day.

Chapters III-IV Roxy plays a shrewd trick

Terrified that her son can be sold, Roxy dresses him in the other baby’s ruffles and puts the master’s child in the tow-linen shirt. Nobody notices: the difference the town would kill for is laundry. The chapter of aftermath watches the two children grow into the roles the cradle gave them, one insufferable, the other beaten into service.

Chapter III
Roxy tries on the change as an experiment, sees that even she cannot tell them apart, and keeps it.
Chapter III
Wilson has been taking impressions of the town’s fingers as a hobby for years, including the children’s, and files them away.
Chapter IV
Tom grows up spoiled and cruel; Chambers becomes his protector, his swimming champion and his whipping boy.
Chapter IV
Freed at Percy Driscoll’s death, she goes to work on the river boats and leaves her son to the inheritance she stole for him.

Chapters V-XI The Capello twins, Tom’s debts and the duel

The Capello twins take lodgings in the town and are received as a sensation. Tom, back from Yale and deeper in debt than anyone knows, learns from Roxy that he is her son and a slave in law; he insults Luigi, is kicked across a stage in front of the town, and then will not fight the duel his uncle’s code requires. Through all of it Wilson keeps taking finger-prints, and the town keeps finding it comic.

Chapter V
Angelo and Luigi arrive, play the piano four-handed, and are inspected by the whole town at Aunt Patsy’s.
Chapter VII
Tom’s thefts have begun; a girl in the window of Judge Driscoll’s house is seen, and is not a girl.
Chapter VIII
Roxy returns, penniless, to ask her son for help, and is refused with contempt.
Chapter XI
Wilson takes the twins’ and Tom’s finger-prints as a parlour game, and reads in Luigi’s palm that he has killed a man.

Chapters XII-XVIII Roxy tells Tom the truth

Now that Tom knows what he is, the balance of power in the book turns over: the heir is a slave in law, the slave is his mother, and she can hang him with a sentence. She takes half his allowance, orders him to reform, and when he sells her down the river to clear his debts she comes back up it to make her terms plainer.

Chapter XII
The refused duel disgraces the family; the Judge fights it himself, past sixty, because the code allows nothing else.
Chapter XIV
She takes charge of her son’s conduct and his money, and he obeys because the alternative is being sold.
Chapter XVI
Tom sells his own mother to an Arkansas cotton planter and tells himself it is temporary.
Chapter XVIII
Escaped and back in the town, she gives him one instruction: get the money from his uncle, whatever it takes.

Chapters XIX-Conclusion The murder, the trial and the fingerprints

Tom robs his uncle in disguise, kills him with the twins’ Indian knife, and the town arrests the foreigners. Wilson, defending them, finds finger-prints on the knife-handle, matches it to a record in his files, and in doing so discovers the exchange of 1830. The last page disposes of both men as property: one unfit for the drawing room, the other worth too much to be imprisoned.

Chapter XIX
The Judge is stabbed in his own house by a figure in women’s clothes.
Chapter XX
Tom watches the twins charged with his crime and enjoys it.
Chapter XXI
Wilson’s courtroom demonstration: the natal autograph, the glass strips, and the sentence that names the murderer and the changeling together.
Conclusion
Chambers cannot use the life he is given back; Roxy’s spirit is broken; and the state, having pardoned a murderer, sells him to pay the estate’s debts.
