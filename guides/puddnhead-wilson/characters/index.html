The Tragedy of Pudd’nhead Wilson · Mark Twain

Characters

Roxana

A slave in the Driscoll house, and the mother of the boy raised as Tom; first appears Chapter II

Handsome, quick and twenty, with a manner that changes the moment a white person is in the room. She commits the novel’s central act out of terror and then has to live inside it for twenty-three years, first as a servant to her own son, then as his creditor, and finally as the person he sells. She is the only character in the book who acts; the rest drift.

Tom Driscoll

Roxy’s son, put in the heir’s cradle at seven months old; first appears Chapter II

Petted, cowardly and cruel from childhood, a gambler by twenty and a thief by twenty-three. Twain does not put his badness down to his blood, or to his upbringing alone: he is what unchecked indulgence plus a family fortune plus a terror of exposure produces, and the novel leaves the causes visible and unsorted.

Chambers

Percy Driscoll’s son, raised as a slave; first appears Chapter II

Strong, kind and entirely powerless. He takes the beatings meant for the other boy, saves him from drowning, and is thanked with contempt. When the exchange is undone he is twenty-three, cannot read, speaks the dialect of the kitchen, and is at home in neither the parlour nor the place he has been turned out of.

David Wilson

A lawyer with no practice, called Pudd’nhead for twenty years; first appears Chapter I

An educated outsider whose one joke is taken literally, after which the town files him under harmless. He surveys, keeps accounts, collects finger-prints and writes a calendar of aphorisms nobody reads. The plot turns when his hobby becomes evidence, and his reward is to be elected mayor by the people who could not follow his joke.

Judge York Leicester Driscoll

Percy’s brother, Tom’s uncle, and the town’s first citizen; first appears Chapter I

A Virginian gentleman of the old school, an unbeliever in private and a stickler for the code in public. The duel he fights at sixty and the will he tears up twice are both matters of honour, and the code that requires them leaves him alone at night with a nephew who has come to rob him.

Percy Northumberland Driscoll

Roxy’s owner, and Chambers’s father; first appears Chapter II

A land speculator who considers himself a humane master because he sells his thieving slaves locally when he could have sold them down the river, and records the act in his diary as mercy. He never discovers the exchange, and dies believing the boy in the ruffles is his son.

Luigi Capello

One of the Italian twins, and the man accused of the murder; first appears Chapter V

The darker, quicker-tempered brother, who has killed a man before and says so. His kick at Tom is the most popular act in the town’s recent history and the start of Tom’s ruin; his knife, stolen, becomes the murder weapon and the evidence against him.

Angelo Capello

The fairer twin, a teetotaller and a churchgoer; first appears Chapter V

Mild where his brother is fierce, and treated by the town as half of one curiosity more than as a person. The pair are the remains of the conjoined-twin farce Twain cut out of the book, and what survives of that joke is a running question about how much of anyone’s character is their own.

Pembroke Howard

Lawyer, Virginian, and the Judge’s second; first appears Chapter I

The keeper of the code in a town that has half forgotten why it has one. He arranges the duel, prosecutes the twins for the murder of his friend, and never once doubts that the rules he grew up with are the same thing as justice.
