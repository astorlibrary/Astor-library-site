A Doll's House · Henrik Ibsen

Form and language

Analytic structure

A play whose decisive events all happened before the curtain rose and reach the audience as disclosure.

The forgery, the illness, the journey to Italy and the father’s death are all in the past; the three acts are the process by which they come out, which is why so much of the play is people telling each other things.

It was I who saved Torvald’s life.

Nora, Act I

The single set

Confining every scene to one interior so that entrances and exits carry the pressure.

Nothing can be escaped except by leaving, which is why the final door has the force it does; the same doors admit Krogstad, Rank and the letter.

The sound of a door shutting is heard from below.

Stage direction, Act III

Stage properties

Objects that carry meaning and change state across the acts.

The Christmas tree is dressed in Act I and stripped in Act II; the macaroons, the locked letterbox, the fancy-dress costume and the returned bond each mark a stage of the concealment.

As soon as I am quite certain that the worst has come, I shall send you my card with a black cross on it, and then you will know that the loathsome end has begun.

Doctor Rank, Act II

Pet names

Characterising a relationship through the names one person is allowed to use for another.

Lark, squirrel, skylark, spendthrift, little person: in the first two acts Helmer calls his wife by a pet name almost as often as by her own, and in the last act she stops answering to either.

Is that my little lark twittering out there?

Helmer, Act I

The tarantella

A performance inside the play that the audience watches being performed.

Nora rehearses the tarantella wildly to keep her husband from the letterbox; he corrects her steps, which tells us exactly what he thinks he is watching.

Parallel couples

A second pairing whose arrangement comments on the first.

Mrs Linde and Krogstad negotiate their future in one honest conversation, which is the thing the Helmers have never once managed, and they do it in the same room.

Two on the same piece of wreckage would stand a better chance than each on their own.

Mrs Linde, Act III
