Marriage and performance

Nora is addressed as a bird, a squirrel and a child for two and a half acts, and answers in the same register because it works. When she finally describes the arrangement, she does it as a line of descent: her father played with her, Torvald played with her, and she has been playing with her own children in exactly the same way.

Through the play Pet names and a hidden bag of macaroons in Act I; a costume, a rehearsed dance and a husband as audience in Act II; and in Act III the word doll used by Nora herself, at a table, with the tarantella dress changed for ordinary clothes.

In the text

Is that my little lark twittering out there?

Helmer, Act I

The first thing said to Nora is a question about a noise; nobody uses her name. Ibsen puts the whole marriage in the opening exchange: a man who addresses his wife as wildlife, from another room, and a woman who answers in the character he has named.

How should you understand it? A wonderful thing is going to happen!

Nora, Act II

She never says what the wonderful thing is, and the refusal is not coyness: naming it would require her to admit she has been counting on her husband to sacrifice himself. The phrase recurs half a dozen times and means something different each time she uses it.

Money and the law

The plot depends on a legal fact: Nora could not borrow without a man’s name, so she wrote her dying father’s. Krogstad’s answer, that the law cares nothing about motives, is an accurate statement of the position, and the play lets it stand.

Through the play A secret debt repaid out of housekeeping in Act I, a letter in the letterbox in Act II, and in Act III a woman announcing that she will find out whether the law or she is right.

In the text

The law cares nothing about motives.

Krogstad, Act I

Six words, and they are the play’s legal position stated correctly by its least admired character. Ibsen gives the blackmailer the accurate account of how the statute works, so that Nora’s outrage has nothing to push against except the law itself.

According to it a woman has no right to spare her old dying father, or to save her husband’s life. I can’t believe that.

Nora, Act III

She has taken Krogstad’s legal lesson from Act I and made it a test the law has to pass. The last four words do all the work. They make no claim about what the law is. Nora declines to accept it, and the play leaves the question there.

Reputation

Helmer’s whole conduct is governed by how things look to the bank. He will not keep Krogstad because the man speaks to him familiarly in front of the staff, and when the letter arrives his first thought is for whether he might be suspected of prompting her.

Through the play A promotion in Act I, a refusal on a point of manners in Act II, and in Act III a proposal that the marriage continue in the eyes of the world while ceasing in every other respect.

In the text

Do you suppose I am going to make myself ridiculous before my whole staff, to let people think that I am a man to be swayed by all sorts of outside influence?

Helmer, Act II

His reason is that changing his mind would look like being led by someone; he does not say Krogstad is dishonest. A few lines later he admits the real objection is that the man calls him by his first name in front of the clerks, and a marriage is destroyed on that point of etiquette.

Helmer must know all about it. This unhappy secret must be disclosed; they must have a complete understanding between them, which is impossible with all this concealment and falsehood going on.

Mrs Linde, Act III

She takes the decision that ends the marriage on a principle: a household run on a secret is worse off than one facing a scandal. Everything Nora suffers in the last twenty minutes is set in motion by the only person in the play who has been supporting herself.

Duties to others and duties to oneself

The last fifteen minutes are an argument about the word duty, conducted at a table with both parties sitting down. Helmer names the duties of a wife and mother as though listing established facts; Nora accepts them as widely held opinions and says she must first find out whether they are true for her.

Through the play Sacred duties asserted, other duties just as sacred claimed, and then the shortest and most disruptive line in the play: duties to myself.

In the text

Then it must be a very foolish law.

Nora, Act I

She dismisses the entire legal code in eight words and then changes the subject, which in Act I reads as childishness. The last act picks the same sentence up and makes it a programme: she will go away and find out whether the law is right or she is.

Before all else, you are a wife and a mother.

Helmer, Act III

He states it as a definition, and a definition can be answered. Nora’s reply takes ‘before all else’ from him word for word, and with that theft she stops being managed.

Inheritance, sickness and the sins of fathers

Helmer believes moral corruption passes from parent to child and says so at length in Act I, not knowing whose household he is describing. Rank’s spinal disease is attributed to his father’s amusements, so the play sets a medical inheritance beside a moral one and declines to separate them cleanly.

Through the play A lawyer’s theory of deceitful mothers in Act I, a dying man’s account of his father in Act II, and a mother in Act III who concludes that she is unfit to bring up children until she has educated herself.

In the text

Almost everyone who has gone to the bad early in life has had a deceitful mother.

Helmer, Act I

He delivers it as professional experience and it lands on the woman standing in front of him, who has just been threatened over a forged signature. The scene ends with Nora keeping the children out, the first thing she decides entirely on her own.

As soon as I am quite certain that the worst has come, I shall send you my card with a black cross on it, and then you will know that the loathsome end has begun.

Doctor Rank, Act II

He arranges his own death as a piece of correspondence, so that nobody has to be present for it. The card with the black cross is the play’s most economical stage property: it converts a man into a piece of paper in a letterbox, which is what Krogstad’s letter does to Nora.

Women and work

Every character is defined by employment: a new managership, a subordinate clerkship, a widow looking for a post, a nurse who left her own child to take one, and a wife whose earnings had to be hidden because she was not supposed to have any. Nora describes the copying she did at night as the happiest work she has known.

Through the play Secret night work in Act I, a job asked for and refused in Act II, and in Act III a woman leaving with nothing and intending to find something to do.

In the text

It was I who saved Torvald’s life.

Nora, Act I

The emphasis is placed on the pronoun, and it is the first sentence in the play in which Nora is the subject of a serious verb. She has been treated as an expense for two scenes and now produces the one fact that would reverse the household’s opinion of her, and immediately makes her friend promise to keep it.

Two on the same piece of wreckage would stand a better chance than each on their own.

Mrs Linde, Act III

A proposal expressed as arithmetic, offered by a woman who has already once chosen security over love and does not pretend otherwise. This is the only negotiation in the play conducted by two people who tell each other the truth, and Ibsen stages it in the same room that has held eight years of concealment.
