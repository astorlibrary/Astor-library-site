A Doll's House · Henrik Ibsen

Themes

Marriage and performance

Nora is addressed as a bird, a squirrel and a child for two and a half acts, and answers in the same register because it works. When she finally describes the arrangement, she does it as a line of descent: her father played with her, Torvald played with her, and she has been playing with her own children in exactly the same way.

Pet names and a hidden bag of macaroons in Act I; a costume, a rehearsed dance and a husband as audience in Act II; and in Act III the word doll used by Nora herself, at a table, with the tarantella dress changed for ordinary clothes.

Is that my little lark twittering out there?

Helmer, Act I

How should you understand it? A wonderful thing is going to happen!

Nora, Act II

Money and the law

The plot depends on a legal fact: Nora could not borrow without a man’s name, so she wrote her dying father’s. Krogstad’s answer, that the law cares nothing about motives, is an accurate statement of the position, and the play lets it stand.

A secret debt repaid out of housekeeping in Act I, a letter in the letterbox in Act II, and in Act III a woman announcing that she will find out whether the law or she is right.

It was I who saved Torvald’s life.

Nora, Act I

The law cares nothing about motives.

Krogstad, Act I

Reputation

Helmer’s whole conduct is governed by how things look to the bank. He will not keep Krogstad because the man speaks to him familiarly in front of the staff, and when the letter arrives his first thought is for whether he might be suspected of prompting her.

A promotion in Act I, a refusal on a point of manners in Act II, and in Act III a proposal that the marriage continue in the eyes of the world while ceasing in every other respect.

Almost everyone who has gone to the bad early in life has had a deceitful mother.

Helmer, Act I

He suffers from a diseased moral character, Mrs. Helmer; but even he began talking of its being highly important that he should live.

Doctor Rank, Act I

Duties to others and duties to oneself

The last fifteen minutes are an argument about the word duty, conducted at a table with both parties sitting down. Helmer names the duties of a wife and mother as though listing established facts; Nora accepts them as widely held opinions and says she must first find out whether they are true for her.

Sacred duties asserted, other duties just as sacred claimed, and then the shortest and most disruptive line in the play: duties to myself.

Then it must be a very foolish law.

Nora, Act I

How should you understand it? A wonderful thing is going to happen!

Nora, Act II

Inheritance, sickness and the sins of fathers

Helmer believes moral corruption passes from parent to child and says so at length in Act I, not knowing whose household he is describing. Rank’s spinal disease is attributed to his father’s amusements, so the play sets a medical inheritance beside a moral one and declines to separate them cleanly.

A lawyer’s theory of deceitful mothers in Act I, a dying man’s account of his father in Act II, and a mother in Act III who concludes that she is unfit to bring up children until she has educated herself.

Almost everyone who has gone to the bad early in life has had a deceitful mother.

Helmer, Act I

He suffers from a diseased moral character, Mrs. Helmer; but even he began talking of its being highly important that he should live.

Doctor Rank, Act I

Women and work

Every character is defined by employment: a new managership, a subordinate clerkship, a widow looking for a post, a nurse who left her own child to take one, and a wife whose earnings had to be hidden because she was not supposed to have any. Nora describes the copying she did at night as the happiest work she has known.

Secret night work in Act I, a job asked for and refused in Act II, and in Act III a woman leaving with nothing and intending to find something to do.

It was I who saved Torvald’s life.

Nora, Act I

Two on the same piece of wreckage would stand a better chance than each on their own.

Mrs Linde, Act III
