Uncle Tom’s Cabin · Harriet Beecher Stowe

What happens

Chapters I–VIII Tom and Harry sold, and Eliza’s escape

Mr Shelby, who considers himself a kind man, settles his accounts by selling Tom and the child Harry to a trader. Eliza overhears the arrangement and runs that night; Tom, who could run, stays because leaving would mean the rest of the estate going instead. The novel’s whole argument is set up in a dining parlour before anybody is cruel to anyone.

Chapters I–III
Haley and Shelby over the wine; Eliza listens at the door; George Harris tells his wife he will not endure it much longer.
Chapters IV–VI
An evening of hymn-singing in Tom’s cabin, then Eliza waking Harry at midnight and the household covering for her.
Chapters VII–VIII
Eliza crosses the Ohio on the breaking floes, and the slave-catchers are hired in a tavern.

Chapters IX–XIII The fugitives go north, and Tom goes south

The chapters alternate between the fugitives and their cargo counterpart. A state senator who has just voted to forbid helping fugitives finds one in his kitchen; George Harris, disguised at a country tavern, tells his old employer he has no country; and Haley, on a steamboat south, sells a woman’s infant off her lap and records the consequence in his account book.

Chapter IX
Mrs Bird argues the law down in her own parlour, and the senator drives Eliza to a safe house himself.
Chapters X–XII
Tom is taken from his family and shipped downriver; George Harris, in disguise, declares himself a man without a country.
Chapter XIII
Rachel Halliday’s kitchen: the novel’s model household, run without owning anybody.

Chapters XIV–XVIII New Orleans and the St Clare household

Tom saves a child who falls overboard and is bought by her father, and the book’s middle section installs him in the most comfortable slavery it can imagine. Augustine St Clare argues eloquently against the system he profits from; his cousin Ophelia arrives from Vermont opposed to slavery and unable to bear the people held under it.

Chapters XIV–XV
Tom is bought after pulling Evangeline St Clare out of the Mississippi.
Chapters XVI–XVIII
Marie’s invalidism, Ophelia’s efficiency, and St Clare’s long argument that knowing better changes nothing.

Chapters XIX–XXVIII Eva’s death, and St Clare’s

St Clare sets out the economics of ownership more clearly than any abolitionist in the book and does nothing. Ophelia is given Topsy to educate and discovers her own revulsion. Eva dies at length and in public, as sentimental fiction required; her father is stabbed in a tavern brawl before he can free Tom, and the household is sold.

Chapter XIX
St Clare on his brother’s plantation, on his own idleness, and on what the law actually permits.
Chapters XX–XXV
A northern woman’s antislavery principle meets a child she cannot bring herself to touch.
Chapters XXVI–XXVIII
Eva’s death, St Clare’s sudden one, and Marie’s decision to sell.

Chapters XXIX–XXXVIII Legree’s plantation

Tom is sold at auction to Simon Legree and taken to a plantation run on the principle that people are cheaper to work to death than to maintain. Cassy, who has survived it longest, tells Tom her history; Legree, who is afraid of nothing visible, turns out to be terrified of a lock of hair and a noise in the garret.

Chapters XXIX–XXXI
The New Orleans sale rooms, and the journey upriver with Legree’s new purchases.
Chapters XXXII–XXXIV
Tom refuses to flog a sick woman; Cassy gives the plantation’s history in her own voice.
Chapters XXXV–XXXVIII
Legree’s superstition, Cassy’s plan, and Tom’s refusal to say where the women have gone.

Chapters XXXIX–XLV Cassy’s escape, Tom’s death and the address to the reader

Cassy and Emmeline hide in the haunted garret while the plantation searches the swamp for them. Tom is beaten to death for his silence, and George Shelby arrives two days late with the money to buy him. The scattered family is reassembled improbably, sails for Liberia, and Stowe steps forward to tell her readers what she wants them to do.

Chapters XXXIX–XLI
Cassy uses Legree’s superstition against him; Tom is flogged and dies; George Shelby buries him.
Chapters XLII–XLIV
Cassy discovers that Eliza is her daughter; the Harrises decide on Liberia; George Shelby frees the people at home.
Chapter XLV
Stowe drops the fiction, states which incidents are drawn from record, and addresses the mothers of America directly.
