Uncle Tom’s Cabin · Harriet Beecher Stowe

Form and language

Double plot

A second story running beside the first, repeating its problem in another key and commenting on it.

Eliza going north and Tom going south are told in alternating blocks, so that every act of the flight plot is read against a corresponding stage of the sale plot, and neither is allowed to stand for the whole.

Direct address

A narrator or speaker turning outward to address the reader or the audience by name.

Stowe interrupts constantly: to tell a reader that his own heart could be brought to the same place as a trader’s, and finally to address the mothers of America directly in the closing chapter.

It’s a shameful, wicked, abominable law, and I’ll break it, for one, the first time I get a chance

Mrs Bird, Chapter IX

The tableau

A scene composed and held still so that its emotional meaning can be read off it.

The deathbeds, the cabin at hymn-time, the auction block, Tom shackled in the wagon at the Shelbys’ door: scenes arranged with the stillness of an engraving, which is one reason the book moved so easily onto the stage.

I would die for them, Tom, if I could

Eva St Clare, Chapter XXIV

Dialect

Spelling and grammar used to mark where a speaker comes from, and what being heard that way costs them.

Stowe writes four or five distinct speech registers (Kentucky field, New Orleans drawing room, Vermont, Quaker plain speech) and uses the gap between them to mark exactly how much education each character has been allowed.

Well, I’ve got just as much conscience as any man in business can afford to keep

Haley, Chapter I

Scriptural argument

Using scripture as the ground of an argument.

Characters throw scripture at each other for opposite purposes (a clergyman on the steamboat citing the curse on Canaan and a young man answering with the golden rule; Legree demanding ‘Servants, obey yer masters’), so that the novel stages a dispute about interpretation more than belief.

No! no! no! my soul an’t yours, Mas’r! You haven’t bought it

Tom, Chapter XXXIII

Weekly serial structure

A narrative shaped by instalments, each needing its own crisis.

Written to a weekly deadline for The National Era, the book cuts between two plots to keep both alive from one instalment to the next.
