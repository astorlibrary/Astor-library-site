Uncle Tom’s Cabin · Harriet Beecher Stowe

Context

Dates

1811
Harriet Beecher born in Litchfield, Connecticut Into a famous clerical family; her father Lyman Beecher and several brothers were preachers.
1832
The Beechers move to Cincinnati Eighteen years living on the Ohio, across the river from a slave state, gave her most of what she knew at first hand.
1836
Marriage to Calvin Stowe A biblical scholar; the couple later moved to Brunswick, Maine, where the novel was begun.
1850
The Fugitive Slave Act Part of the Compromise of 1850, it required citizens of free states to assist in recapture and is the immediate provocation for the book.
1851
Serialisation begins in The National Era From June, in an abolitionist weekly in Washington; the story ran far beyond its planned length.
1852
Published in two volumes by John P. Jewett It sold on a scale no American novel had reached before, and was pirated and translated across Europe within the year.
1852
The first stage versions appear Unlicensed dramatisations began immediately; the ‘Tom shows’ outlived the book’s reputation and coarsened everything in it.
1853
Stowe publishes A Key to Uncle Tom’s Cabin Stowe answered accusations of invention by publishing the court records, advertisements and narratives behind her scenes.
1857
The Dred Scott decision The Supreme Court held that a black American could not be a citizen, hardening the argument the novel had entered.
1861
The American Civil War begins The story that Lincoln greeted Stowe as the little woman who started the war is undocumented and long-lived.
1865
The Thirteenth Amendment Slavery abolished in the United States, thirteen years after the book appeared.
1896
Stowe dies at Hartford, Connecticut By then the novel’s stage afterlife had largely replaced the book in the public mind.

Places

Maysville, Kentucky
The Ohio-river country north of Lexington that Stowe knew, and the setting of the Shelby farm at the novel’s opening.
Ripley, Ohio
The free-state bank of the Ohio opposite Kentucky; a crossing of this kind is the model for Eliza’s.
Cincinnati
Where Stowe lived for eighteen years, and the city through which the northern chapters’ traffic in people and rumour passes.
New Orleans
The St Clare household, and the auction rooms where Tom is sold after St Clare’s death.
Alexandria, Louisiana
On the Red River, the region in which Legree’s isolated cotton plantation is placed.
Brunswick, Maine
Where Stowe was living when she began writing, after the Fugitive Slave Act reached into the free states.

Critics

James Baldwin

“Everybody’s Protest Novel”, in Partisan Review (1949)

Baldwin called it a very bad novel. He argued that its self-righteous sentimentality denies its Black characters full humanity, and that the protest novel in general, however good its intentions, confirms the categories it sets out to attack.

Jane Tompkins

“Sentimental Power: Uncle Tom’s Cabin and the Politics of Literary History”, in Sensational Designs (1985)

Tompkins defended the sentimental novel against its modernist critics. She argued that Stowe used the most widely shared beliefs of her readers, about the Christian home, mothers and redemptive suffering, to make an argument for remaking American society.
