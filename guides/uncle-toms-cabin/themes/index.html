Uncle Tom’s Cabin · Harriet Beecher Stowe

Themes

Law, debt and property in people

Stowe’s method is to show ownership working through ordinary paperwork more than through the whip: a debt, a bill of sale, an unsigned deed of manumission, a new state law, voted for by a senator, that forbids his neighbours to help a fugitive. The cruelty of the Red River is the same legal power that the Shelbys exercise politely.

A private debt in Chapter I, the new law against helping fugitives in Senator Bird’s parlour, St Clare’s failure to sign in Chapter XXVIII, and the auction rooms of Chapter XXX where the law and the market are the same thing.

Well, I’ve got just as much conscience as any man in business can afford to keep

Haley, Chapter I

a delicately formed hand and a trim foot and ankle were items of appearance that did not escape the quick eye of the trader, well used to run up at a glance the points of a fine female article.

Chapter I

Feeling and politics

Stowe asks her readers to imagine an enslaved family through the domestic affections they already honour, and then to act on the recognition. Every device of popular fiction (the deathbed, the lost child, the reunion) is put to a legislative end, and the concluding chapter says so explicitly.

From the sale overheard in Chapter I, through Eva’s death, to the direct instruction in Chapter XLV that every individual can see to it that they feel right.

It’s a shameful, wicked, abominable law, and I’ll break it, for one, the first time I get a chance

Mrs Bird, Chapter IX

His heart was exactly where yours, sir, and mine could be brought, with proper effort and cultivation.

Chapter XII

Christianity

Nearly every character has a religion and the novel spends its length distinguishing between them: the piety that sanctions a sale, the piety that sings hymns in a cabin, the northern church that deplores slavery and returns fugitives. Stowe’s final pages put the account squarely at the door of the American churches.

the prayer meeting in Tom’s cabin in Chapter IV, Mrs Bird against her husband’s vote in Chapter IX, St Clare’s mockery of clerical apologists, Legree’s use of scripture as a licence, and the judgement pronounced in Chapter XLV.

I always thought that I must obey my master and mistress, or I couldn’t be a Christian.

Eliza, Chapter III

It is a sin to hold a slave under laws like ours

Mrs Shelby, Chapter V

Mothers and children

The novel’s engine is separation: a sleeping child sold on a steamboat while his mother looks for her husband on the wharf, a mother crossing broken ice, a woman who poisoned her own infant rather than see it sold, and a reader addressed at the end as a mother who has stood beside a cradle. Stowe treats the destruction of families as slavery’s defining product, where its apologists called it an excess.

Eliza’s flight in Chapter VII, Lucy on Haley’s boat in Chapter XII, Cassy’s history in Chapter XXXIV, and the address to the mothers of America in Chapter XLV.

God helping me, I crossed on the ice

Eliza, Chapter IX

The trader, therefore, sat discontentedly down, with his little account-book, and put down the missing body and soul under the head of losses!

Chapter XII

Flight and the geography of freedom

North and south in this book are distances with laws attached, more than moral categories. Eliza’s route runs through Ohio, where a new law forbids anyone to help her, and ends outside the country altogether; Tom’s runs downriver, by way of a kind owner in New Orleans, to the worst place in the novel.

The river crossing in Chapter VII, the Quaker settlement in Chapter XIII, George’s declaration on the rock in Chapter XVII, and the decision in Chapter XLIII to leave for Liberia rather than stay in a country that will not have them.

I always thought that I must obey my master and mistress, or I couldn’t be a Christian.

Eliza, Chapter III

God helping me, I crossed on the ice

Eliza, Chapter IX

Race, type and caricature

Stowe assigns temperaments by descent (Tom’s patience, George’s spirit credited to his father’s proud Kentucky family, Topsy’s comic energy), and the novel’s sympathy runs along those lines, never across them. This is the book’s deepest problem, and it is there in the same scenes that made it politically effective.

The narrator’s generalisations about African nature in the New Orleans chapters, the handling of Topsy, the whiteness of the characters permitted to argue and escape, and the Liberian ending that removes the freed family from America altogether.

a delicately formed hand and a trim foot and ankle were items of appearance that did not escape the quick eye of the trader, well used to run up at a glance the points of a fine female article.

Chapter I

You loathe them as you would a snake or a toad, yet you are indignant at their wrongs.
