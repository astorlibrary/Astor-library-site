Law, debt and property in people

Stowe’s method is to show ownership working through ordinary paperwork more than through the whip: a debt, a bill of sale, an unsigned deed of manumission, a new state law, voted for by a senator, that forbids his neighbours to help a fugitive. The cruelty of the Red River is the same legal power that the Shelbys exercise politely.

Through the book A private debt in Chapter I, the new law against helping fugitives in Senator Bird’s parlour, St Clare’s failure to sign in Chapter XXVIII, and the auction rooms of Chapter XXX where the law and the market are the same thing.

In the text

Well, I’ve got just as much conscience as any man in business can afford to keep

Haley, Chapter I

Conscience is presented as an overhead, priced like any other, and the joke is that the speaker is entirely sincere. Stowe puts the novel’s economic case in its first pages and in the mouth of its least reflective character, so that everything said later by thoughtful men is an elaboration of a trader’s small talk.

It’s a shameful, wicked, abominable law, and I’ll break it, for one, the first time I get a chance

Mrs Bird, Chapter IX

Three adjectives and then a plan, which is the order Stowe wants her readers to follow. The chapter is constructed so that the opportunity arrives within the hour, and the senator who defended the law in the abstract is soon being jolted through the mud at midnight in his own carriage, with the fugitive beside him.

Feeling and politics

Stowe asks her readers to imagine an enslaved family through the domestic affections they already honour, and then to act on the recognition. Every device of popular fiction (the deathbed, the lost child, the reunion) is put to a legislative end, and the concluding chapter says so explicitly.

Through the book From the sale overheard in Chapter I, through Eva’s death, to the direct instruction in Chapter XLV that every individual can see to it that they feel right.

In the text

His heart was exactly where yours, sir, and mine could be brought, with proper effort and cultivation.

Chapter XII

The narrator turns from the trader to the reader mid-paragraph and includes herself in the indictment, which stops the sentence from being a simple accusation. ‘Cultivation’ is the word a Victorian reader would apply to their own moral improvement, redeployed here to describe the process of learning not to care.

they can see to it that they feel right

Chapter XLV

It is the most-quoted sentence in the book and the most criticised, because it makes the reader’s inner state the unit of political action. Read in context it is less passive than it sounds (the paragraph goes on to talk about influence and atmosphere), but the novel does end by asking for feeling and leaving the legislation to follow.

Christianity

Nearly every character has a religion and the novel spends its length distinguishing between them: the piety that sanctions a sale, the piety that sings hymns in a cabin, the northern church that deplores slavery and returns fugitives. Stowe’s final pages put the account squarely at the door of the American churches.

Through the book the prayer meeting in Tom’s cabin in Chapter IV, Mrs Bird against her husband’s vote in Chapter IX, St Clare’s mockery of clerical apologists, Legree’s use of scripture as a licence, and the judgement pronounced in Chapter XLV.

In the text

I always thought that I must obey my master and mistress, or I couldn’t be a Christian.

Eliza, Chapter III

She states the doctrine she has been taught as a condition of her faith, and within four chapters she will break it with a child on her hip. The novel gets its Christian readers to abandon that teaching by letting a good woman hold it first, and then making obedience unthinkable.

not surer is the eternal law by which the millstone sinks in the ocean, than that stronger law, by which injustice and cruelty shall bring on nations the wrath of Almighty God!

Chapter XLV

The comparison is to a physical certainty, so judgement is presented as a law of physics. Nine years before the first shots, Stowe ends a domestic novel by telling the United States that the consequence is already in motion and that repentance is the only variable left.

Mothers and children

The novel’s engine is separation: a sleeping child sold on a steamboat while his mother looks for her husband on the wharf, a mother crossing broken ice, a woman who poisoned her own infant rather than see it sold, and a reader addressed at the end as a mother who has stood beside a cradle. Stowe treats the destruction of families as slavery’s defining product, where its apologists called it an excess.

Through the book Eliza’s flight in Chapter VII, Lucy on Haley’s boat in Chapter XII, Cassy’s history in Chapter XXXIV, and the address to the mothers of America in Chapter XLV.

In the text

The trader, therefore, sat discontentedly down, with his little account-book, and put down the missing body and soul under the head of losses!

Chapter XII

A suicide is recorded as a bookkeeping entry, and the sentence adopts the ledger’s own calm until the last three words. ‘Body and soul’ is the trade’s own phrase used against it: the column exists because the law allows a soul to be entered as stock.

And say, mothers of America, is this a thing to be defended, sympathized with, passed over in silence?

Chapter XLV

Stowe names her actual constituency last and most directly, and the three verbs run from active approval to mere silence, closing every available position. The fiction has been put down by this point: the last chapter is a pamphlet, and the novel has been its evidence.

Flight and the geography of freedom

North and south in this book are distances with laws attached, more than moral categories. Eliza’s route runs through Ohio, where a new law forbids anyone to help her, and ends outside the country altogether; Tom’s runs downriver, by way of a kind owner in New Orleans, to the worst place in the novel.

Through the book The river crossing in Chapter VII, the Quaker settlement in Chapter XIII, George’s declaration on the rock in Chapter XVII, and the decision in Chapter XLIII to leave for Liberia rather than stay in a country that will not have them.

In the text

God helping me, I crossed on the ice

Eliza, Chapter IX

The novel’s most spectacular action is reported afterwards, flatly, by the person who performed it, and the room’s astonishment tells us what a description would. She credits God, and the syntax credits herself: the main verb is hers, and the help is a subordinate phrase.

I’ll fight for my liberty to the last breath I breathe. You say your fathers did it; if it was right for them, it is right for me!

George Harris, Chapter XI

He does not ask for the revolution’s principles to be extended to him; he points out that they already cover his case and dares his listener to deny it. An American readership in 1852 could reject the claim only by unsaying its own founding story, and that is the trap the sentence sets.

Race, type and caricature

Stowe assigns temperaments by descent (Tom’s patience, George’s spirit credited to his father’s proud Kentucky family, Topsy’s comic energy), and the novel’s sympathy runs along those lines, never across them. This is the book’s deepest problem, and it is there in the same scenes that made it politically effective.

Through the book The narrator’s generalisations about African nature in the New Orleans chapters, the handling of Topsy, the whiteness of the characters permitted to argue and escape, and the Liberian ending that removes the freed family from America altogether.

In the text

a delicately formed hand and a trim foot and ankle were items of appearance that did not escape the quick eye of the trader, well used to run up at a glance the points of a fine female article.

Chapter I

The sentence begins as a conventional novelistic description of a pretty woman and ends in the vocabulary of livestock, with ‘items’ and ‘points’ and ‘article’ doing the turn. The reader is made to notice that they have been looking at her in the same way the trader has, one clause earlier.

You loathe them as you would a snake or a toad, yet you are indignant at their wrongs.

Augustine St Clare, Chapter XVI

The line is aimed past Ophelia at the book’s actual readership, most of whom lived north of the Ohio and had subscribed to an antislavery paper. Stowe puts her sharpest attack on her own supporters into the mouth of a slaveholder, which makes it harder to dismiss and impossible to enjoy.
