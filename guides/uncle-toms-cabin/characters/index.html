Uncle Tom’s Cabin · Harriet Beecher Stowe

Characters

Tom

An enslaved man, sold three times in the course of the novel; first appears Chapter I

He is a literate, married man of about forty who reads his Bible slowly with a finger, and the novel’s central question is whether his refusal to escape is obedience or a decision. On the Red River he draws a line and will not move it, and the line costs him his life.

Eliza Harris

An enslaved woman in the Shelby household, Harry’s mother; first appears Chapter I

She is given the novel’s most famous physical action and almost none of its dialogue about principle: she hears the sale discussed through a door and is over the river before anyone has finished arguing. Everything she does afterwards is organised around keeping a five-year-old alive and quiet.

George Harris

Eliza’s husband, hired out to a factory by his owner; first appears Chapter II

He invented a machine for cleaning hemp and was taken out of the factory for it, which is the novel’s compressed statement of what slavery wastes. He argues where others endure, arms himself, and is the one character allowed to reject the country outright, without appealing to its conscience.

Haley

A slave trader; first appears Chapter I

Stowe makes him neither a monster nor an aberration but a man with a business model, who prides himself on humane methods because damaged goods sell badly. He supplies the novel’s running joke and its coldest passages: the same voice that talks about conscience keeps a ledger of losses.

Mrs Shelby

Mistress of the Kentucky farm; first appears Chapter I

She has taught the people she owns to read and to pray and believes this makes the arrangement tolerable; the sale shows her that her opinions have no legal force whatever. Her outrage is real and completely ineffective, which is the use Stowe makes of her.

Augustine St Clare

A New Orleans gentleman, Tom’s second owner; first appears Chapter XIV

He can describe the economics and the hypocrisy of slavery better than anyone else in the novel, and he keeps a household of people he has not freed. Stowe gives the clearest analysis to a man who will not act on it, which makes his sudden death the most pointed thing that happens in the book.

Evangeline St Clare

St Clare’s daughter; first appears Chapter XIV

She is the novel’s sentimental engine: a child who says the unanswerable thing and then dies of it slowly enough for everyone to gather. Readers who find her unbearable and readers who weep over her are responding to the same technique, which Stowe deploys with complete deliberation.

Miss Ophelia

St Clare’s cousin, from Vermont; first appears Chapter XV

She arrives with northern antislavery opinions and a horror of disorder, and the novel uses her to make the point it most wanted its readers to feel: that opposing slavery in principle and being unable to touch a black child are perfectly compatible. Her slow correction by Topsy is the only conversion in the book that takes work.

Topsy

A child in the St Clare household; first appears Chapter XX

She answers questions about her origins by saying she had none, and the answer is the most efficient indictment of the trade in the novel. She is also the character in whom Stowe’s comic writing comes closest to the minstrel stage, and the two facts have to be held together; one does not cancel the other.

Simon Legree

A cotton planter on the Red River, Tom’s last owner; first appears Chapter XXX

He runs his plantation on a stated policy of using people up and buying more, and he sets the enslaved to guard each other so that nobody can be trusted. Stowe makes him a New Englander by birth, which removes the comfort of treating him as a southern problem.

Cassy

An enslaved woman on Legree’s plantation; first appears Chapter XXXIV

She has been educated, sold repeatedly, separated from her two children, and has given a third child laudanum at two weeks old sooner than let him grow up; she tells all of it herself in the first person. She is also the only person in the novel who defeats a white man by out-thinking him, using his own fear of the house against him.
