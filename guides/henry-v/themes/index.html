Kingship

Again and again the play comes back to what the crown actually consists of, and the answer Henry reaches alone at night is ceremony: forms that buy obedience and cost sleep. Nothing in the victory revises that answer; it is simply not spoken of again once there are people watching.

Through the play Set out in the bishops’ account of the reformed prince in 1.1, tested in the dark in 4.1, and closed off by an Epilogue that reduces the whole reign to a short time greatly lived.

In the text

May I with right and conscience make this claim?

King Henry V, 1.2.96

It is the right question, asked of the one man in England with a financial interest in the answer. Canterbury does not say yes: he says ‘The sin upon my head, dread sovereign’, offering to take the blame without removing it, and Henry accepts the offer without pressing further.

Every subject’s duty is the king’s; but every subject’s soul is his own.

King Henry V, 4.1.156

It is a clean and quotable separation, and it answers a question nobody asked: Williams had raised the guilt of the man who ordered the battle; the state of each soldier’s conscience is Henry’s change of subject. The neatness is the evasion, and the prayer that follows a hundred lines later shows that Henry knows it.

The justice of the war

The claim is manufactured in Act I by men with a financial motive, blessed by a churchman who offers to bear the sin, and never examined again by anyone at court. It is a common soldier, in the dark, who asks the question the archbishop had a financial interest in settling.

Through the play Canterbury’s genealogy in 1.2; Williams’s objection in 4.1; the order to kill the prisoners in 4.6, which nobody on stage condemns.

In the text

for many a thousand widows Shall this his mock mock out of their dear husbands; Mock mothers from their sons, mock castles down;

King Henry V, 1.2.286

He is answering an insult, and he answers it by itemising the dead in advance: widows, then mothers, then buildings, in descending order of grief. ‘Mock’ is used four times in three lines, so a joke about tennis becomes the word for the whole war, and the responsibility is carefully assigned to the man who made the joke.

I am afeard there are few die well that die in a battle; for how can they charitably dispose of any thing, when blood is their argument?

Williams, 4.1.126

Williams objects to dying unprepared, which in 1599 was a real and specific fear; the politics of the claim do not concern him. ‘Blood is their argument’ compresses the case: men killing cannot also be settling their affairs, and someone has put them in that position. Henry spends the next forty lines not answering that sentence.

England, Wales, Scotland and Ireland

The English army contains a Welshman, an Irishman and a Scot who quarrel among themselves while they fight the French, and the play laughs at their accents and takes their professionalism seriously. On the French side, Katharine’s English lesson makes the conquest audible as a problem of language.

Through the play The captains’ quarrel in 3.2, the lesson in 3.4, Fluellen’s leek in 5.1 and Henry’s claim in 4.7 that he is Welsh himself.

In the text

The game’s afoot: Follow your spirit, and upon this charge Cry ‘God for Harry, England, and Saint George!’

King Henry V, 3.1.32

The order of the three names is worth noticing: the king comes before the country, and the saint comes last. ‘The game’s afoot’ makes a siege into a hunt, the register the whole speech has been reaching for, greyhounds in the slips, straining at the start.

From this day to the ending of the world, But we in it shall be remember’d; We few, we happy few, we band of brothers; For he to-day that sheds his blood with me Shall be my brother

King Henry V, 4.3.60

The tense is the engine: almost the whole speech is in the future, describing old men in flowing cups remembering a day that has not happened. Brotherhood is offered as payment, and the play checks it straight away: Williams, who was promised gentling, spends the next scenes in a quarrel over a glove with the man who promised it.

Persuasion

Henry’s speeches do practical work: they are how a tired, outnumbered army comes to agree to fight. The play stages the same skill turned in four directions (law, terror, fellowship and courtship), so that the audience can see it working and notice that it is a technique.

Through the play Legal argument in 1.2, terror at Harfleur in 3.3, fellowship at Agincourt in 4.3, and a deliberately clumsy plainness at Troyes in 5.2.

In the text

That he which hath no stomach to this fight, Let him depart; his passport shall be made And crowns for convoy put into his purse: We would not die in that man’s company That fears his fellowship to die with us.

King Henry V, 4.3.36

The offer of a passport and travelling money is safe to make because nobody can now accept it, which is the trick of the whole speech: he presents a shortage as a privilege before naming the day. He does not call such a man a coward, only poor company to die with.

I speak to thee plain soldier: If thou canst love me for this, take me: if not, to say to thee that I shall die, is true; but for thy love, by the Lord, no; yet I love thee too.

King Henry V, 5.2.143

The man who has produced perfect verse on demand for four acts spends the courtship insisting he has no eloquence, which is itself the most calculated performance in the play. The honesty is real in one respect: he will not claim he would die for her, and the sentence about love arrives after the treaty has already awarded her to him.

Ceremony and the private man

Stripped of recognition by a borrowed cloak, Henry discovers that the difference between a king and a soldier is a set of observances that cannot cure a fever or buy an hour’s sleep. The speech is delivered to nobody, and the play never returns to it in company.

Through the play 4.1: the disguise, the argument, the soliloquy on ceremony and the prayer about his father’s crown, all in one unbroken scene.

In the text

Upon the king! let us our lives, our souls, Our debts, our careful wives, Our children and our sins lay on the king! We must bear all.

King Henry V, 4.1.202

He is mimicking his subjects’ voices, piling their possessions onto himself in a list that starts with lives and ends with sins. The pronoun slips from ‘us’ to ‘we’ without settling whose side he is arguing from, which is the closest the play comes to showing the man inside the office losing track of the boundary.

And what art thou, thou idle ceremony? What kind of god art thou, that suffer’st more Of mortal griefs than do thy worshippers?

King Henry V, 4.1.212

He addresses ceremony as a person and then as a deity, and finds it a god that suffers more than the people who pray to it. The speech is an audit: rents, comings in, place, degree and form on one side, and a slave’s untroubled sleep on the other, with the balance clearly against the crown.

Memory and fame

The Crispin’s Day speech is about the future tense: old men forgetting, feats remembered with advantages, a story taught to a son. The play is interested in the making of the legend as much as in the battle, which is why it ends by pointing out that everything won here was lost within a generation.

Through the play The Chorus builds the legend across five acts; 4.3 predicts its transmission; the Epilogue’s sonnet takes it all back in fourteen lines.

In the text

O for a Muse of fire, that would ascend The brightest heaven of invention, A kingdom for a stage, princes to act And monarchs to behold the swelling scene!

Chorus, Prologue.1

The play begins by wishing it were a different play, performed by real kings in a real kingdom. ‘A kingdom for a stage’ reverses Richard III’s famous bargain, and the wish is impossible by design: everything the Chorus asks for is exactly what the theatre cannot supply, and that is how the audience gets recruited.

The poor condemned English, Like sacrifices, by their watchful fires Sit patiently and inly ruminate The morning’s danger

Chorus, Act 4 Chorus.22

‘Condemned’ and ‘sacrifices’ are the Chorus’s own words for an army it otherwise celebrates, and the tone of the fourth Prologue is quite unlike the first. It is the one place where the narrator’s version and the play’s version agree, and the scene that follows puts the king among those fires in a borrowed cloak.
