Henry V · William Shakespeare

Form and language

The Chorus

A narrator who addresses the audience directly between the acts.

A Chorus appears before every act and returns to speak the Epilogue. It supplies scale, apologises for the stage, and consistently describes a more heroic play than the one being performed.

O for a Muse of fire, that would ascend The brightest heaven of invention, A kingdom for a stage, princes to act And monarchs to behold the swelling scene!

Chorus, 1.0.1

Anaphora

Beginning successive clauses with the same word.

Henry’s speeches run on it (“Once more… once more”, “mock out of their dear husbands; Mock mothers from their sons, mock castles down”), and the repetition does the work of an argument the speeches never actually make.

for many a thousand widows Shall this his mock mock out of their dear husbands; Mock mothers from their sons, mock castles down;

King Henry V, 1.2.286

Juxtaposition of scenes

Placing two scenes side by side so that each comments on the other.

The breach speech at 3.1 is followed straight away by Bardolph echoing it, and by Nym, Pistol and the Boy staying where they are until Fluellen drives them on; the reckoning of the dead at 4.8 comes minutes after the order to kill the prisoners.

for his nose was as sharp as a pen, and a' babbled of green fields.

Hostess, 2.3.12

Dialect and broken English

Representing national or foreign speech in the spelling of the line.

Fluellen, Macmorris and Jamy are written phonetically, and in Katharine’s lesson English is a set of sounds to be got wrong. The nation the play assembles is audibly made of several.

Soliloquy

A character alone on stage, thinking aloud.

Henry has only one of real substance, and it comes in the small hours, after an argument with a private soldier. Everything else he says is performed for somebody.

Upon the king! let us our lives, our souls, Our debts, our careful wives, Our children and our sins lay on the king! We must bear all.

King Henry V, 4.1.202

The sonnet epilogue

A fourteen-line rhymed poem used to close a play.

The Epilogue is a sonnet, and it spends its last six lines on Henry’s infant son, whose many managers lost France and made England bleed. Fourteen lines are enough to cancel five acts of triumph.

Small time, but in that small most greatly lived This star of England: Fortune made his sword; By which the world's best garden be achieved, And of it left his son imperial lord.

Chorus, Epilogue.5
