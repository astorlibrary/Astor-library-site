Henry V · William Shakespeare

Characters

King Henry V

King of England; first appears 1.2

He can produce whatever voice a situation needs, legal, terrifying, brotherly, awkwardly amorous, and the play never lets the audience behind all of them. The one scene in which nobody knows he is the king is the one in which he is contradicted and cannot answer, and it is followed immediately by a prayer about his father’s stolen crown.

The Chorus

The play’s narrator and apologist; first appears 1.0

It appears before every act to apologise for the theatre and to recruit the audience into supplying what the stage cannot. Its Henry is a mirror of all Christian kings moving through a clean epic; the scenes that follow are consistently more complicated, and the gap between the two is the play’s method.

The Archbishop of Canterbury

Archbishop of Canterbury; first appears 1.1

He opens the play worrying about a bill that would confiscate Church lands and closes his first scene proposing to fund a French war. His genealogical argument takes sixty-two lines, and when Henry asks whether the claim is honest he offers to carry the sin himself, which is not the same as saying yes.

Katharine

Princess of France, later Queen of England; first appears 3.4

She learns English from her waiting-woman in the scene after Harfleur falls, and the lesson collapses into laughter at two words that sound obscene in French: a comic scene about a woman preparing to be handed over with a country. In the last scene she wonders whether it is possible to love the enemy of France, and never gives an answer.

Fluellen

A Welsh captain; first appears 3.2

He talks constantly about the disciplines of the wars and tests every event against a classical precedent, which makes him funny; he is also the only officer who reports the killing of the boys as a breach of the law of arms. The comic accent and the moral accuracy belong to the same character and the play does not separate them.

Michael Williams

A common soldier; first appears 4.1

He states the case against the war more clearly than anyone else in the play: if the cause is bad, the reckoning belongs to the man who ordered it. Henry answers him at length about the souls of individual soldiers, which is not the question he asked, and the disguise means he never knows he has been debating the king.

Pistol

An ancient, formerly of Eastcheap; first appears 2.1

He speaks in fragments of old plays and treats the campaign as a business opportunity, ransoming a terrified Frenchman he cannot understand. What is left of Falstaff’s world goes to France and comes back as a man planning to pass off his beating as war wounds.

The Boy

Servant to Pistol, Bardolph and Nym; first appears 2.1

He gives a clear-eyed account of the three men he works for, decides that their swaggering is not for him, and would trade all his fame for a pot of ale and safety. He is with the luggage when the French attack the camp, and the play does not show his death.

Exeter

The king’s uncle; first appears 1.2

He carries the ultimatum to Paris, answers the Dauphin’s tennis balls with a threat, and it is he who tells the French king that the coming widows and orphans will be laid at his door. He functions as the war’s clear voice: no argument about the claim, only a statement of what refusing it will cost.

The Dauphin

Heir to the French throne; first appears 2.4

He sends the tennis balls, spends the night before Agincourt praising his own horse, and is the play’s argument that aristocratic confidence is a military weakness. His contempt for the English is the engine of the first act and the reason the French lose the fourth.
