Knowledge and its limits

Faustus is not punished for curiosity. He is shown abandoning it. The opening soliloquy measures every discipline by what it can do for him, and once he has a devil to question he settles for answers Wagner could give. The one question that matters, who made the world, is the one Mephistopheles refuses, and Faustus lets it drop.

Through the play From the survey of the faculties in Scene 1, through the ‘freshmen’s suppositions’ of the astronomy lesson in Scene 6, to the wish in Scene 14 that he had never seen Wittenberg or read a book.

In the text

The reward of sin is death: that’s hard.

Faustus, Scene 1

The verse he is reading goes on to say that the gift of God is eternal life. He stops at the half that suits his mood and delivers a verdict on the whole of divinity in two colloquial words. A doctor of theology would know the rest, so the misreading is either wilful or the first sign of a mind already closing.

I cannot read, and therefore wish all books were burnt.

Envy, Scene 6

It is a throwaway joke in the parade, and the last line of the tragedy turns it serious. The most learned man in Germany will end by offering to burn his books too, and the echo puts him, at the finish, in the position of the sin that hates what it cannot use. Faustus watches the show as a spectator; several of the Sins are portraits.

Ambition

The Prologue gives the pattern in one image: waxen wings that mount above their reach. What the play adds is the anticlimax. Faustus plans to be emperor of the world and ends up performing for one, and the distance between the programme of Scene 1 and the grapes of Scene 12 is Marlowe’s harshest comment on what the bargain bought.

Through the play The plans are largest before the contract is signed. Afterwards nobody mentions walling Germany with brass again, Faustus least of all.

In the text

His waxen wings did mount above his reach, And, melting, heavens conspir’d his overthrow

Chorus, Prologue

Icarus was warned, flew too high and fell, which seems a tidy moral until the verb arrives. ‘Conspir’d’ makes heaven an active party to the overthrow, and that one word plants the question the play never settles: whether Faustus falls or is dropped.

I will be Paris, and for love of thee, Instead of Troy, shall Wertenberg be sack’d

Faustus, Scene 13

The scholar casts himself in the story he used to teach, and offers his own university town as the city to be burned. It is said as gallantry. Every comparison in the speech, Paris, Semele, Arethusa, is a mortal ruined by contact with something greater, and the speaker never notices what his examples have in common.

Appetite and habit

Faustus names his own god early: ‘thine own appetite’. Each time conscience stirs, something pleasant is put in front of him, and he takes it. The play is unusually exact about how a free choice becomes a habit and a habit becomes a hardened heart, until he can say ‘I cannot repent’ and mean it as a description.

Through the play Devils with crowns in Scene 5, the Sins in Scene 6, a quiet sleep in Scene 11, Helen in Scene 13: each is asked for or accepted at a moment of doubt.

In the text

Had I as many souls as there be stars, I’d give them all for Mephistophilis.

Faustus, Scene 3

The hyperbole works by multiplying the one thing he has only one of. It is also a lover’s sentence, given for a person and not for power, and the play holds that note: he cuts his arm ‘for love of thee’. What he wants from the bargain, at this moment, is company.

Was this the face that launch’d a thousand ships.

Faustus, Scene 13

The most famous compliment in English drama is a question, and its measure of beauty is a fleet of warships. ‘Was this’ carries a faint note of surprise, as if the scholar were checking the legend against the exhibit. He has told the Emperor that such figures are spirits in borrowed shapes, and he greets this one as the woman herself.

Despair and repentance

In the theology the play works with, despair is the conviction that one’s sin is too great to be forgiven, and it is itself the sin that cannot be forgiven, because it turns away the remedy. Faustus is told by an Angel, an Old Man and three scholars that mercy is available, and answers each time that it is not available to him. Whether he is right is what the play leaves open.

Through the play ‘Despair in God’ is a resolution in Scene 5 and a condition by Scene 13, where ‘I do repent; and yet I do despair’ puts both in one line.

In the text

O, Faustus, leave these frivolous demands, Which strike a terror to my fainting soul!

Mephistopheles, Scene 3

The tempter asks his victim to stop. It is the oddest moment in the scene and the most persuasive warning Faustus ever receives, because it comes from the only witness with direct experience and nothing to gain by it. Faustus’s reply is to tell a devil to learn courage from him.

My heart’s so harden’d, I cannot repent: Scarce can I name salvation, faith, or heaven, But fearful echoes thunder in mine ears

Faustus, Scene 6

The hardened heart is Pharaoh’s in Exodus, where the text says both that Pharaoh hardened it and that God did. Marlowe leaves the verb passive, so the question of who did the hardening stays open. The speech then shows the mechanism at work: despair offers him knives and halters, and pleasure, in the shape of Homer singing, talks him round.

Grace, sin and the Reformation argument

The play is set in Luther’s university and written for an audience taught that salvation is God’s gift and not man’s achievement. That makes Faustus’s case hard. If he cannot repent, is that because he will not, or because grace has been withheld? The 1604 text keeps both answers alive and gives the Evil Angel the bleaker one.

Through the play The half-quoted verses of Scene 1 start the argument; ‘Christ cannot save thy soul, for he is just’ in Scene 6 is its darkest moment; the blood in the firmament in Scene 14 reopens it with minutes to go.

In the text

Why, this is hell, nor am I out of it

Mephistopheles, Scene 3

Ten monosyllables replace the hell of fire and pitchforks with a state of loss carried everywhere. The line is spoken to a man standing in the same spot, so ‘this’ takes in Faustus’s study too. He has asked for a location and been given a definition, and he responds by changing the subject to his own fortitude.

Christ cannot save thy soul, for he is just: There’s none but I have interest in the same.

Lucifer, Scene 6

Lucifer argues from justice and leaves mercy out, exactly as Faustus left out the second half of his verses in Scene 1. ‘Interest’ is a lender’s word: the soul is a property with a claim on it. The fact that Lucifer needs to come in person suggests the prayer was more dangerous to him than he admits.

Time

Twenty-four years pass in a few hundred lines of clowning and court visits, and the final hour takes fifty-seven lines of verse with a clock striking through it. Marlowe makes the audience feel how the years were wasted by wasting them in front of us, and then makes a single hour unbearable by counting it.

Through the play First mentioned as a contractual term in Scene 3, felt as ‘the restless course that time doth run’ in Scene 11, and turned into sound in Scene 14.

In the text

Tush, Christ did call the thief upon the Cross; Then rest thee, Faustus, quiet in conceit.

Faustus, Scene 11

The penitent thief was the standard proof that repentance is possible at the last moment. Faustus uses it as a sedative. ‘Tush’ and ‘quiet in conceit’ show him taking a doctrine of mercy as permission to put things off, and the play’s cruellest stage direction follows: he sleeps, and a cheated customer pulls his leg off.

Stand still, you ever-moving spheres of heaven, That time may cease, and midnight never come

Faustus, Scene 14

He opens the last speech in the imperative, the mood of a conjuror, and addresses the same spheres he once questioned Mephistopheles about. Nothing obeys. ‘Ever-moving’ concedes the defeat inside the command. The speech goes on asking for less and less: a year, a month, a week, a natural day.

Power and service

The deed of gift makes Mephistopheles the servant and Faustus the master, and the play shows that arrangement to be a fiction from the first meeting, when the devil explains he came of his own accord. Faustus’s requests are refused whenever they matter. The comic scenes repeat the pattern downwards: Wagner hires a clown, Robin summons a devil, and nobody who gives orders in this play is in charge.

Through the play ‘How pliant is this Mephistophilis’ in Scene 3; ‘Thou traitor, Faustus, I arrest thy soul’ in Scene 13.

In the text

For, when we hear one rack the name of God, Abjure the Scriptures and his Saviour Christ, We fly, in hope to get his glorious soul

Mephistopheles, Scene 3

The spell did nothing; the blasphemy did. Faustus has just congratulated himself as ‘conjuror laureat’, and the devil removes the ground of the boast in three lines without Faustus appearing to notice. ‘Glorious’ is the striking word: the hunter values the soul more highly than its owner does.

But such spirits as can lively resemble Alexander and his paramour shall appear before your grace

Faustus, Scene 10

An honest disclaimer, in courtly prose, from the man who was going to raise the dead and be a god. Magic can supply likenesses and nothing more. The Emperor forgets this within a minute and takes the spirits for ‘true substantial bodies’; Faustus forgets it three scenes later, when the likeness is Helen and the one deceived is himself.
