Doctor Faustus · Christopher Marlowe

Themes

Knowledge and its limits

Faustus is not punished for curiosity. He is shown abandoning it. The opening soliloquy measures every discipline by what it can do for him, and once he has a devil to question he settles for answers Wagner could give. The one question that matters, who made the world, is the one Mephistopheles refuses, and Faustus lets it drop.

From the survey of the faculties in Scene 1, through the ‘freshmen’s suppositions’ of the astronomy lesson in Scene 6, to the wish in Scene 14 that he had never seen Wittenberg or read a book.

Yet art thou still but Faustus, and a man.

Faustus, Scene 1

The reward of sin is death: that's hard.

Faustus, Scene 1

Ambition

The Prologue gives the pattern in one image: waxen wings that mount above their reach. What the play adds is the anticlimax. Faustus plans to be emperor of the world and ends up performing for one, and the distance between the programme of Scene 1 and the grapes of Scene 12 is Marlowe’s harshest comment on what the bargain bought.

The plans are largest before the contract is signed. Afterwards nobody mentions walling Germany with brass again, Faustus least of all.

His waxen wings did mount above his reach, And, melting, heavens conspir'd his overthrow

Chorus, Prologue

Yet art thou still but Faustus, and a man.

Faustus, Scene 1

Appetite and habit

Faustus names his own god early: ‘thine own appetite’. Each time conscience stirs, something pleasant is put in front of him, and he takes it. The play is unusually exact about how a free choice becomes a habit and a habit becomes a hardened heart, until he can say ‘I cannot repent’ and mean it as a description.

Devils with crowns in Scene 5, the Sins in Scene 6, a quiet sleep in Scene 11, Helen in Scene 13: each is asked for or accepted at a moment of doubt.

I'll have them fly to India for gold, Ransack the ocean for orient pearl, And search all corners of the new-found world For pleasant fruits and princely delicates

Faustus, Scene 1

Had I as many souls as there be stars, I'd give them all for Mephistophilis.

Faustus, Scene 3

Despair and repentance

In the theology the play works with, despair is the conviction that one’s sin is too great to be forgiven, and it is itself the sin that cannot be forgiven, because it turns away the remedy. Faustus is told by an Angel, an Old Man and three scholars that mercy is available, and answers each time that it is not available to him. Whether he is right is what the play leaves open.

‘Despair in God’ is a resolution in Scene 5 and a condition by Scene 13, where ‘I do repent; and yet I do despair’ puts both in one line.

The reward of sin is death: that's hard.

Faustus, Scene 1

O, Faustus, leave these frivolous demands, Which strike a terror to my fainting soul!

Mephistopheles, Scene 3

Grace, sin and the Reformation argument

The play is set in Luther’s university and written for an audience taught that salvation is God’s gift and not man’s achievement. That makes Faustus’s case hard. If he cannot repent, is that because he will not, or because grace has been withheld? The 1604 text keeps both answers alive and gives the Evil Angel the bleaker one.

The half-quoted verses of Scene 1 start the argument; ‘Christ cannot save thy soul, for he is just’ in Scene 6 is its darkest moment; the blood in the firmament in Scene 14 reopens it with minutes to go.

Time

Twenty-four years pass in a few hundred lines of clowning and court visits, and the final hour takes fifty-seven lines of verse with a clock striking through it. Marlowe makes the audience feel how the years were wasted by wasting them in front of us, and then makes a single hour unbearable by counting it.

First mentioned as a contractual term in Scene 3, felt as ‘the restless course that time doth run’ in Scene 11, and turned into sound in Scene 14.

Never too late, if Faustus can repent.

Good Angel, Scene 6

Tush, Christ did call the thief upon the Cross; Then rest thee, Faustus, quiet in conceit.

Faustus, Scene 11

Power and service

The deed of gift makes Mephistopheles the servant and Faustus the master, and the play shows that arrangement to be a fiction from the first meeting, when the devil explains he came of his own accord. Faustus’s requests are refused whenever they matter. The comic scenes repeat the pattern downwards: Wagner hires a clown, Robin summons a devil, and nobody who gives orders in this play is in charge.

‘How pliant is this Mephistophilis’ in Scene 3; ‘Thou traitor, Faustus, I arrest thy soul’ in Scene 13.

But his dominion that exceeds in this, Stretcheth as far as doth the mind of man; A sound magician is a mighty god

Faustus, Scene 1

For, when we hear one rack the name of God, Abjure the Scriptures and his Saviour Christ, We fly, in hope to get his glorious soul

Mephistopheles, Scene 3
