Doctor Faustus · Christopher Marlowe

Characters

Doctor Faustus

Doctor of divinity at Wittenberg; conjuror; party to the bond; first appears Scene 1

A man of low birth who has reached the top of his university and found the view disappointing. He is a brilliant talker and a careless reader, quick to quote half a verse of scripture and build a philosophy on it. What he wants changes from scene to scene: a god’s power in Scene 1, a wife in Scene 5, a nap in Scene 11. He addresses himself by name throughout, as though Faustus were someone he was managing.

Mephistopheles

A devil, servant to Lucifer, bound to Faustus for twenty-four years; first appears Scene 3

Spelt ‘Mephistophilis’ in the 1604 text. He is the most honest speaker in the play: he says plainly that conjuring did not compel him, that he is in hell as he stands there, and that the loss of heaven is a torment he would rather not discuss. Faustus takes none of it in. Once the deed is signed the candour hardens into management, and by Scene 13 he is handing Faustus a dagger and threatening to tear him in pieces.

Lucifer

Prince of devils; the other party to the contract; first appears Scene 6

He appears once in the 1604 text, at the exact moment Faustus calls on Christ to save his soul, and treats the prayer as a breach of contract. His method is a lawyer’s and then a showman’s: first the reminder of what was promised, then the Seven Deadly Sins to take Faustus’s mind off it.

The Good Angel

A voice from the morality plays, urging repentance; first appears Scene 1

Always enters with the Evil Angel and usually speaks first. The advice is short and practical: put the book down, read the scriptures, think of heaven. In Scene 6 the Good Angel gives the play’s most important line of doctrine, that it is never too late if Faustus can repent, and the whole argument turns on that ‘can’.

The Evil Angel

The opposing voice, urging Faustus on; first appears Scene 1

Where the Good Angel offers heaven, the Evil Angel offers honour, wealth and the rule of the elements, and when those stop working offers fear instead: repent and devils will tear you in pieces. The Evil Angel generally gets the last word in each exchange, and Faustus’s next speech generally picks it up.

The Old Man

An unnamed believer who makes the last appeal; first appears Scene 13

He has no history and no stake in the matter, and that makes him persuasive. He sees an angel over Faustus’s head with a vial of grace and talks him out of suicide. Faustus repays him by asking Mephistopheles to torture him. The devils can hurt his body and nothing else, and he leaves the stage for God as Faustus leaves it for Helen.

Wagner

Faustus’s servant and student; first appears Scene 1

A sharp boy who has picked up his master’s manner without his learning. He defeats two scholars with mock logic, hires a clown with borrowed devils, and in Scene 13 notices, puzzled, that a man who has just made his will is carousing with students. He inherits everything.

Valdes and Cornelius

Two German magicians, Faustus’s tutors in the art; first appears Scene 1

They appear in one scene, promise spirits like lions and argosies from Venice, lend the books and the method, and are never seen again. Their disappearance is pointed: they know the art well enough to teach it and have evidently never signed anything themselves.

The Scholars

Faustus’s university colleagues; first appears Scene 2

They frame the play. In Scene 2 they fear he has fallen into a damned art and go for help; in Scene 13 they ask, innocently, to see the most beautiful woman who ever lived; in Scene 14 they tell him to look up to heaven, and go next door to pray. They are the ordinary, decent, companionable life he says he wishes he had kept.

Robin and Ralph

An ostler and his friend; first appears Scene 8

Robin steals one of Faustus’s conjuring books and means to use it for drink and for Nan Spit the kitchen-maid. The joke is that it works: his gibberish fetches Mephistopheles all the way from Constantinople. Their ambitions are Faustus’s with the poetry taken out, and their punishment, to be an ape and a dog, delights them.

Helen of Troy

A silent figure brought by Mephistopheles; first appears Scene 13

She crosses the stage twice and says nothing. Faustus has already told the Emperor that he cannot raise true bodies, only spirits that resemble them, so the play has explained what she is before she enters. She is requested as a cure for thoughts of repentance, and she works.

The Chorus

Speaker of the prologue, two linking speeches and the epilogue; first appears Prologue

The Chorus tells the ending before the beginning and the moral after the end, and its tone is cooler than anything in between. It praises the scholar, names the fault as self-conceit, and closes by recommending that the wise wonder at unlawful things from a distance. How far the play agrees with its own Chorus is the oldest argument about it.
