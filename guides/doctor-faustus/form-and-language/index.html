Doctor Faustus · Christopher Marlowe

Form and language

Soliloquy

A character alone on stage, thinking aloud to the audience.

The play opens and closes on long solo speeches, and they mirror each other: a man alone with books deciding he has time for everything, and the same man alone with a clock. The first is orderly and wrong; the last is broken and clear-sighted.

Yet art thou still but Faustus, and a man.

Faustus, Scene 1

Blank verse

Unrhymed iambic pentameter, in which the sentence and the line are free to disagree.

Marlowe’s line is end-stopped and cumulative, each verse a complete boast: ‘I’ll have them’, ‘I’ll have them’, ‘I’ll have them’. In the final speech the same metre is made to stumble, with short lines, a line of Latin, and outcries that will not scan.

But his dominion that exceeds in this, Stretcheth as far as doth the mind of man; A sound magician is a mighty god

Faustus, Scene 1

The Chorus

A speaker outside the action who introduces, links and judges it.

Four speeches fix the play’s moral frame and cover its gaps in time. Because the Chorus gives the verdict before the evidence, the audience watches knowing the end, and attention moves from what will happen to why nothing prevents it.

His waxen wings did mount above his reach, And, melting, heavens conspir'd his overthrow

Chorus, Prologue

Allegory

Abstractions given bodies and names so an argument can be seen.

The Good and Evil Angels and the parade of Sins come from the older morality drama, where a representative man is fought over. Marlowe keeps the machinery and changes the man: Faustus has a name, a degree and a city, and the Angels may be heard as his own divided mind.

O, Faustus, lay that damned book aside, And gaze not on it, lest it tempt thy soul

Good Angel, Scene 1

Parody subplot

A low plot that repeats the main plot’s action in a debased form.

Wagner binds the Clown for seven years straight after Faustus offers himself for twenty-four; Robin wants a stolen book for wine and a kitchen-maid. The comic scenes are not relief. They state, without the verse, what Faustus’s ambitions come to.

he would give his soul to the devil for a shoulder of mutton, though it were blood-raw.

Wagner, Scene 4

Scriptural argument

Quoting or contesting scripture as a way of settling a dispute.

Faustus reads half of Romans 6:23 and half of 1 John 1:8–9, leaving out the gift of eternal life and the promise of forgiveness that complete them. ‘Consummatum est’, Christ’s last words in John’s gospel, is what he says on signing the deed.

The reward of sin is death: that's hard.

Faustus, Scene 1

Allusion

A deliberate reference to another text, used to borrow its argument.

Icarus in the Prologue, Paris and Semele in the Helen speech, and a line from Ovid’s Amores in the last hour, where a lover’s plea for the night to go slowly is spoken by a man waiting for devils. The classical references are beautiful and every one of them ends in a fall or a fire.

His waxen wings did mount above his reach, And, melting, heavens conspir'd his overthrow

Chorus, Prologue

Third-person self-address

A speaker who habitually refers to himself by name, as though to someone else.

‘Settle thy studies, Faustus’; ‘What art thou, Faustus, but a man condemn’d to die?’ He gives himself orders, encouragement and sentence in the second and third person, which splits the self that acts from the self that watches, and lets him talk about damnation as something that is happening to Faustus.

Yet art thou still but Faustus, and a man.

Faustus, Scene 1
