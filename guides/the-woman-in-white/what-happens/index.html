The Woman in White · Wilkie Collins

What happens

The First Epoch: Hartright The road, Limmeridge and the likeness

Walter Hartright takes a post teaching drawing in Cumberland, having met a woman dressed entirely in white on a lonely road near Hampstead at night and helped her into a cab. At Limmeridge he finds his pupil’s face is the same face, falls in love with an engaged woman, and is eased out of the household.

The First Epoch: Gilmore The settlement

The family solicitor takes over the narrative and the story becomes a document. Sir Percival’s lawyer refuses to protect Laura’s twenty thousand pounds, Mr Fairlie will not exert himself to object, and Gilmore signs a settlement he says no daughter of his would have been married under.

The First Epoch: Marian’s diary The marriage agreed

Marian’s diary begins, and with it the novel’s most immediate voice: dated, unrevised and written by somebody who does not know what happens next. Laura tells Sir Percival the truth about her feelings and he marries her anyway.

The Second Epoch: Blackwater Park Fosco, surveillance and a diary interrupted

The couple return with Count and Countess Fosco, and Blackwater Park becomes a place where letters are read and servants are managed. Marian climbs onto the verandah roof in the rain to hear the two men plan, and writes down what she has heard until the fever takes the pen out of her hand.

The Second Epoch: other hands The substitution

Mr Fairlie, the housekeeper Eliza Michelson, a cook, a doctor and a headstone carry the story through the exchange. Anne Catherick dies in London under Laura’s name; Laura is taken to the asylum under Anne’s, and every step is attested by somebody who saw only a fragment.

The Third Epoch: Hartright returns Walter’s return, and Laura’s grave

Walter comes back from Central America to find a stone with Laura’s name on it and a woman nobody will identify. The investigation becomes a search for dates: what is needed is proof that the journey to London happened after the death on the certificate, more than a confession.

The Third Epoch: Mrs Catherick and Fosco Mrs Catherick’s letter and Fosco’s confession

Mrs Catherick’s letter explains the vestry, the forged register and the secret Sir Percival died trying to burn. Fosco, writing under threat of exposure to the Brotherhood, gives the conspiracy as a piece of intellectual autobiography and awards himself the credit for it.

The Third Epoch: the conclusion Laura’s name restored

Fosco is killed in Paris by the Brotherhood he betrayed, the false inscription is cut away from the stone, and Laura’s name is publicly restored in front of the tenants at Limmeridge. Walter closes the file he has spent the novel assembling and hands the last word to Marian.
