The Woman in White · Wilkie Collins

Context

Dates

1824
Wilkie Collins born in London Son of the landscape painter William Collins, and trained for the law before he gave it up for fiction.
1851
Collins meets Dickens An amateur theatrical brings them together, and Dickens’s magazines become Collins’s main platform for the next decade.
1859
Composition begins Collins started writing on 15 August at Broadstairs, with no complete manuscript and a serial slot already booked.
1859
Serialisation starts The first instalment appeared on 26 November in All the Year Round and, the same week, in Harper’s Weekly in New York.
1860
The manuscript is finished Collins completed it on 26 July, while the serial was still running; the British serial ended on 25 August.
1860
First English book edition Three volumes, in the expensive format that circulating libraries preferred.
1861
The revised one-volume text Collins corrected the chronology and added a dated preface; this is the text most modern editions follow.
1868
Collins’s The Moonstone published Collins uses the same method again: a case assembled from the statements of people who each saw one part of it.
1871
Collins’s own stage version He dramatised the novel himself, part of a long effort to keep control of a story that was being adapted without him.
1889
Collins dies He had published more than twenty novels, and the sensation novel had long since been absorbed into the detective story.
1948
Warner Bros. film Directed by Peter Godfrey, with Eleanor Parker in the double role of Laura and Anne, which is how the cinema usually solves the resemblance.
1982
BBC television serial A serial version with room to keep the narrators separate, which is how the novel’s documentary structure survives adaptation.
1997
BBC television film A single-film adaptation that compresses the eleven narratives into one line of action, as most screen versions do.

Places

Cumberland
Limmeridge House and its churchyard: where Walter is engaged, where the false inscription is cut, and where Laura’s name is finally read out to the tenants.
London
Clement’s Inn, the Finchley Road at midnight, the asylum, and the poor lodgings where Walter, Marian and Laura hide while the case is built.
Hampshire
Blackwater Park, with its stagnant lake and dilapidated wing, and Welmingham, where Mrs Catherick sits at her window collecting respectability.
Paris
The Morgue by the river, where Fosco’s body is displayed to a crowd and identified by a mark on his left arm.
Liverpool
The port Walter sails from with the expedition to Central America, and the passenger lists that later help fix the dates.
Honduras
Where Walter lands with the archaeological expedition, and spends the months in which the conspiracy is carried out at home.

Critics

E. S. Dallas

The Times (1860)

Reviewing the book on 30 October 1860, Dallas showed that the dates around Laura’s journey to London could not be right. Collins admitted the mistake, and corrected it in the one-volume edition of 1861.

Margaret Oliphant

“Sensation Novels”, in Blackwood’s Edinburgh Magazine (1862)

Oliphant treated the book as the leader of the new sensation novel, and admired its restraint: its horror comes from an ordinary-seeming plot against a woman’s identity, not from the supernatural.
