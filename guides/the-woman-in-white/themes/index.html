The Woman in White · Wilkie Collins

Themes

Identity and documents

A person in this novel is whatever the paperwork says. A gravestone, a doctor’s certificate and a parish register are enough to kill a living woman and to make a commoner a baronet, and no amount of embodied self-knowledge will overturn any of them.

Anne’s white dress and Laura’s face set up the resemblance; the inscription in Limmeridge churchyard executes it; and the counter-attack, when it comes, is made of dates where recognition has failed.

the story here presented will be told by more than one pen, as the story of an offence against the laws is told in Court by more than one witness⁠—with the same object, in both cases, to present the truth always in its most direct and most intelligible aspect; and to trace the course of one complete series of events, by making the persons who have been most closely connected with them, at each successive stage, relate their own experience, word for word.

Walter Hartright, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter I

There, in the middle of the broad bright high road⁠—there, as if it had that moment sprung out of the earth or dropped from the heaven⁠—stood the figure of a solitary woman, dressed from head to foot in white garments, her face bent in grave inquiry on mine, her hand pointing to the dark cloud over London, as I faced her.

Walter Hartright, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter IV

Testimony and the record

The book is arranged as evidence in a trial that never takes place, and the arrangement is itself the argument: no single witness sees enough, and the person who compiles them holds the real power. Walter says so in the preamble and then exercises it for six hundred pages.

From Hartright’s opening statement of method, through Marian’s dated diary and Mr Fairlie’s self-serving deposition, to Fosco’s written confession, extracted under threat of exposure.

This is the story of what a woman’s patience can endure, and what a Man’s resolution can achieve.

Walter Hartright, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter I

Marriage, money and the law

The conspiracy needs no magic, only a settlement. Laura’s twenty thousand pounds passes to her husband if she dies first, and the whole plot is an attempt to bring that clause forward by about fifty years.

Drafted in Gilmore’s narrative, refused correction by an indolent guardian, and executed at Blackwater Park by a man who needs the money before his creditors arrive.

Seriously and sorrowfully I repeat here the parting words that I spoke at Limmeridge House:⁠—No daughter of mine should have been married to any man alive under such a settlement as I was compelled to make for Laura Fairlie.

Vincent Gilmore, The First Epoch, Vincent Gilmore’s narrative, Chapter IV

No man under heaven deserves these sacrifices from us women. Men! They are the enemies of our innocence and our peace⁠—they drag us away from our parents’ love and our sisters’ friendship⁠—they take us body and soul to themselves, and fasten our helpless lives to theirs as they chain up a dog to his kennel.

Marian Halcombe, The First Epoch, Marian Halcombe’s diary, Chapter II

Confinement and the asylum

Two women are shut up in this novel and neither is mad. Private asylum admission needed certificates and somebody willing to sign them, and Collins makes his most frightening scenes out of that ordinary administrative fact, with nothing Gothic in them.

Anne escapes in the first chapters, Laura is admitted under her name in the second epoch, and the rescue depends on a nurse being bribed; nobody raises any doubt.

Done! She has escaped from my asylum. Don’t forget; a woman in white. Drive on.

The proprietor of the asylum, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter IV

What women are allowed to do

The preamble divides the world into a woman’s patience and a man’s resolution, and the novel immediately disobeys it. Marian does the investigating, the climbing and the remembering; Laura’s own word about who she is counts for nothing in law.

Stated as a formula in the first paragraph, contradicted throughout Marian’s diary, and restored without comment at the end, when the record is closed by Walter and the estate passes to his son.

This is the story of what a woman’s patience can endure, and what a Man’s resolution can achieve.

Walter Hartright, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter I

She left the window⁠—and I said to myself, The lady is dark. She moved forward a few steps⁠—and I said to myself, The lady is young.

Walter Hartright, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter VI

Rank, name and forgery

Sir Percival’s secret is that his rank is a forgery, and the novel treats this as worse than the conspiracy it produced. A title, like an identity, turns out to be a line of writing in a book kept in a badly locked cupboard.

Hinted by Anne’s question about men of the rank of Baronet, investigated through the marriage register at Old Welmingham, and settled by a fire in a vestry.

English Society, Miss Halcombe, is as often the accomplice as it is the enemy of crime.

Count Fosco, The Second Epoch, Marian Halcombe’s narrative, Chapter III

I was struck by the insecurity of the place in which the register was kept. The door of the press was warped and cracked with age, and the lock was of the smallest and commonest kind. I could have forced it easily with the walking-stick I carried in my hand.

Walter Hartright, The Third Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter IX
