Identity and documents

A person in this novel is whatever the paperwork says. A gravestone, a doctor’s certificate and a parish register are enough to kill a living woman and to make a commoner a baronet, and no amount of embodied self-knowledge will overturn any of them.

Through the book Anne’s white dress and Laura’s face set up the resemblance; the inscription in Limmeridge churchyard executes it; and the counter-attack, when it comes, is made of dates where recognition has failed.

In the text

There, in the middle of the broad bright high road⁠—there, as if it had that moment sprung out of the earth or dropped from the heaven⁠—stood the figure of a solitary woman, dressed from head to foot in white garments, her face bent in grave inquiry on mine, her hand pointing to the dark cloud over London, as I faced her.

Walter Hartright, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter IV

The repeated ‘there’ holds her in place for a whole clause before the sentence admits what she is, and the two alternatives offered (out of the earth or down from the heaven) are both supernatural. She is described entirely by her clothes and her gesture; the novel will spend six hundred pages establishing that she also has a name.

The fool’s crime is the crime that is found out, and the wise man’s crime is the crime that is not found out.

Count Fosco, The Second Epoch, Marian Halcombe’s narrative, Chapter III

The antithesis is perfectly balanced and completely circular: the only evidence of a wise crime is that you have never heard of it. Fosco is describing the plan he is about to execute, out loud, to the woman who will try to stop him, and the pleasure of saying it is what he cannot forgo.

Testimony and the record

The book is arranged as evidence in a trial that never takes place, and the arrangement is itself the argument: no single witness sees enough, and the person who compiles them holds the real power. Walter says so in the preamble and then exercises it for six hundred pages.

Through the book From Hartright’s opening statement of method, through Marian’s dated diary and Mr Fairlie’s self-serving deposition, to Fosco’s written confession, extracted under threat of exposure.

In the text

the story here presented will be told by more than one pen, as the story of an offence against the laws is told in Court by more than one witness⁠—with the same object, in both cases, to present the truth always in its most direct and most intelligible aspect; and to trace the course of one complete series of events, by making the persons who have been most closely connected with them, at each successive stage, relate their own experience, word for word.

Walter Hartright, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter I

The comparison is to a court, and it promises the reader a jury’s job. What the sentence does not mention is the person who chose the witnesses and set them in order; ‘word for word’ guarantees the accuracy of each statement and says nothing about the selection.

The illness of our excellent Miss Halcombe has afforded me the opportunity of enjoying an unexpected intellectual pleasure.

Count Fosco, The Second Epoch, Marian Halcombe’s narrative, postscript

The violation is done entirely in the register of a compliment, and the reader discovers it at the same moment as the page is turned. It is also a formal outrage: the novel’s most private document is annotated by its antagonist, which tells you exactly how safe any of these testimonies are.

Marriage, money and the law

The conspiracy needs no magic, only a settlement. Laura’s twenty thousand pounds passes to her husband if she dies first, and the whole plot is an attempt to bring that clause forward by about fifty years.

Through the book Drafted in Gilmore’s narrative, refused correction by an indolent guardian, and executed at Blackwater Park by a man who needs the money before his creditors arrive.

In the text

Seriously and sorrowfully I repeat here the parting words that I spoke at Limmeridge House:⁠—No daughter of mine should have been married to any man alive under such a settlement as I was compelled to make for Laura Fairlie.

Vincent Gilmore, The First Epoch, Vincent Gilmore’s narrative, Chapter IV

He puts his objection on the record, draws the document anyway, and then quotes himself in the past tense at the end of his own statement, as a professional man does when he expects to be asked about it later. It is the most accurate picture of legal helplessness in the novel: he sees the clause, names it, and signs.

No man under heaven deserves these sacrifices from us women. Men! They are the enemies of our innocence and our peace⁠—they drag us away from our parents’ love and our sisters’ friendship⁠—they take us body and soul to themselves, and fasten our helpless lives to theirs as they chain up a dog to his kennel.

Marian Halcombe, The First Epoch, Marian Halcombe’s diary, Chapter II

The simile is domestic and brutal: a wife is a dog chained to a kennel, and the verbs (drag, take, fasten) are all transitive, with women as their object. It is the most explicit political statement in the novel, and Collins gives it to a woman writing privately in a diary she does not expect anyone to read.

Confinement and the asylum

Two women are shut up in this novel and neither is mad. Private asylum admission needed certificates and somebody willing to sign them, and Collins makes his most frightening scenes out of that ordinary administrative fact, with nothing Gothic in them.

Through the book Anne escapes in the first chapters, Laura is admitted under her name in the second epoch, and the rescue depends on a nurse being bribed; nobody raises any doubt.

In the text

Done! She has escaped from my asylum. Don’t forget; a woman in white. Drive on.

The proprietor of the asylum, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter IV

Four sentences, and the only description offered is a colour. The policeman asks what she has done and is answered with a word that means the opposite of an offence, which is the novel’s first demonstration that a certificate can achieve what would otherwise take a criminal charge.

What women are allowed to do

The preamble divides the world into a woman’s patience and a man’s resolution, and the novel immediately disobeys it. Marian does the investigating, the climbing and the remembering; Laura’s own word about who she is counts for nothing in law.

Through the book Stated as a formula in the first paragraph, contradicted throughout Marian’s diary, and restored without comment at the end, when the record is closed by Walter and the estate passes to his son.

In the text

This is the story of what a woman’s patience can endure, and what a Man’s resolution can achieve.

Walter Hartright, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter I

Two virtues, two sexes, and the capital letters fall on Man and not on woman. The book then reverses the assignment without ever amending the sentence: it is Marian who acts and Walter who spends years waiting for a date to be proved, and the formula stands at the front of the file like an uncorrected heading.

I am nothing but a bundle of nerves dressed up to look like a man.

Frederick Fairlie, The Second Epoch, Frederick Fairlie’s narrative

He describes himself as a bundle of parts, and does it with obvious pleasure. Collins gives him his own narrative so that he can volunteer this self-portrait: the man who will not be troubled, and whose indifference costs his niece her name, is allowed to explain himself, and does.

Rank, name and forgery

Sir Percival’s secret is that his rank is a forgery, and the novel treats this as worse than the conspiracy it produced. A title, like an identity, turns out to be a line of writing in a book kept in a badly locked cupboard.

Through the book Hinted by Anne’s question about men of the rank of Baronet, investigated through the marriage register at Old Welmingham, and settled by a fire in a vestry.

In the text

English Society, Miss Halcombe, is as often the accomplice as it is the enemy of crime.

Count Fosco, The Second Epoch, Marian Halcombe’s narrative, Chapter III

The foreigner is given the outsider’s privilege of describing the host country’s machinery, and the sentence turns out to be a plot summary: the conspiracy works because respectable people accept a certificate. Collins puts his own thesis in the villain’s mouth, which is a cheap trick that works.

I was struck by the insecurity of the place in which the register was kept. The door of the press was warped and cracked with age, and the lock was of the smallest and commonest kind. I could have forced it easily with the walking-stick I carried in my hand.

Walter Hartright, The Third Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter IX

A baronetcy is kept in a warped cupboard behind a lock that a man could break with a walking-stick, and Collins gives us the exact means to hand. The register is the same kind of object as the gravestone and the death certificate: a piece of writing that makes a person, kept somewhere nobody guards.
