The Woman in White · Wilkie Collins

Characters

Walter Hartright

A teacher of drawing, later the editor of the whole narrative; first appears The First Epoch, Chapter I

He is the novel’s hero and also its archivist, which are two different kinds of power. He collects, orders and introduces every other statement in the book, so the reader’s impression of Marian, Fosco and Laura has already passed through his hands before it arrives.

Marian Halcombe

Laura’s half-sister; first appears The First Epoch, Chapter VI

She is introduced through a description that admires her figure and recoils from her face, and then proceeds to do everything the plot requires: investigate, climb, travel, negotiate and remember. The novel gives her no money, no legal standing and the best prose in the book.

Laura Fairlie

Heiress of Limmeridge, later Lady Glyde; first appears The First Epoch, Chapter VIII

She is the object the conspiracy moves around, and Collins gives her almost no narrative of her own: her testimony is the one thing the plot establishes is legally worthless. What is stolen from her is the acknowledged fact of being herself, which matters more than the money.

Anne Catherick

The woman in white; first appears The First Epoch, Chapter IV

She is dressed in white because a kind woman once praised her in it, shut up in an asylum because she knew a secret she did not understand, and killed by the conspiracy because her heart gives out at the right moment for it. She gives the novel its title and almost nothing else of her own.

Sir Percival Glyde

Baronet, of Blackwater Park; first appears The First Epoch, Vincent Gilmore’s narrative

Charming for exactly as long as the settlement takes to sign, and after that a man locking doors. His secret is smaller and more frightening than the plot implies: he has no title at all, only a page added to a parish register, which makes him a forgery more than a villain.

Count Fosco

An Italian exile, Sir Percival’s friend; first appears The Second Epoch, Marian’s narrative

Enormous, silent-footed, fond of white mice and pastry, and the only character in the novel who thinks in terms of systems. He is also the one who writes his own confession and cannot resist making it a lecture; his vanity is what eventually kills him.

Frederick Fairlie

Laura’s uncle, master of Limmeridge House; first appears The First Epoch, Chapter VII

A collector of etchings and coins who has made his own nerves into a full-time occupation, and whose refusal to be troubled is what allows every stage of the conspiracy to proceed. Collins hands him a narrative of his own so that he can convict himself in his own voice.

Mrs Catherick

Anne’s mother, of Welmingham; first appears The Second Epoch, Marian’s narrative

She sold her silence for respectability and has spent twenty years collecting it in instalments: a house, a clock, and the clergyman’s bow every Sunday. Her narrative is the coldest thing in the book, because she is not ashamed of any of it.

Vincent Gilmore

The Fairlie family solicitor; first appears The First Epoch, Gilmore’s narrative

He writes the second narrative and supplies the novel’s legal spine: the marriage settlement, the twenty thousand pounds, and the clause that makes Laura worth more dead than alive. He sees exactly what is coming and signs anyway, because his client will not be bothered.
