The Woman in White · Wilkie Collins

Form and language

Multiple narrators

Telling one story through a sequence of first-person accounts by different people.

Eleven narrators, from a solicitor and a housekeeper to a cook, a doctor and a headstone, each speaking only of what they saw, and the differences between their voices carry the class comedy as well as the evidence.

the story here presented will be told by more than one pen, as the story of an offence against the laws is told in Court by more than one witness⁠—with the same object, in both cases, to present the truth always in its most direct and most intelligible aspect; and to trace the course of one complete series of events, by making the persons who have been most closely connected with them, at each successive stage, relate their own experience, word for word.

Walter Hartright, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter I

Documentary form

Building a novel out of objects that would exist in the world it describes.

Diaries with dates, letters, a marriage settlement, a death certificate, a parish register and a carved inscription are not illustrations of the plot; they are the plot, and altering one of them is the crime.

This is the story of what a woman’s patience can endure, and what a Man’s resolution can achieve.

Walter Hartright, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter I

Doubling

Two characters constructed so that one can stand in for the other.

Anne and Laura are half-sisters without knowing it, share a face and a childhood dress, and the conspiracy consists simply of letting the world act on the resemblance.

There, in the middle of the broad bright high road⁠—there, as if it had that moment sprung out of the earth or dropped from the heaven⁠—stood the figure of a solitary woman, dressed from head to foot in white garments, her face bent in grave inquiry on mine, her hand pointing to the dark cloud over London, as I faced her.

Walter Hartright, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter IV

Serial suspense

Shaping instalments so that each ends where a reader cannot stop.

Collins was writing weekly while the novel was appearing, and the seams show in the best way: narratives break off mid-crisis, and Marian’s diary simply stops in the middle of a sentence.

Done! She has escaped from my asylum. Don’t forget; a woman in white. Drive on.

The proprietor of the asylum, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter IV

Dates

Making the exact timing of events the decisive fact in a plot.

The conspiracy is overturned by proof that she travelled to London after the date on her own death certificate; recognising Laura is not enough.

she must have started on the twenty-sixth, and must have come to London one day after the date of her own death on the doctor’s certificate. If we can prove that date, we prove our case against Sir Percival and the Count.

Walter Hartright, The Third Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter IV

Physiognomy

Treating physical appearance as evidence of character, in the manner of Victorian physiognomy.

Walter grades Marian’s figure and then her face in two consecutive paragraphs, and the novel spends the rest of its length disproving the second reading with her conduct.

There, in the middle of the broad bright high road⁠—there, as if it had that moment sprung out of the earth or dropped from the heaven⁠—stood the figure of a solitary woman, dressed from head to foot in white garments, her face bent in grave inquiry on mine, her hand pointing to the dark cloud over London, as I faced her.

Walter Hartright, The First Epoch, Walter Hartright’s narrative, Chapter IV
