Robinson Crusoe · Daniel Defoe

Themes

Providence and interpretation

The book is less interested in whether God intervenes than in how Crusoe reads events. Barley springs up and is a miracle until he remembers the bag; an earthquake passes without a serious religious thought, and it takes a fever to bring one. Defoe keeps the event, the first reading and the later reading visibly separate, so belief is shown being made.

Warnings ignored in the storms of Chapters I–II, a conversion dated to a verse opened at random in Chapter VI, and a settled providential account of the whole life by Chapter XIV.

But my ill fate pushed me on now with an obstinacy that nothing could resist; and though I had several times loud calls from my reason and my more composed judgment to go home, yet I had no power to do it.

Chapter III

Work and property

For Crusoe survival becomes ownership: what he has made or fenced or fed becomes his, and the island follows. The novel gives unusual space to work that fails, a canoe he cannot launch, a ruined first harvest, pots that collapse, so the claim to possession is built visibly out of hours.

Salvage first, then manufacture, then agriculture and livestock; by Chapter IX he calls himself lord of the whole manor, and by Chapter XVI lord and lawgiver over people.

the middle station of life was calculated for all kind of virtue and all kind of enjoyments; that peace and plenty were the handmaids of a middle fortune

Chapter I

I was king and lord of all this country indefensibly, and had a right of possession; and if I could convey it, I might have it in inheritance as completely as any lord of a manor in England.

Chapter VII

Solitude and company

Twenty-four years pass before another human being is added, and the loneliness is described mostly through its substitutes: the parrot taught his own name, the dog at the table, the journal addressed to nobody. What Crusoe wants is a servant more than a companion, and the book grants that wish exactly.

Comfort in routine, then terror at the footprint, then a dream of rescuing a man who will serve him, and the dream comes true.

September 30, 1659⁠—I, poor miserable Robinson Crusoe, being shipwrecked during a dreadful storm in the offing, came on shore on this dismal, unfortunate island, which I called “The Island of Despair”; all the rest of the ship’s company being drowned, and myself almost dead.

Chapter V

how much more happy this life I now led was, with all its miserable circumstances, than the wicked, cursed, abominable life I led all the past part of my days

Chapter VIII

Slavery, service and empire

Crusoe is enslaved at Salé, sells Xury, is wrecked on a voyage to buy Africans, and ends with three subjects of three religions and a fleet of settlers to place. The novel neither hides this sequence nor comments on it; the connections are all in the plot, waiting for a reader to make them.

Slave in Chapter II, slave-trader in Chapter III, master in Chapter XIV, governor and proprietor by Chapter XVIII.

He offered me also sixty pieces of eight more for my boy Xury, which I was loth to take; not that I was unwilling to let the captain have him, but I was very loth to sell the poor boy’s liberty, who had assisted me so

Chapter III

Fear and its arithmetic

The footprint changes nothing material and everything else: the same island, the same stores, years of bad sleep. Defoe is exact about how fear reasons: it plans massacres, invents enemies, and then has to be argued down by an appeal to law and jurisdiction that Crusoe constructs himself.

Storms and drowning at first, then the sea, then other people; the last fear is the ordinary one of losing the money he has accumulated by post.

But my ill fate pushed me on now with an obstinacy that nothing could resist; and though I had several times loud calls from my reason and my more composed judgment to go home, yet I had no power to do it.

Chapter I

I was exceedingly surprised with the print of a man’s naked foot on the shore, which was very plain to be seen on the sand. I stood like one thunderstruck, or as if I had seen an apparition.

Chapter XI

Keeping the account

Crusoe counts everything: days on a post, seeds, goats, pieces of eight, mercies against afflictions in two columns. The novel is written in the idiom of a merchant’s books, and it takes the form seriously enough to let the ink run out and the journal stop, which is a limit no spiritual autobiography would admit.

The tally-post, the ledger of evil and good, the dated journal, and finally the Lisbon accounts that pay out the profits of his Brazilian plantation.
