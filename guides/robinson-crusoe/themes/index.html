Providence and interpretation

The book is less interested in whether God intervenes than in how Crusoe reads events. Barley springs up and is a miracle until he remembers the bag; an earthquake passes without a serious religious thought, and it takes a fever to bring one. Defoe keeps the event, the first reading and the later reading visibly separate, so belief is shown being made.

Through the book Warnings ignored in the storms of Chapters I–II, a conversion dated to a verse opened at random in Chapter VI, and a settled providential account of the whole life by Chapter XIV.

In the text

“Call on Me in the day of trouble, and I will deliver thee, and thou shalt glorify Me.”

Chapter VI

He opens the book casually, as he says, and the method is the one Betteredge uses with Robinson Crusoe itself in The Moonstone a century and a half later. Crusoe dates his deliverance from this reading, long before any rescue, so the turning point of the novel is an act of interpretation.

I have been, in all my circumstances, a memento to those who are touched with the general plague of mankind, whence, for aught I know, one half of their miseries flow

Chapter XIV

He offers himself as an exhibit, which is what a providential autobiography is supposed to do, and names the disease as discontent with one’s station. The claim sits oddly beside a narrator who is at that moment fortifying an island he has declared his own property.

Work and property

For Crusoe survival becomes ownership: what he has made or fenced or fed becomes his, and the island follows. The novel gives unusual space to work that fails, a canoe he cannot launch, a ruined first harvest, pots that collapse, so the claim to possession is built visibly out of hours.

Through the book Salvage first, then manufacture, then agriculture and livestock; by Chapter IX he calls himself lord of the whole manor, and by Chapter XVI lord and lawgiver over people.

In the text

I was lord of the whole manor; or, if I pleased, I might call myself king or emperor over the whole country which I had possession of: there were no rivals; I had no competitor, none to dispute sovereignty or command with me

Chapter IX

The passage climbs from manor to king to emperor and then collapses, because sovereignty with no rivals is indistinguishable from having nothing. Defoe puts the emptiness and the grandeur in the same sentence, and the reflection that follows, that he had nothing to covet, is the nearest the novel comes to contentment.

My island was now peopled, and I thought myself very rich in subjects; and it was a merry reflection, which I frequently made, how like a king I looked. First of all, the whole country was my own property, so that I had an undoubted right of dominion. Secondly, my people were perfectly subjected⁠—I was absolutely lord and lawgiver⁠—they all owed their lives to me, and were ready to lay down their lives, if there had been occasion for it, for me. It was remarkable, too, I had but three subjects, and they were of three different religions⁠—my man Friday was a Protestant, his father was a Pagan and a cannibal, and the Spaniard was a Papist. However, I allowed liberty of conscience throughout my dominions.

Chapter XVI

Three men become dominion, property and a constitutional settlement, complete with liberty of conscience granted from above to a Protestant, a pagan and a Catholic. The passage is offered as a merry reflection, and it is also a precise short description of how a colony describes itself.

Solitude and company

Twenty-four years pass before another human being is added, and the loneliness is described mostly through its substitutes: the parrot taught his own name, the dog at the table, the journal addressed to nobody. What Crusoe wants is a servant more than a companion, and the book grants that wish exactly.

Through the book Comfort in routine, then terror at the footprint, then a dream of rescuing a man who will serve him, and the dream comes true.

In the text

September 30, 1659⁠—I, poor miserable Robinson Crusoe, being shipwrecked during a dreadful storm in the offing, came on shore on this dismal, unfortunate island, which I called “The Island of Despair”; all the rest of the ship’s company being drowned, and myself almost dead.

Chapter V

The entry is dated to a day on which he had neither ink nor the heart to write, so the journal is a reconstruction pretending to be immediate. Naming the place is the first act of possession in the book, and the name will be dropped without comment as the island becomes an estate.

how much more happy this life I now led was, with all its miserable circumstances, than the wicked, cursed, abominable life I led all the past part of my days

Chapter VIII

The comparison is between two lives and not two places, and it is the argument the whole island section rests on: solitude with belief is preferable to society without it. Defoe needs this settled before the footprint arrives, because everything after Chapter XI tests it.

Slavery, service and empire

Crusoe is enslaved at Salé, sells Xury, is wrecked on a voyage to buy Africans, and ends with three subjects of three religions and a fleet of settlers to place. The novel neither hides this sequence nor comments on it; the connections are all in the plot, waiting for a reader to make them.

Through the book Slave in Chapter II, slave-trader in Chapter III, master in Chapter XIV, governor and proprietor by Chapter XVIII.

In the text

He offered me also sixty pieces of eight more for my boy Xury, which I was loth to take; not that I was unwilling to let the captain have him, but I was very loth to sell the poor boy’s liberty, who had assisted me so

Chapter III

The reluctance and the price are in the same sentence, and the price wins. Crusoe records that he was loth to sell the boy’s liberty, which shows he knows exactly what is being sold; the transaction is entered in the narrative with the same care as the eighty pieces of eight for the boat.

kissed the ground, and laid his head upon the ground, and taking me by the foot, set my foot upon his head; this, it seems, was in token of swearing to be my slave forever.

Chapter XIV

Crusoe reports the gesture and supplies its meaning in the same breath (‘this, it seems’), although he has no language in common with the man making it. The relationship is therefore interpreted into existence by the person it benefits, before a single word has been exchanged.

Fear and its arithmetic

The footprint changes nothing material and everything else: the same island, the same stores, years of bad sleep. Defoe is exact about how fear reasons: it plans massacres, invents enemies, and then has to be argued down by an appeal to law and jurisdiction that Crusoe constructs himself.

Through the book Storms and drowning at first, then the sea, then other people; the last fear is the ordinary one of losing the money he has accumulated by post.

In the text

But my ill fate pushed me on now with an obstinacy that nothing could resist; and though I had several times loud calls from my reason and my more composed judgment to go home, yet I had no power to do it.

Chapter I

He describes a compulsion and simultaneously reports that his reason was calling him home, so the sentence holds two explanations, fate and a man choosing. Defoe uses that doubleness throughout: Crusoe is never so determined by Providence that he is not also answerable.

I was exceedingly surprised with the print of a man’s naked foot on the shore, which was very plain to be seen on the sand. I stood like one thunderstruck, or as if I had seen an apparition.

Chapter XI

Everything hangs on the singular: one print, and no others, which is why it cannot be explained and cannot be ignored. Defoe surrounds it with sober detail (‘about noon’, ‘very plain to be seen’), and that plainness makes it the most frightening moment in eighteenth-century English fiction.

Keeping the account

Crusoe counts everything: days on a post, seeds, goats, pieces of eight, mercies against afflictions in two columns. The novel is written in the idiom of a merchant’s books, and it takes the form seriously enough to let the ink run out and the journal stop, which is a limit no spiritual autobiography would admit.

Through the book The tally-post, the ledger of evil and good, the dated journal, and finally the Lisbon accounts that pay out the profits of his Brazilian plantation.

In the text

I was born in the year 1632, in the city of York, of a good family, though not of that country, my father being a foreigner of Bremen

Chapter I

Year, city, family standing and the father’s country of origin, in that order: the opening reads like a deposition or a parish record. Defoe buys credit with it before a single event has happened, and many first readers spent it on believing the book was true.

I stated very impartially, like debtor and creditor, the comforts I enjoyed against the miseries I suffered

Chapter IV

The simile is not decorative: what follows is literally ruled into evil and good, item against item, cast off wretched against alive when the rest drowned. A merchant’s habit is applied to the state of a soul, and Crusoe’s comfort comes from the form of the accounts, whatever is in the entries.
