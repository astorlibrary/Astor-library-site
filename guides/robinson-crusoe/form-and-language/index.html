Robinson Crusoe · Daniel Defoe

Form and language

Retrospective first person

A narrator recounting his own past with the benefit of knowing how it ended.

Crusoe repeatedly corrects his younger self (the barley he took for a miracle grew from chicken feed he had shaken out of a bag), so the book contains its own commentary.

I was born in the year 1632, in the city of York, of a good family, though not of that country, my father being a foreigner of Bremen

Chapter I

The interpolated journal

A dated record set inside a continuous narrative, covering ground the narrative has already told.

Chapter V begins the island story again from 30 September 1659 at a different distance, and the two accounts of the same months can be read against each other.

September 30, 1659⁠—I, poor miserable Robinson Crusoe, being shipwrecked during a dreadful storm in the offing, came on shore on this dismal, unfortunate island, which I called “The Island of Despair”; all the rest of the ship’s company being drowned, and myself almost dead.

Chapter V

Circumstantial realism

Conviction produced by the quantity and dullness of physical detail.

Eleven trips to the wreck, the exact list of what each raft carried, the months spent on a canoe that is never launched: the novel is persuasive because it is willing to be tedious.

I was born in the year 1632, in the city of York, of a good family, though not of that country, my father being a foreigner of Bremen

Chapter I

Providential reading

Treating events as a message and looking for what they are meant to teach.

A Bible opened at random supplies the date of his conversion, and thereafter every deliverance is entered on the credit side of a spiritual account.

I began to suggest that God had miraculously caused His grain to grow without any help of seed sown, and that it was so directed purely for my sustenance on that wild, miserable place.

Chapter V

Self-justification

Reasoning conducted to license a course of action already wanted.

The argument that the cannibals have committed no crime against him stops a massacre; the same faculty lets him sell Xury for sixty pieces of eight once the captain promises to free the boy in ten years.

But my ill fate pushed me on now with an obstinacy that nothing could resist; and though I had several times loud calls from my reason and my more composed judgment to go home, yet I had no power to do it.

Chapter I

The inventory

A list of goods used as narrative.

Tools, powder, clothes, money and Bibles are counted off the wreck, and the running stock-take doubles as the measure of how far the island has been turned into an estate.

He offered me also sixty pieces of eight more for my boy Xury, which I was loth to take; not that I was unwilling to let the captain have him, but I was very loth to sell the poor boy’s liberty, who had assisted me so
