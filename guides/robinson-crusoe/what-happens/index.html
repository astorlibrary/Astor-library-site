Robinson Crusoe · Daniel Defoe

What happens

Chapters I–III York, Salé and the Brazilian plantation

Crusoe leaves against his father’s advice, is wrecked within days of his first voyage and goes to sea again anyway. Captured by a Salé rover, he escapes in a boat with a boy called Xury, sells him to the Portuguese captain who rescues them both, and becomes a planter in Brazil. He is prosperous and bored when he agrees to fetch enslaved Africans from Guinea.

Chapter I
The father’s argument for the middle station, the storm off Yarmouth, and the obstinacy that survives it.
Chapter II
Two years a slave at Salé, then a stolen boat and a voyage down the African coast with Xury.
Chapter III
A Portuguese ship takes them to Brazil and Xury is sold; the plantation thrives, and a voyage for slaves ends on a sandbank; Crusoe alone reaches the shore.

Chapters IV–V The wreck, the raft and the journal

Eleven trips to the wreck bring off tools, guns, powder, ink and money he laughs at and keeps. He builds a fortified dwelling, notches a post to hold the calendar, draws up a ledger of evil against good, and then starts the narrative again from the beginning in the form of a dated journal, so the same months are told twice at two distances.

Chapter IV
Salvage, the tent under the hill, and the balance sheet of comforts against miseries.
Chapter V
The dated journal opens on 30 September 1659 and runs until the ink begins to fail.

Chapters VI–VIII Fever, tobacco and a Bible opened at random

An earthquake and then a long fever break the routine. Crusoe dreams of a figure with a flame about him, steeps tobacco in rum, opens a Bible at a verse about the day of trouble, and dates his deliverance from that reading, long before any rescue. The barley he first called a miracle is traced back to a bag he shook out himself.

Chapter VI
The fever, the dream, the tobacco cure and the first prayer he calls sincere.
Chapter VII
Seasons learned by loss, seed hoarded, and a survey of the island that leaves him lord of all he sees.
Chapter VIII
The calendar corrected, the Sabbath kept, and a settled comparison between this life and the one he led before.

Chapters IX–X Work, failure and a second dwelling

The middle of the book is a record of processes: a canoe too heavy to launch, earthenware discovered by accident in the ashes, bread made from grinding to baking, goats tamed and penned. Defoe gives as much space to the failures as to the successes, which is what separates the whole novel from every abridgement of it.

Chapter IX
A canoe hollowed out over months and left where it lies; the country house, the grapes, the parrot.
Chapter X
Pottery, a grindstone, a flock of his own, and a coasting voyage that nearly carries him out to sea for good.

Chapters XI–XIII The footprint, and the cannibals

A single print in the sand ends fifteen years of feeling safe. Crusoe fortifies, hides his goats, plans massacres and argues himself out of them on the ground that the cannibals have committed no crime against him. The chapters are a long study of what fear does to a man who has nobody to check his reasoning.

Chapter XI
The print, the thunderstruck stop, and two years of broken sleep.
Chapter XII
The bones on the shore, the plan to kill, and the argument that stops him.
Chapter XIII
A second wreck, more salvage, and the wish for one companion that shapes the rest of the book.

Chapters XIV–XVI Friday, the Spaniard and Friday’s father

Crusoe dreams of rescuing a prisoner who will serve him, and then does it. Friday is named for the day of the week, taught to say Master almost before anything else, and converted, though he asks why, if God is so strong, he does not kill the devil. By the time a Spaniard and Friday’s father arrive, Crusoe is describing himself as lord and lawgiver of three subjects and three religions.

Chapter XIV
The dream of a servant, the pursuit across the sand, and the token of submission Friday offers on the beach.
Chapter XV
Language, clothes, roast meat instead of human flesh, and a theological question Crusoe cannot answer.
Chapter XVI
The Spaniard and Friday’s father are saved; the island acquires a population and a constitution.

Chapters XVII–XX The mutiny, the passage home and the reckoning

An English ship arrives with a captain and his officers about to be marooned by their own crew. Crusoe presents himself as the island’s governor, takes the ship, and sails after twenty-eight years to find that the Brazilian plantation has been making him rich in his absence. The last chapter crosses the Pyrenees in snow and ends with wolves, so the book does not finish on the beach.

Chapter XVII
Three men set ashore, the captain rescued, and a governor invented for the occasion.
Chapter XVIII
The ship retaken, the ringleader shot and his body hung at the yardarm, and the worst of the mutineers left on the island in Crusoe’s place.
Chapter XIX
Lisbon, the old Portuguese captain, and an estate that has been growing without him for nearly thirty years.
Chapter XX
The overland journey through the Pyrenees, a wolf pack, and a promise of a second part.
