Robinson Crusoe · Daniel Defoe

Characters

Robinson Crusoe

Merchant, planter, slave, castaway, and self-appointed governor; first appears Chapter I

A man who narrates his own life twice over: once as it happened and once as he later decides it should be understood. He is practical, repetitive and exhaustive about work, and equally exhaustive about accounting for his soul, using the same arithmetic of loss and gain for both.

Crusoe’s father

A merchant of Bremen settled at York; first appears Chapter I

He appears for one speech and is quoted for the rest of the book. His case for the middle station (fewest disasters, fewest distempers, peace and plenty) is the standard against which Crusoe measures every subsequent calamity, usually while calling himself a warning to others.

Xury

A boy enslaved with Crusoe at Salé; first appears Chapter II

He escapes with Crusoe, keeps watch through the nights on the African coast, offers to go ashore first and be shot first, and is sold within a chapter for sixty pieces of eight. Defoe records Crusoe’s reluctance and the terms of the sale in the same sentence, which is as close as the book comes to an admission.

The Portuguese captain

The master who picks Crusoe up off the African coast; first appears Chapter III

The one figure in the book who is repeatedly generous without calculation: he takes nothing for the passage, buys the boat at a price Crusoe does not set, and nearly thirty years later, in Lisbon, gives an honest account of the Brazilian plantation and pays what he owes. Defoe uses him as evidence that honest dealing is possible, and therefore that its absence elsewhere is a choice.

Friday

A prisoner rescued from cannibals on the shore; first appears Chapter XIV

He is given a name from the calendar, taught the word Master before he is taught yes and no, and shown enormous affection within a relationship he never had the option of refusing. He is also the only character who asks Crusoe a question he cannot answer, and the book lets that stand.

Friday’s father

An old prisoner saved from the canoe; first appears Chapter XVI

His arrival makes the island a settlement instead of a household, and gives Friday the one relationship that is not owed to Crusoe. The reunion is the most straightforwardly felt scene in the book, and Crusoe reports it with some surprise at the strength of a feeling he has not arranged.

The Spaniard

A survivor of the wrecked ship, saved from the same canoe; first appears Chapter XVI

He brings news that sixteen more of his countrymen are living on the mainland, and with him the island becomes a colonial question: who feeds them, who governs them, and on what terms they may be brought over. Crusoe draws up conditions before he will allow it.

The English captain

Master of the ship taken by mutineers; first appears Chapter XVII

His arrival converts Crusoe’s private kingdom into something with a flag: the captain is told he is dealing with the governor, and the fiction is maintained through the whole recapture. He supplies the means of rescue and, in return, legitimacy for a claim Crusoe has been making to himself for years.
