Tyranny and performance

Richard does not conceal power so much as dramatise it: a fake arrest, a fake emergency, a fake refusal of the crown, all played to audiences who are given their cues. The play argues that consent can be manufactured in public, in daylight, by people who know they are acting.

Through the play Private improvisation in Act I becomes a civic production in 3.7, and collapses in Act V when there is nobody left to play to.

In the text

Now is the winter of our discontent Made glorious summer by this son of York; And all the clouds that loured upon our house In the deep bosom of the ocean buried.

Gloucester, 1.1.1

The famous first clause is only half a sentence, and the half that follows reverses it: the winter is over. ‘Son of York’ is also a pun on the sun, Edward’s badge, and the whole passage is a victory speech delivered by the one man in the family who has no use for victory.

Thus, like the formal vice, Iniquity, I moralize two meanings in one word.

Gloucester, 3.1.82

He names his own theatrical ancestor: the Vice of the morality plays, who joked with the audience while damning the hero. ‘Moralize’ means to draw out a meaning, and he is boasting that he can do it twice over, one meaning for the boy and the other for us.

The audience

Richard tells us his plans, invites us to admire the workmanship and jokes with us about the people he is about to kill. The discomfort is structural: the play gives the audience a better relationship with the murderer than it gives anyone on stage.

Through the play Once he is crowned the soliloquies shrink to bookkeeping, and the confidence is withdrawn just as the murders become unbearable.

In the text

Was ever woman in this humour woo’d? Was ever woman in this humour won? I’ll have her; but I will not keep her long.

Gloucester, 1.2.221

Two rhetorical questions to us, and then the third line disposes of her in ten words. The rhyme of woo’d and won makes the whole seduction sound like a completed piece of business, and the audience is being invited to applaud a performance whose victim has only just walked off.

What do I fear? myself? there’s none else by: Richard loves Richard; that is, I am I. Is there a murderer here? No. Yes, I am: Then fly. What, from myself?

King Richard III, 5.3.180

The soliloquy that was always a performance for us becomes a quarrel with no audience at all, and the syntax splits into question and answer as though two people were speaking. ‘I am I’ should be the most secure sentence in the language; here it is the last thing he can get to hold still.

Curses, prophecy and memory

Margaret’s curses in 1.3 function as a programme for the rest of the play, and the play’s dead are its most reliable record-keepers. Whether this is providence or simply the way the guilty are made to remember is left open.

Through the play Named in Act I, quoted by Rivers at Pomfret, by Hastings on the block and by Buckingham at Salisbury, and finally embodied by the ghosts at Bosworth.

In the text

Thou elvish-mark’d, abortive, rooting hog! Thou that wast seal’d in thy nativity The slave of nature and the son of hell!

Queen Margaret, 1.3.228

‘Rooting hog’ is his own badge, the white boar, turned into an insult, and ‘seal’d in thy nativity’ claims that his body was a signature written at birth. Richard’s answer is to wait for her to finish and then substitute her name for his at the end of the curse, the only way anyone in the play ever interrupts her.

O momentary grace of mortal men, Which we more hunt for than the grace of God! Who builds his hope in air of your good looks Lives like a drunken sailor on a mast, Ready with every nod to tumble down Into the fatal bowels of the deep.

Hastings, 3.4.95

The image is exact about what he got wrong: a sailor high on a mast can see further than anybody on deck and is still the first to fall. ‘Nod’ does double work, as the roll of the ship and the small movement of a great man’s head, so favour and shipwreck become the same gesture.

Conscience

The word is argued over by two hired murderers before it ever troubles Richard, and their comic debate about the inconvenience of having a conscience sets the terms for his nightmare four acts later. He calls it a word cowards use, in a speech that is itself an attempt to talk himself out of one.

Through the play A joke in 1.4, an absence through Acts II to IV, and by 5.3 a thousand several tongues, every one of them his.

In the text

Off with his head! Now, by Saint Paul I swear, I will not dine until I see the same.

Gloucester, 3.4.75

The domestic detail is the horror: an execution is fitted into the gap before lunch, and the oath is sworn on a saint. Hastings has spoken one cautious conditional, ‘if they have done this thing’, and the word ‘if’ is treated as the treason.

My conscience hath a thousand several tongues, And every tongue brings in a several tale, And every tale condemns me for a villain.

King Richard III, 5.3.191

The word he chose for himself in the first scene is now the verdict returned against him, and by a court made entirely of voices. Repeating ‘every’ makes conscience into a crowd, and for the first time in the play Richard is outnumbered in a room.

The women’s laments

Three women who have every reason to hate each other end up sitting on the ground together, comparing dead sons in almost liturgical sequence. It is the one form of speech in the play that Richard cannot enter, interrupt or turn to his advantage.

Through the play Rivalry in 1.3 and 2.2, shared grief in 4.1, and in 4.4 a set of matched laments that stop the plot for well over a hundred lines.

In the text

Why strew’st thou sugar on that bottled spider, Whose deadly web ensnareth thee about? Fool, fool! thou whet’st a knife to kill thyself.

Queen Margaret, 1.3.243

Margaret is right about everything and heard by nobody, which is her function until Act IV, when Elizabeth comes to her to be taught this vocabulary. The same ‘bottled spider’ comes back from Elizabeth’s own mouth in 4.4, quoted almost exactly: the play remembers who was listening.

Bloody thou art, bloody will be thy end; Shame serves thy life and doth thy death attend.

Duchess of York, 4.4.194

A rhymed couplet from a mother is a formal withdrawal of blessing, and the chiasmus of blood at either end of the first line makes his beginning and his end the same substance. He has had the drums sounded through her speech, so the audience hears it over noise he ordered.

History and legitimacy

The play inherits a Richard already blackened by Tudor chroniclers writing for the dynasty his defeat created, and it ends by announcing that dynasty as the end of civil war. Any reading has to hold both the play’s theatrical delight in Richard and its political obligation to bury him.

Through the play Yorkist triumph in 1.1, usurpation staged as election in 3.7, and in 5.5 a marriage that joins the roses and closes the sequence begun in the Henry VI plays.

In the text

And therefore, since I cannot prove a lover, To entertain these fair well-spoken days, I am determined to prove a villain And hate the idle pleasures of these days.

Gloucester, 1.1.28

‘Determined’ carries both senses, resolved and settled in advance by something outside him, and the play never chooses between them. The reasoning is also theatrical: he cannot get the lover’s part, so he takes the other one.

We will unite the white rose and the red: Smile heaven upon this fair conjunction, That long have frown’d upon their enmity!

Richmond, 5.5.19

The settlement is announced, never shown: the bride is a woman who never appears, and the peace is described in the future tense throughout. After five acts of a man who told the audience the truth and lied to everyone else, the play ends with a public statement nobody is invited to examine.
