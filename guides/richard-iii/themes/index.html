Richard III · William Shakespeare

Themes

Tyranny and performance

Richard does not conceal power so much as dramatise it: a fake arrest, a fake emergency, a fake refusal of the crown, all played to audiences who are given their cues. The play argues that consent can be manufactured in public, in daylight, by people who know they are acting.

Private improvisation in Act I becomes a civic production in 3.7, and collapses in Act V when there is nobody left to play to.

Now is the winter of our discontent Made glorious summer by this son of York; And all the clouds that loured upon our house In the deep bosom of the ocean buried.

Gloucester, 1.1.1

Why, I, in this weak piping time of peace, Have no delight to pass away the time, Unless to spy my shadow in the sun And descant on mine own deformity

Gloucester, 1.1.24

The audience

Richard tells us his plans, invites us to admire the workmanship and jokes with us about the people he is about to kill. The discomfort is structural: the play gives the audience a better relationship with the murderer than it gives anyone on stage.

Once he is crowned the soliloquies shrink to bookkeeping, and the confidence is withdrawn just as the murders become unbearable.

Curses, prophecy and memory

Margaret’s curses in 1.3 function as a programme for the rest of the play, and the play’s dead are its most reliable record-keepers. Whether this is providence or simply the way the guilty are made to remember is left open.

Named in Act I, quoted by Rivers at Pomfret, by Hastings on the block and by Buckingham at Salisbury, and finally embodied by the ghosts at Bosworth.

Thou elvish-mark'd, abortive, rooting hog! Thou that wast seal'd in thy nativity The slave of nature and the son of hell!

Queen Margaret, 1.3.228

Queen Margaret, 1.3.243

Conscience

The word is argued over by two hired murderers before it ever troubles Richard, and their comic debate about the inconvenience of having a conscience sets the terms for his nightmare four acts later. He calls it a word cowards use, in a speech that is itself an attempt to talk himself out of one.

A joke in 1.4, an absence through Acts II to IV, and by 5.3 a thousand several tongues, every one of them his.

Off with his head! Now, by Saint Paul I swear, I will not dine until I see the same.

Gloucester, 3.4.75

I am not in the giving vein to-day.

King Richard III, 4.2.115

The women’s laments

Three women who have every reason to hate each other end up sitting on the ground together, comparing dead sons in almost liturgical sequence. It is the one form of speech in the play that Richard cannot enter, interrupt or turn to his advantage.

Rivalry in 1.3 and 2.2, shared grief in 4.1, and in 4.4 a set of matched laments that stop the plot for well over a hundred lines.

Duchess of York, 4.4.194

History and legitimacy

The play inherits a Richard already blackened by Tudor chroniclers writing for the dynasty his defeat created, and it ends by announcing that dynasty as the end of civil war. Any reading has to hold both the play’s theatrical delight in Richard and its political obligation to bury him.

Yorkist triumph in 1.1, usurpation staged as election in 3.7, and in 5.5 a marriage that joins the roses and closes the sequence begun in the Henry VI plays.
