Richard III · William Shakespeare

Characters

Richard, Duke of Gloucester

Later King Richard III; first appears 1.1

He is the funniest person in the play and knows it, which is the trap: the audience laughs at the jokes and is therefore in the room for the murders. He is not a hypocrite in the ordinary sense, because he tells us the truth constantly; he simply tells nobody on stage.

The Duke of Buckingham

Richard’s chief accomplice; first appears 1.3

The only person who is let into part of the plan, and he is good at it: the staged emergency and the bogus refusal of the crown are largely his productions. He hesitates exactly once, over the princes, and discovers that there was never a second place in the arrangement.

Queen Margaret

Widow of Henry VI, survivor of the previous three plays; first appears 1.3

Historically she was not in England at all by this date, and Shakespeare keeps her anyway because the play needs someone who remembers. Her curses are memory with a very long reach, and the characters who dismiss her in Act I quote her accurately on their way to the block.

Queen Elizabeth

Edward IV’s widow, mother of the princes; first appears 1.3

She begins as a court politician anxious about her relatives’ standing and ends as the one person who holds a long conversation with Richard and gives nothing away. Whether she agrees to give him her daughter or plays for time is a question the text leaves open.

Lady Anne

Widow of Prince Edward, later Richard’s queen; first appears 1.2

She is given one great scene, in which she out-argues Richard line for line and then takes his ring anyway, and one short scene four acts later in which she explains what the marriage has cost her. The play never accounts for the change, which is why the scene is still argued about.

The Duchess of York

Mother of Edward IV, Clarence and Richard; first appears 2.2

She has outlived a husband and is watching her sons destroy one another, and the play gives her the one curse that is delivered by someone with an unquestionable right to it. Her farewell to Richard is a mother’s formal withdrawal of blessing, and he asks the drums to drown it.

George, Duke of Clarence

Richard’s elder brother; first appears 1.1

He is imprisoned on the strength of a prophecy his brother invented and killed on the strength of a warrant his brother sent, and he spends his last scene describing a dream in which the dead he betrayed at Tewkesbury come for him. He is not innocent, and the murder is still shocking.

Lord Hastings

Lord Chamberlain; first appears 1.1

He celebrates the execution of his enemies in the morning and is executed himself after lunch, having explained an hour earlier that he knows Richard’s face too well to be in danger. His speech on the block is the play’s plainest statement about reading the powerful.

The Earl of Richmond

Henry Tudor, later Henry VII; first appears 5.2

He arrives with fewer than two hundred lines and no inner life at all, and that is a choice: after four acts of a man who talks to us all the time, the play gives us a leader who talks only to his army and to God. The play announces the Tudor settlement without dramatising it.
