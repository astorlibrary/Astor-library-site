Consciousness as a disease

To be too conscious is an illness: that is the narrator’s first idea. A man who sees every side of every question cannot act, cannot revenge himself honestly and cannot even become an insect; he has been going on like this for twenty years, and he learns to enjoy his own degradation because he understands it so well. The whole book is a demonstration: at forty he analyses himself without mercy, and at twenty-four his self-consciousness wrecks every encounter he has.

Through the book The sick man; the conscious mouse and the man of action; the pleasure in toothache; three hours from the table to the stove; I cannot be good.

In the text

I am a sick man.... I am a spiteful man. I am an unattractive man. I believe my liver is diseased.

Part I, chapter I

The book opens with four short sentences, each a confession and each a provocation. He introduces himself by his flaws, as if daring the reader to disagree, and within a page he admits that the second one was a lie told from spite. The voice is sick, clever and theatrical, and it does not change for the rest of the book.

Either to be a hero or to grovel in the mud—there was nothing between.

Part II, chapter II

His inner life has two settings, grandeur and degradation, and each excuses the other. When he was in the mud he comforted himself that at other times he was a hero, and the hero was a cloak for the mud. The sentence describes a familiar trap, and the dinner in the next chapters shows it at work.

Free will against reason

Against the belief that science will one day tabulate every desire, the narrator sets the freedom to choose, even to choose what is harmful and stupid. A man, he says, will do something perverse simply to prove he is a man and not a piano-key, and would go mad to escape reason if madness were calculated too. The argument is serious and extreme at the same time, and Part II shows what this freedom looks like in the life of the man who defends it.

Through the book Advantage and the advantage every list leaves out; the organ-stop; twice two makes four as the beginning of death; the ungrateful biped; caprice guaranteed when necessary.

In the text

The impossible means the stone wall! What stone wall? Why, of course, the laws of nature, the deductions of natural science, mathematics.

Part I, chapter III

The wall is the book’s first great image of necessity. Other people accept it with relief; the narrator refuses to be reconciled to it simply because it is a wall and he is not strong enough to knock it down. His rebellion is hopeless and he knows it, and the knowledge becomes part of the ache.

What man wants is simply independent choice, whatever that independence may cost and wherever it may lead.

Part I, chapter VII

This is the heart of Part I. The most advantageous advantage, the one that fits no classification, is choice itself, and it matters more than any benefit it could bring. Taken alone, the sentence reads like a founding statement of modern individualism; the story that follows shows how badly independent choice can serve the man who claims it.

The palace of crystal

The crystal palace was a real building, the glass hall of the Great Exhibition of 1851, which Dostoevsky saw in London in 1862. Chernyshevsky’s novel What Is to Be Done? (1863) made a palace of iron and glass the home of a rational socialist future, and Part I answers it. The narrator fears a building that can never be destroyed and at which nobody could put out his tongue, and he refuses to call a hen-house a mansion. It was in the chapter about it that the censor made his cuts.

Through the book Tables of logarithms and encyclopaedic lexicons; the Palace of Crystal will be built; the ant-heap; the hen-house and the mansion; I would rather my hand were withered off than bring one brick.

In the text

Then the “Palace of Crystal” will be built. Then.... In fact, those will be halcyon days.

Part I, chapter VII

The crystal palace arrives as the crowning building of the rational future, and the narrator trails off before describing it. Readers in 1864 would recognise Chernyshevsky’s palace of iron and glass from What Is to Be Done?, and behind it the real Crystal Palace in London. He adds straight away that such halcyon days might be frightfully dull.

You see, if it were not a palace, but a hen-house, I might creep into it to avoid getting wet, and yet I would not call the hen-house a palace out of gratitude to it for keeping me dry.

Part I, chapter X

What he objects to in the utopia is that it asks him to accept a shelter as a fulfilment. He will take shelter if he must, but he will not call it the crown of his desires, and he demands a better ideal. Dostoevsky told his brother that the censors had cut the passages in this chapter where he drew from all this the need for faith and Christ.

Humiliation and spite

Everything in Part II turns on being noticed or not noticed. The officer moves him like furniture, the schoolfellows treat him as a fly, Zverkov says he is incapable of insulting anybody, and Apollon stares him down. Each humiliation produces spite, and each piece of spite is passed downward, until he tells Liza plainly that because he had been humiliated he wanted to humiliate someone else.

Through the book The sword clanking in his office; moved without being noticed; the collision on the Nevsky; the dinner; the war over wages; the five-rouble note.

In the text

I could have forgiven blows, but I could not forgive his having moved me without noticing me.

Part II, chapter I

The insult is invisibility. A blow would at least have treated him as an opponent; being moved like a chair treats him as nothing. Years of stalking, planning and dreaming follow from this moment, which is the clearest case in the book of a small wound becoming a way of life.

Insulted? You insulted me? Understand, sir, that you never, under any circumstances, could possibly insult me.

Zverkov, Part II, chapter IV

Zverkov’s reply is the cruellest thing said at the dinner because it is not meant to be cruel. To be incapable of insulting someone is to be beneath consideration, and it echoes the officer who moved him without noticing him. The narrator stands, he says, as though spat upon.

Reading and romance

Without books, the narrator says, we would not know what to love or hate. He lives by them: his revenge fantasies come from Pushkin and Lermontov, his dreams of heroism from Byron and Napoleon, his speech to Liza from the novels of the day. Liza sees it straight away and says he speaks like a book. At the end he says that all of us agree privately that life is better in books.

Through the book A satirical story sent to Otechestvennye zapiski; the challenge written like a novel; Silvio and Masquerade in the sledge; he speaks somehow like a book; it is better in books.

In the text

I was actually on the point of tears, though I knew perfectly well at that moment that all this was out of Pushkin’s Silvio and Lermontov’s Masquerade.

Part II, chapter V

Pushkin’s story The Shot and Lermontov’s play supply the revenge fantasy ready-made, and he weeps at it while knowing where it came from. Consciousness does not stop the fantasy; it only adds shame. He gets out of the sledge, stands in the snow, and then gets back in.

“Why, you … speak somehow like a book,” she said, and again there was a note of irony in her voice.

Liza, Part II, chapter VI

Liza sees through him, but not as he thinks. The narrator writes that he did not understand then that her irony was a shield for feelings she was ashamed to show. Wounded, he decides to go further, and the speech that follows is crueller and far more effective.

Love and power

Liza offers him the only real feeling anyone shows him in the book, and he can only treat it as a contest. He wins her tears with a speech, confesses that what he wanted was power, and afterwards writes that for him loving meant tyrannising and showing his moral superiority. Liza understands his unhappiness and loves him anyway, and her leaving the money on the table is the one act in the book he cannot answer.

Through the book The speech in the dark; the student’s letter; the dressing-gown; the embrace; the five roubles; cheap happiness or exalted sufferings.

In the text

It was the exercise of my power that attracted me most.

Part II, chapter VI

He admits his motive in a single flat sentence in the middle of his most eloquent performance. Power over another person’s feelings is the one thing he has never had, and he finds it with the most vulnerable person in the book. The honesty of the admission is real, and it does not make the act any better.

In the first place, by then I was incapable of love, for I repeat, with me loving meant tyrannizing and showing my moral superiority.

Part II, chapter X

Merciless as a diagnosis, the sentence then becomes a generalisation, suggesting that love may really be the right, freely given, to tyrannise over the beloved. The reader has just seen what that means for Liza. The same chapter shows her understanding him better than he understands himself.

The underground

The underground is his corner, his room on the outskirts, and the state of mind he calls his underground world. He has been listening to the world for forty years through a crack under the floor. At twenty-four he had no friends, avoided schoolfellows and visited his superior only when a dream required a human being. He hails the underground, curses it and admits he wants something he cannot name, and his last word is that he does not want to write any more from it.

Through the book Settled in his corner; the mouse-hole; hurrah for underground! Damn underground!; my shell, my cave; the empty street after Liza.

In the text

But what can a decent man speak of with most pleasure? Answer: Of himself.

Part I, chapter I

Self-absorption is announced as a joke about everyone, decent men included. The question-and-answer form mimics a catechism. It is also a fair description of the whole book, which never leaves his head, and of the genre of confession Dostoevsky is writing inside and against.

The long and the short of it is, gentlemen, that it is better to do nothing! Better conscious inertia! And so hurrah for underground!

Part I, chapter XI

Part I concludes with a cheer for paralysis. It lasts a few sentences: he admits he is lying, that he is thirsting for something quite different that he cannot find, and ends with damn underground! The swing from hurrah to damn is the book’s rhythm, and it prevents any reader from taking the underground as a philosophy to live by.

St Petersburg

Petersburg, the narrator says, is the most theoretical and intentional town on the whole terrestrial globe, a city planned by decree and full of people who live in their heads. Part II is set in its wet snow and among its landmarks: the Nevsky where he stalks the officer, the Haymarket and the waterlogged Volkovo Cemetery he describes to Liza, and the Five Corners where his superior lives.

Through the book The Petersburg climate that is bad for him; the Nevsky at four o’clock; Gostiny Dvor and the German beaver; the Hôtel de Paris; the Haymarket; snow falling almost perpendicularly on an empty street.

In the text

Petersburg, the most theoretical and intentional town on the whole terrestrial globe.

Part I, chapter II

Peter the Great built Petersburg on a marsh by decree, and the narrator makes the city a symptom: a town planned in theory produces citizens who live in their heads. He adds in brackets that there are intentional and unintentional towns. The joke places his disease in a real place and a real history.

I closed my eyes, and we ran full tilt, shoulder to shoulder, against one another!

Part II, chapter I

All this revenge comes down to a shove on a pavement, prepared with borrowed money, new gloves and a beaver collar. He goes home triumphant and sings Italian arias; the officer does not even look round. The comedy is exact, and so is the sadness of a man whose victories have to be this small.
