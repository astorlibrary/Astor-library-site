Notes from Underground · Fyodor Dostoevsky

Themes

Consciousness as a disease

To be too conscious is an illness: that is the narrator’s first idea. A man who sees every side of every question cannot act, cannot revenge himself honestly and cannot even become an insect; he has been going on like this for twenty years, and he learns to enjoy his own degradation because he understands it so well. The whole book is a demonstration: at forty he analyses himself without mercy, and at twenty-four his self-consciousness wrecks every encounter he has.

The sick man; the conscious mouse and the man of action; the pleasure in toothache; three hours from the table to the stove; I cannot be good.

I am a sick man.... I am a spiteful man. I am an unattractive man. I believe my liver is diseased.

Part I, chapter I

It was not only that I could not become spiteful, I did not know how to become anything: neither spiteful nor kind, neither a rascal nor an honest man, neither a hero nor an insect.

Part I, chapter I

Free will against reason

Against the belief that science will one day tabulate every desire, the narrator sets the freedom to choose, even to choose what is harmful and stupid. A man, he says, will do something perverse simply to prove he is a man and not a piano-key, and would go mad to escape reason if madness were calculated too. The argument is serious and extreme at the same time, and Part II shows what this freedom looks like in the life of the man who defends it.

Advantage and the advantage every list leaves out; the organ-stop; twice two makes four as the beginning of death; the ungrateful biped; caprice guaranteed when necessary.

The impossible means the stone wall! What stone wall? Why, of course, the laws of nature, the deductions of natural science, mathematics.

Part I, chapter III

What man wants is simply independent choice, whatever that independence may cost and wherever it may lead.

Part I, chapter VII

The palace of crystal

The crystal palace was a real building, the glass hall of the Great Exhibition of 1851, which Dostoevsky saw in London in 1862. Chernyshevsky’s novel What Is to Be Done? (1863) made a palace of iron and glass the home of a rational socialist future, and Part I answers it. The narrator fears a building that can never be destroyed and at which nobody could put out his tongue, and he refuses to call a hen-house a mansion. It was in the chapter about it that the censor made his cuts.

Tables of logarithms and encyclopaedic lexicons; the Palace of Crystal will be built; the ant-heap; the hen-house and the mansion; I would rather my hand were withered off than bring one brick.

Then the “Palace of Crystal” will be built. Then.... In fact, those will be halcyon days.

Part I, chapter VII

I admit that twice two makes four is an excellent thing, but if we are to give everything its due, twice two makes five is sometimes a very charming thing too.

Part I, chapter IX

Humiliation and spite

Everything in Part II turns on being noticed or not noticed. The officer moves him like furniture, the schoolfellows treat him as a fly, Zverkov says he is incapable of insulting anybody, and Apollon stares him down. Each humiliation produces spite, and each piece of spite is passed downward, until he tells Liza plainly that because he had been humiliated he wanted to humiliate someone else.

The sword clanking in his office; moved without being noticed; the collision on the Nevsky; the dinner; the war over wages; the five-rouble note.

“Well? Even in toothache there is enjoyment,” I answer.

Part I, chapter IV

I could have forgiven blows, but I could not forgive his having moved me without noticing me.

Part II, chapter I

Reading and romance

Without books, the narrator says, we would not know what to love or hate. He lives by them: his revenge fantasies come from Pushkin and Lermontov, his dreams of heroism from Byron and Napoleon, his speech to Liza from the novels of the day. Liza sees it straight away and says he speaks like a book. At the end he says that all of us agree privately that life is better in books.

A satirical story sent to Otechestvennye zapiski; the challenge written like a novel; Silvio and Masquerade in the sledge; he speaks somehow like a book; it is better in books.

Either to be a hero or to grovel in the mud—there was nothing between.

Part II, chapter II

I was actually on the point of tears, though I knew perfectly well at that moment that all this was out of Pushkin’s Silvio and Lermontov’s Masquerade.

Part II, chapter V

Love and power

Liza offers him the only real feeling anyone shows him in the book, and he can only treat it as a contest. He wins her tears with a speech, confesses that what he wanted was power, and afterwards writes that for him loving meant tyrannising and showing his moral superiority. Liza understands his unhappiness and loves him anyway, and her leaving the money on the table is the one act in the book he cannot answer.

The speech in the dark; the student’s letter; the dressing-gown; the embrace; the five roubles; cheap happiness or exalted sufferings.

“Why, you ... speak somehow like a book,” she said, and again there was a note of irony in her voice.

Liza, Part II, chapter VI

It was the exercise of my power that attracted me most.

Part II, chapter VI

The underground

The underground is his corner, his room on the outskirts, and the state of mind he calls his underground world. He has been listening to the world for forty years through a crack under the floor. At twenty-four he had no friends, avoided schoolfellows and visited his superior only when a dream required a human being. He hails the underground, curses it and admits he wants something he cannot name, and his last word is that he does not want to write any more from it.

Settled in his corner; the mouse-hole; hurrah for underground! Damn underground!; my shell, my cave; the empty street after Liza.

But what can a decent man speak of with most pleasure? Answer: Of himself.

Part I, chapter I

It may be an acutely conscious mouse, yet it is a mouse, while the other is a man

Part I, chapter III

St Petersburg

Petersburg, the narrator says, is the most theoretical and intentional town on the whole terrestrial globe, a city planned by decree and full of people who live in their heads. Part II is set in its wet snow and among its landmarks: the Nevsky where he stalks the officer, the Haymarket and the waterlogged Volkovo Cemetery he describes to Liza, and the Five Corners where his superior lives.

The Petersburg climate that is bad for him; the Nevsky at four o’clock; Gostiny Dvor and the German beaver; the Hôtel de Paris; the Haymarket; snow falling almost perpendicularly on an empty street.

Petersburg, the most theoretical and intentional town on the whole terrestrial globe.

Part I, chapter II

I closed my eyes, and we ran full tilt, shoulder to shoulder, against one another!

Part II, chapter I
