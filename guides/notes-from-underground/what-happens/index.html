Notes from Underground · Fyodor Dostoevsky

What happens

Part I, I–IV A sick man, a mouse and a stone wall

The narrator introduces himself as sick, spiteful and unattractive, then admits he was lying. He is forty, retired, and living in his corner. Too much consciousness, he argues, is a disease: it makes action impossible, stretches every insult into years of brooding and finds pleasure even in humiliation. The man of action runs into the stone wall of the laws of nature and stops; the conscious man, a mouse beside him, cannot accept the wall and cannot break it.

Part I, chapter I
Spite towards petitioners, a feud with an officer over a sword, and a man who could not become anything.
Part I, chapter II
Consciousness as illness, and the secret enjoyment of one’s own degradation.
Part I, chapter III
The man of action, the conscious mouse, and twice two makes four.
Part I, chapter IV
The educated man’s malicious moans, and whether a man of perception can respect himself.

Part I, V–IX The most advantageous advantage

He attacks the theory that people act badly only because they misunderstand their interests and would be good once enlightened. History is full of people choosing against their advantage. There is one advantage every list leaves out: the freedom to choose, even to choose harm, simply to prove that one is a man and not a piano-key or an organ-stop. Twice two makes four, he says, is the beginning of death, and twice two makes five is sometimes a charming thing too.

Part I, chapters V–VI
Invented quarrels, love tried twice from boredom, and the dream of being a respected sluggard.
Part I, chapter VII
Buckle, blood spilt like champagne, tables of logarithms, and a gentleman who kicks over the whole show.
Part I, chapters VIII–IX
The ungrateful biped, the ant-heap, and the love of building roads more than arriving.

Part I, X–XI The palace of crystal, and why he writes

He will not accept a hen-house as a palace, and he fears the crystal palace because nobody could put out his tongue at it. Better to do nothing: hurrah for underground! Then he admits that he is lying, that he wants something else that he cannot find, and that he believes none of what he has written. He explains that he writes for himself alone, in the form of an address to readers because it is easier, and that the wet snow falling today has brought back a memory he means to write down.

Part I, chapter X
A building one cannot put one’s tongue out at; the chapter the censor cut.
Part I, chapter XI
Conscious inertia, the imagined readers’ accusations, Heine on lying autobiographies, and the snow.

Part II, I–II The officer on the Nevsky, and the dreams

At twenty-four he is a clerk who hates and fears his colleagues and lives by reading and furtive vice. An officer in a tavern moves him out of the way by the shoulders without noticing him, and he spends years planning revenge: a satirical story, an unsent letter, a new beaver collar bought on borrowed money, and at last a collision on the Nevsky in which he does not give way. Between bouts of dissipation he dreams for months of being a hero, and when the dreams require it he visits his superior Anton Antonitch on Tuesdays.

Part II, chapter I
Moved like a piece of furniture, then two years of stalking, gloves, a collar and a shoulder.
Part II, chapter II
Dreams of Austerlitz and Lake Como, Tuesdays at Anton Antonitch’s, and a visit to Simonov.

Part II, III–V The farewell dinner

At Simonov’s he learns that three old schoolfellows are giving a dinner for Zverkov, whom he has always hated, and invites himself. He arrives an hour early because nobody told him the time had changed, quarrels, makes an insulting toast, challenges Ferfitchkin, and then walks up and down the room from table to stove for three hours while the others ignore him. When they leave for a brothel he borrows six roubles from Simonov and follows them in a sledge through the wet snow, meaning to slap Zverkov’s face.

Part II, chapter III
Seven roubles each, an uninvited guest, and memories of a hateful school.
Part II, chapter IV
Five o’clock instead of six, a toast against phrasemongers, and three hours from the table to the stove.
Part II, chapter V
Silvio and Masquerade in his head, a blow to the driver’s neck, and an empty drawing-room.

Part II, VI–VIII Liza

The others have gone, and he spends two hours with a young woman without speaking to her. Waking in the dark, he learns that she is Liza, twenty, from Riga, and in the house a fortnight, and he sets out to move her: a coffin carried from a basement, the future waiting for her, a home, a husband and a child. She weeps and bites her hand, shows him a respectful love letter from a student, and promises to come to his address. For days afterwards he dreads her coming and quarrels with his servant Apollon over seven roubles of wages.

Part II, chapter VI
A wheezing clock, a coffin in the Haymarket, and pictures of family happiness; she says he speaks like a book.
Part II, chapter VII
Her future in the Haymarket, her grief, the student’s letter, and his address.
Part II, chapter VIII
A letter of excuses to Simonov, a war of stares over wages, and Liza at the door at seven o’clock.

Part II, IX–X Liza’s visit, and the five roubles

Liza finds him in a ragged dressing-gown, shouting at his servant. He weeps, then tells her that he was laughing at her that night, that he wanted power over her, and that he will never forgive her for seeing him like this. She understands that he is unhappy and embraces him. Afterwards, from spite, he presses a five-rouble note into her hand; she leaves it on the table and goes. He runs after her into the snow, stops, and goes home. He never sees her again, and he ends his notes, though an editor adds that the paradoxalist could not stop writing.

Part II, chapter IX
Tea and rusks, hysterics, a confession of cruelty, and Liza’s embrace.
Part II, chapter X
The five roubles, the empty street, cheap happiness or exalted suffering, and the anti-hero’s last words.
