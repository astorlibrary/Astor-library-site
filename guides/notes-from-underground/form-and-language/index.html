Notes from Underground · Fyodor Dostoevsky

Form and language

The self-contradicting narrator

A narrator whose account cannot be taken at face value.

He lies from spite on his first page and owns up a few lines later. He calls his jokes bad and keeps them, claims to write only for himself while arguing with readers, and ends Part I by saying he believes nothing he has written. Part II is more reliable in its facts, and he is at his most honest when he admits the worst of what he did.

I am a sick man.... I am a spiteful man. I am an unattractive man. I believe my liver is diseased.

Part I, chapter I

Arguing with imagined readers

A speaker turning to address the reader, or someone within the story, directly.

Gentlemen, he calls them, and he answers their laughter, their objections and their contempt before they have been expressed. In chapter XI he admits that he has invented every one of their remarks himself, listening through a crack under the floor.

I swear, gentlemen, that to be too conscious is an illness—a real thorough-going illness.

Part I, chapter II

Objection and reply

An argument that states the opponent’s case in order to answer it.

Part I is built as a quarrel: you will say that science has proved there is no free will; you will laugh; you will scream at me. The narrator gives the rationalist position its best form, in quotation marks, and then tears it down, which is why the argument still reads as alive.

But what can a decent man speak of with most pleasure? Answer: Of himself.

Part I, chapter I

Argument by reversal

An argument that turns an accepted idea on its head.

Even in toothache there is enjoyment; the conscious man is a mouse; it is better to do nothing; twice two makes five is sometimes a charming thing; suffering is the sole origin of consciousness. The editor’s last note calls him a paradoxalist.

It was not only that I could not become spiteful, I did not know how to become anything: neither spiteful nor kind, neither a rascal nor an honest man, neither a hero nor an insect.

Part I, chapter I

Remembering at forty

An account given after the event by a narrator who already knows how it ends.

Part II is written by the man of Part I about himself at twenty-four, and he interrupts it to judge himself, to say what he has learned since, and to insist on the loathsome truth. The two parts show the ideas first and the life that produced them second.

I could have forgiven blows, but I could not forgive his having moved me without noticing me.

Part II, chapter I

The editor’s frame

A story placed inside another story or presented by an outside voice.

A footnote signed by the author opens the book, saying that the diarist is imaginary but a type who must exist; an unnamed editor closes it, saying that the paradoxalist’s notes go on but that we may stop here. The frame keeps Dostoevsky at a distance from his narrator.

The notes of this paradoxalist do not end here, however. He could not refrain from going on with them, but it seems to us that we may stop here.

Part II, chapter X

The repeated sentence

A phrase or image that comes back through a work and gathers meaning.

Twice two makes four returns through Part I as the figure of every closed system. The good and the beautiful comes back as mockery of Romantic idealism, and the wet snow falls at the end of Part I and all through Part II.

the whole work of man really seems to consist in nothing but proving to himself every minute that he is a man and not a piano-key!

Part I, chapter VIII

Allusion

A reference to another work, person or event that the reader is expected to recognise.

Chernyshevsky’s palace, Buckle’s History of Civilization, Diderot’s piano-keys, Heine on Rousseau’s lying confessions, Gogol’s Lieutenant Pirogov and his madman who thought he was King of Spain, Pushkin’s Silvio, Lermontov’s Masquerade, Byron’s Manfred, and Nekrasov’s poem about a fallen woman, which Part II quotes and then turns upside down.

Then the “Palace of Crystal” will be built. Then.... In fact, those will be halcyon days.

Part I, chapter VII
