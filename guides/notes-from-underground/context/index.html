Notes from Underground · Fyodor Dostoevsky

Context

Dates

1821
Fyodor Dostoevsky is born in Moscow The son of a doctor at the Mariinsky Hospital for the poor.
1845
Nekrasov’s poem When from dark error’s subjugation A poem about a fallen woman redeemed by a man’s words. Part II takes it as an epigraph and quotes its last lines again, when Liza arrives at the narrator’s door.
1849
Arrest, mock execution and Siberia Dostoevsky was arrested with the Petrashevsky circle, reprieved at the place of execution in December, and sent to hard labour.
1851
The Crystal Palace is built in Hyde Park Joseph Paxton’s iron and glass hall for the Great Exhibition; it was later moved to Sydenham in south London.
1862
Dostoevsky visits London On his first journey to Western Europe he saw the Crystal Palace and the International Exhibition, and wrote about them in the chapter called Baal in Winter Notes on Summer Impressions (1863).
1863
Chernyshevsky’s What Is to Be Done? Written in prison and published in Sovremennik: a novel of rational egoism in which the heroine’s fourth dream shows the socialist future in a palace of iron and glass.
1864
Notes from Underground in Epokha Part I in the double first issue, which appeared at the end of March, and Part II in the April issue. The censor cut the passages in Part I, chapter X, where Dostoevsky drew the need for faith and Christ.
1864
Two deaths Dostoevsky’s wife Maria died of consumption in April, in the months he was writing the book, and his brother Mikhail in July.
1866
Crime and Punishment Raskolnikov’s theory and its collapse take up many questions the underground man had raised.
1886
L’Esprit souterrain A French adaptation by Ely Halpérine-Kaminsky and Charles Morice, which joined Dostoevsky’s The Landlady to the second part of Notes from Underground.
1887
Nietzsche finds the book in Nice Picking up L’Esprit souterrain by chance in a bookshop, he wrote to Franz Overbeck in February that he had felt an immediate kinship with its author.
1918
Constance Garnett’s translation Printed after White Nights in the tenth volume of her Novels of Fyodor Dostoevsky.
1956
Kaufmann’s Existentialism from Dostoevsky to Sartre Walter Kaufmann’s anthology began its history of existentialism with Notes from Underground.

Places

Nevsky Prospect, St Petersburg
Where the narrator walks on holidays at four o’clock, stepping aside for everybody, until he collides with the officer.
Sennaya Square (the Haymarket), St Petersburg
The basements where he tells Liza she will end up, and where he says he saw a coffin carried out.
Volkovo Cemetery, St Petersburg
The waterlogged cemetery he describes to Liza, though he admits he had never been there.
Five Corners, St Petersburg
Where Anton Antonitch lives on the fourth floor and receives on Tuesdays.
Yusupov Garden, St Petersburg
One of the crowded evening streets and gardens he walks through, restless, before Liza comes.
Crystal Palace, Sydenham, London
The rebuilt glass palace Dostoevsky saw in 1862, model for the palace of crystal in Part I.
Riga
Liza’s home town, where the student who writes to her knew her as a child.

Critics

Walter Kaufmann

Existentialism from Dostoevsky to Sartre (1956)

Kaufmann opened his anthology with the first part of the book and called it the best overture for existentialism ever written.

Joseph Frank

Dostoevsky: The Stir of Liberation, 1860–1865 (1986)

Frank read the book as a parody of the rational egoism of Chernyshevsky’s What Is to Be Done?: the Underground Man lives out where those ideas lead, and his spite is their result, not Dostoevsky’s creed.
