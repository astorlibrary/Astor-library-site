Notes from Underground · Fyodor Dostoevsky

Characters

The Underground Man

The narrator: forty, a retired collegiate assessor living on a small legacy; first appears Part I, chapter I

Intelligent, well read, spiteful and acutely aware of all of it. At forty he writes that consciousness is a disease and argues that people will always choose freedom over their own advantage. At twenty-four he nursed grudges for years, dreamed of being a hero, forced himself on people who despised him and humiliated the one person who was kind to him. He contradicts himself on principle, admits to lying and then lies about the admission, and he is funnier than most readers expect. Dostoevsky’s footnote calls him a type who must exist in Russian society.

Liza

Twenty, from Riga, a fortnight in a Petersburg brothel; first appears Part II, chapter V

She says little at first and answers his questions curtly. Moved by his pictures of home, marriage and a lonely death, she breaks down, then shows him her one treasure, a respectful love letter from a student who knew her as a child in Riga. When she comes to his lodging she sees his poverty and his rage, understands better than he does that he is unhappy, and embraces him. She leaves his five roubles on the table. Many readers take her as the moral centre of the book.

Apollon

The narrator’s elderly servant, part-time tailor; first appears Part II, chapter III

Dignified, pedantic, vain of his lisp and of every button on his coat, he works for seven roubles a month and despises his master. He reads psalms aloud behind his partition, stares the narrator into submission over his wages and wins every battle. He arrives with Liza at the worst moment and fetches the tea. The narrator says he ended up reading psalms over the dead and killing rats.

Zverkov

An old schoolfellow, now an officer, leaving for a distant province; first appears Part II, chapter III

Handsome, lucky and stupid, according to the narrator, who hated him at school for being a pretty, playful boy everybody liked. He boasted of the village girls on his estate, and now he treats the uninvited guest with a general’s condescension. When the narrator tries to apologise, Zverkov tells him that he could never insult him, and goes off to the brothel.

Simonov

A schoolfellow and the narrator’s last acquaintance from school; first appears Part II, chapter II

Quiet, honest and not stupid, he tolerates the narrator’s visits and is plainly uncomfortable with them. He arranges the dinner, forgets to tell the narrator the time has changed, and at the end of the evening flings six roubles at him with contempt. The narrator repays him the next day with a letter of excuses he is very proud of.

Ferfitchkin

A schoolfellow, Zverkov’s toady; first appears Part II, chapter III

A Russianised German with the face of a monkey, a bitter enemy of the narrator since the lower forms, who sniggers at every humiliation and borrows money from Zverkov. The narrator challenges him to a duel at the dinner, to general laughter.

Trudolyubov

A schoolfellow in the army, Zverkov’s distant relation; first appears Part II, chapter III

Tall, cold and fairly honest, he worships success and thinks of nothing but promotion. He takes the narrator’s side for a moment when the dinner hour is changed, and then orders him to stop spoiling the evening.

The officer

A six-foot officer from a tavern billiard room; first appears Part II, chapter I

He moves the narrator out of his way by the shoulders without a word and without noticing him, which the narrator finds worse than a blow. For two years the narrator follows him, learns his name, writes a story about him and drafts a challenge, and at last collides with him on the Nevsky. The officer does not look round.

Anton Antonitch Syetotchkin

The narrator’s superior at the office; first appears Part II, chapter I

Grave, judicious and unassuming, he never lends money to anyone but twice lends to the narrator, against his salary. He receives on Tuesdays, with two giggling daughters and their aunt pouring tea, and talks of salaries and promotions. The narrator calls him the only permanent acquaintance of his life.
