Anne of Green Gables · L. M. Montgomery

Form and language

Free indirect style

Third-person narration that slips into a character’s own idiom without announcing that it is doing so.

The narration shifts into Marilla’s vocabulary at the moments she would never speak aloud (worrying about what Mrs Rachel will say, admitting to herself that the house has changed), and so a character who keeps her feelings to herself becomes the book’s second centre.

not even a brook could run past Mrs. Rachel Lynde’s door without due regard for decency and decorum

Chapter 1

Idiolect

Giving each character a distinct and consistent way of speaking.

Anne speaks in paragraphs, with subordinate clauses and italics; Matthew begins almost every sentence with ‘Well now’; Marilla answers in one flat line. A page of dialogue needs almost no attribution.

I shall call it⁠—let me see⁠—the Lake of Shining Waters. Yes, that is the right name for it. I know because of the thrill.

Anne Shirley, Chapter 2

Narratorial irony

A narrator whose tone judges what it reports without saying so.

The first page describes a brook that grows well-conducted as it passes Mrs Rachel Lynde’s window: the village’s power over its inhabitants, stated as a fact about hydrology before a single person has spoken.

not even a brook could run past Mrs. Rachel Lynde’s door without due regard for decency and decorum

Chapter 1

Allusion

Quotation or reference used to enlarge, or to puncture, the scene at hand.

Anne stages Tennyson’s Elaine in a leaky flat and nearly drowns, offers to recite “The Dog at His Master’s Grave” at Sunday school, and closes the novel with a line of Browning: her reading is always trying to make Avonlea into something more like a poem.

I can’t. I’m in the depths of despair. Can you eat when you are in the depths of despair?

Anne Shirley, Chapter 3

The comic set piece

A self-contained episode built to a single disaster, complete in one chapter.

The liniment cake, the currant wine, the dyed hair and the ridgepole each occupy a chapter, end in humiliation, and leave one permanent change behind: a new ally, a lesson, or a piece of Avonlea folklore.

It would be so much easier to be good if one’s hair was a handsome auburn, don’t you think?

Anne Shirley, Chapter 10

The year as structure

Organising the story’s time by the seasons and recurring village events.

Blossom, harvest, the Sunday-school picnic, the winter concert and the examination timetable mark the passage of five years; chapters end with weather as often as with incident.
