Belonging

Anne arrives as a clerical error and spends the first six chapters not knowing whether she will be sent back, and the novel never lets the reader forget the alternative: Mrs Blewett’s household, where a child would be a pair of hands. Belonging here is decided by adults in kitchens, and it has to be decided again each time Anne does something disastrous.

Through the book A mistake in Chapter 3, a decision in Chapter 6, a conditional welcome for twenty chapters, and finally Marilla saying it aloud when the household has nothing else left.

In the text

I can’t. I’m in the depths of despair. Can you eat when you are in the depths of despair?

Anne Shirley, Chapter 3

The phrase is borrowed from books and used with complete sincerity, and the question that follows is a real one: she wants to know. Marilla’s flat reply, that she has never been in the depths of despair, sets the two registers of the novel against each other in three lines of dialogue.

A bosom friend⁠—an intimate friend, you know⁠—a really kindred spirit to whom I can confide my inmost soul.

Anne Shirley, Chapter 8

The definition arrives in three stages, each one more literary than the last, and the escalation is the joke. But the sentence also names the novel’s scarcest commodity: Anne has had households, matrons and other people’s twins, and has never once had a person of her own choosing.

Imagination

Anne’s imagination is a way of living inside material limits: nothing else she owns can make a pond into the Lake of Shining Waters. The book is unusual in insisting that the same faculty is dangerous: it produces the Haunted Wood she cannot walk through, the hair dye she believes in and the Elaine who nearly drowns.

Through the book A defence against the asylum in the early chapters, a social gift by the middle of the book, and by Queen’s a thing she can switch off long enough to pass an examination.

In the text

I asked her to go into the ladies’ waiting room, but she informed me gravely that she preferred to stay outside. ‘There was more scope for imagination,’ she said. She’s a case, I should say.

The station master, Chapter 2

Anne’s defining phrase reaches the reader at second hand, inside a railwayman’s report, with quotation marks around it as though it were evidence. ‘She’s a case’ is the verdict Avonlea will keep offering for thirty chapters, and the novel’s method is to let her phrase outlive the judgement.

It would be so much easier to be good if one’s hair was a handsome auburn, don’t you think?

Anne Shirley, Chapter 10

The apology has just been performed with theatrical relish, and this is the sentence that follows it: goodness treated as a matter of appearance, offered to the woman whose remark about her hair caused the quarrel. Montgomery lets Anne win Mrs Rachel over in the same speech in which she reveals she has learned nothing about vanity.

Names

On the drive from the station Anne renames the neighbourhood, and her first act at Green Gables is to negotiate the spelling of her own name. For a child whose clothes, placements and prospects have all been decided elsewhere, naming is the one power she has, and Montgomery treats it as a serious claim.

Through the book Cordelia and the e in Chapter 3, the Lake of Shining Waters and the White Way of Delight on the drive, and by the last chapter the names have stuck: the narrator walks Anne down to the Lake of Shining Waters as if it were on the map.

In the text

But if you call me Anne please call me Anne spelled with an e.

Anne Shirley, Chapter 3

The sentence concedes and demands in the same breath: she gives up Cordelia and instantly opens a second negotiation. A child with no say over where she sleeps is bargaining over orthography, which is the only territory available to her, and the request is granted: Marilla uses the formula back at her twice in the same scene.

“There’s a great deal of difference between being called a crow and being called carrots,” said Anne with dignity. “Gilbert Blythe has hurt my feelings excruciatingly, Diana.”

Anne Shirley, Chapter 15

‘With dignity’ is the narrator’s small betrayal of a girl who has just broken a slate over a boy’s head, and ‘excruciatingly’ is Anne restoring the scale of the injury by vocabulary alone. The distinction she is defending is real to her and invisible to everyone else, which is the shape of most of her quarrels.

Orphans, hired help and respectable farms

The novel is exact about money and position without saying so: a boy from an asylum is labour, a girl is a liability, Mrs Blewett wants someone to mind her children, and the Cuthberts’ respectability makes Green Gables a different proposition. The bank failure in the last chapters shows how narrow that margin of respectability actually is.

Through the book An asylum and a farm in Chapter 6, hand-me-down wincey dresses against puffed sleeves in Chapter 25, and a scholarship given up in Chapter 38 so that Green Gables need not be sold.

In the text

not even a brook could run past Mrs. Rachel Lynde’s door without due regard for decency and decorum

Chapter 1

A brook that is headlong and full of dark secrets in the woods becomes quiet and well-conducted when it reaches a particular window: the village’s disciplinary power is established as a fact of the landscape before any character speaks. The alliteration of ‘decency and decorum’ is Mrs Rachel’s own phrasing, lent to the narrator a paragraph before she is introduced.

There are so many things in this room and all so splendid that there is no scope for imagination. That is one consolation when you are poor⁠—there are so many more things you can imagine about.

Anne Shirley, Chapter 29

The novel’s economics stated in two sentences, and by the person they apply to: imagination is described as a compensation for not having things. It is also a considerable piece of tact, delivered in a velvet-carpeted room belonging to a rich woman who is listening.

School, scholarship and ambition

Ambition in this book is specific and measurable (a pass list in the paper, a First Class licence, the Avery scholarship, a B.A.), and it belongs to a girl in a farming village in the 1890s. Miss Stacy does not soften it; she organises it, and the rivalry with Gilbert is the engine.

Through the book A broken slate in Chapter 15, a class organised in Chapter 30, a tie at the head of the pass list, and then an ambition that has to be recalculated and is not given up.

In the text

“Carrots! Carrots!”

Gilbert Blythe, Chapter 15

Two words, on a line of their own, set off five years of plot. The word is an insult about poverty as much as about colour (red hair is the one feature of herself Anne has never been able to reimagine), and it arrives from the only pupil in Avonlea who is her intellectual equal.

I’m not a bit changed⁠—not really. I’m only just pruned down and branched out.

Anne Shirley, Chapter 34

The metaphor is taken from the orchard outside the window, which is the language Marilla can actually hear: Anne has learned to translate. It also concedes more than it admits: pruning is a loss of growth carried out deliberately, and the last chapters will require exactly that.

Grief and sacrifice

The last two chapters withdraw almost everything the book has been building: Matthew dies, Marilla’s sight is failing, and Anne gives up the scholarship she has won. Montgomery gives her neither the tragic ending nor the consolation prize, and has Anne argue that a narrowed road is not a closed one.

Through the book A bank failure and a death in Chapter 37, a decision in Chapter 38, and a last paragraph in which the horizon has come in and the happiness is described as quiet.

In the text

“Well now, I’d rather have you than a dozen boys, Anne,” said Matthew patting her hand. “Just mind you that⁠—rather than a dozen boys.

Matthew Cuthbert, Chapter 36

The sentence answers the clerical error of Chapter 1 directly, and Matthew repeats it because he knows he is bad at saying things. Montgomery places it the evening before his death, so that the novel’s one explicit declaration of love is also the last thing he manages to do.

When I left Queen’s my future seemed to stretch out before me like a straight road. I thought I could see along it for many a milestone. Now there is a bend in it. I don’t know what lies around the bend, but I’m going to believe that the best does.

Anne Shirley, Chapter 38

The image is the opposite of the one she began with: a road she cannot see along is now preferable to a road she can. Note the verb, ‘I’m going to believe’, which makes optimism an act of will, and answers everyone who has called her a case.
