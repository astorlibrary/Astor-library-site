Anne of Green Gables · L. M. Montgomery

Themes

Belonging

Anne arrives as a clerical error and spends the first six chapters not knowing whether she will be sent back, and the novel never lets the reader forget the alternative: Mrs Blewett’s household, where a child would be a pair of hands. Belonging here is decided by adults in kitchens, and it has to be decided again each time Anne does something disastrous.

A mistake in Chapter 3, a decision in Chapter 6, a conditional welcome for twenty chapters, and finally Marilla saying it aloud when the household has nothing else left.

I asked her to go into the ladies’ waiting room, but she informed me gravely that she preferred to stay outside. ‘There was more scope for imagination,’ she said. She’s a case, I should say.

The station master, Chapter 2

But if you call me Anne please call me Anne spelled with an e.

Anne Shirley, Chapter 3

Imagination

Anne’s imagination is a way of living inside material limits: nothing else she owns can make a pond into the Lake of Shining Waters. The book is unusual in insisting that the same faculty is dangerous: it produces the Haunted Wood she cannot walk through, the hair dye she believes in and the Elaine who nearly drowns.

A defence against the asylum in the early chapters, a social gift by the middle of the book, and by Queen’s a thing she can switch off long enough to pass an examination.

I asked her to go into the ladies’ waiting room, but she informed me gravely that she preferred to stay outside. ‘There was more scope for imagination,’ she said. She’s a case, I should say.

The station master, Chapter 2

I shall call it⁠—let me see⁠—the Lake of Shining Waters. Yes, that is the right name for it. I know because of the thrill.

Anne Shirley, Chapter 2

Names

On the drive from the station Anne renames the neighbourhood, and her first act at Green Gables is to negotiate the spelling of her own name. For a child whose clothes, placements and prospects have all been decided elsewhere, naming is the one power she has, and Montgomery treats it as a serious claim.

Cordelia and the e in Chapter 3, the Lake of Shining Waters and the White Way of Delight on the drive, and by the last chapter the names have stuck: the narrator walks Anne down to the Lake of Shining Waters as if it were on the map.

I shall call it⁠—let me see⁠—the Lake of Shining Waters. Yes, that is the right name for it. I know because of the thrill.

Anne Shirley, Chapter 2

But if you call me Anne please call me Anne spelled with an e.

Anne Shirley, Chapter 3

Orphans, hired help and respectable farms

The novel is exact about money and position without saying so: a boy from an asylum is labour, a girl is a liability, Mrs Blewett wants someone to mind her children, and the Cuthberts’ respectability makes Green Gables a different proposition. The bank failure in the last chapters shows how narrow that margin of respectability actually is.

An asylum and a farm in Chapter 6, hand-me-down wincey dresses against puffed sleeves in Chapter 25, and a scholarship given up in Chapter 38 so that Green Gables need not be sold.

not even a brook could run past Mrs. Rachel Lynde’s door without due regard for decency and decorum

Chapter 1

“We might be some good to her,” said Matthew suddenly and unexpectedly.

Matthew Cuthbert, Chapter 3

School, scholarship and ambition

Ambition in this book is specific and measurable (a pass list in the paper, a First Class licence, the Avery scholarship, a B.A.), and it belongs to a girl in a farming village in the 1890s. Miss Stacy does not soften it; she organises it, and the rivalry with Gilbert is the engine.

A broken slate in Chapter 15, a class organised in Chapter 30, a tie at the head of the pass list, and then an ambition that has to be recalculated and is not given up.

“Carrots! Carrots!”

Gilbert Blythe, Chapter 15

“There’s a great deal of difference between being called a crow and being called carrots,” said Anne with dignity. “Gilbert Blythe has hurt my feelings excruciatingly, Diana.”

Anne Shirley, Chapter 15

Grief and sacrifice

The last two chapters withdraw almost everything the book has been building: Matthew dies, Marilla’s sight is failing, and Anne gives up the scholarship she has won. Montgomery gives her neither the tragic ending nor the consolation prize, and has Anne argue that a narrowed road is not a closed one.

A bank failure and a death in Chapter 37, a decision in Chapter 38, and a last paragraph in which the horizon has come in and the happiness is described as quiet.

“Well now, I’d rather have you than a dozen boys, Anne,” said Matthew patting her hand. “Just mind you that⁠—rather than a dozen boys.

Matthew Cuthbert, Chapter 36

It’s never been easy for me to say things out of my heart, but at times like this it’s easier. I love you as dear as if you were my own flesh and blood and you’ve been my joy and comfort ever since you came to Green Gables.

Marilla Cuthbert, Chapter 37
