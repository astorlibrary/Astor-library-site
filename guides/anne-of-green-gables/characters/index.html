Anne of Green Gables · L. M. Montgomery

Characters

Anne Shirley

An eleven-year-old sent by mistake to Green Gables; first appears Chapter 2

She arrives with a carpet-bag, a vocabulary out of borrowed books and a history of minding other people’s babies, and her talk is the only property she has ever been allowed to keep. The novel is careful never to cure her of imagination, only to teach her when it clarifies a thing and when it hides one that needs attention.

Marilla Cuthbert

Mistress of Green Gables; first appears Chapter 1

Practical, sarcastic and frightened of her own affections, she is the character the novel actually changes. Montgomery gives her the funniest dry lines and then, in the last two chapters, the admission that she has never found it easy to say anything out of her heart, and so the saying of it lands hard.

Matthew Cuthbert

Marilla’s brother, a farmer afraid of women; first appears Chapter 1

He is frightened of everyone except Anne and says almost nothing to anybody, which makes the four or five sentences he does say the emotional pivots of the book. His campaign to buy a dress with puffed sleeves is comedy; his remark about a dozen boys is the reason the last chapters work.

Diana Barry

Anne’s bosom friend; first appears Chapter 12

Pretty, good-natured and entirely without imagination, she is the ordinary against which Anne is measured and the first person ever to want Anne’s company for its own sake. The friendship survives being forbidden, which is more than most of the novel’s adult relationships are asked to do.

Gilbert Blythe

The best scholar in Avonlea school; first appears Chapter 15

He calls Anne carrots in Chapter 15 and spends the rest of the novel paying for it, first with a broken slate and then with five years of refused apologies. Montgomery keeps him mostly offstage, so the rivalry spurs Anne’s ambition and does not become a romance.

Mrs Rachel Lynde

Avonlea’s chief authority on everybody; first appears Chapter 1

She runs the Sewing Circle, the Church Aid Society and, in practice, the village’s opinion of itself, and the book opens by describing a brook that behaves itself as it passes her window. Her quarrel with Anne over red hair and freckles is the first test of whether Avonlea will treat a child as a character or a case.

Miss Stacy

The new teacher at Avonlea school; first appears Chapter 24

She takes the pupils into the woods, gets up concerts, and organises the Queen’s class that gives Anne a route out of the village. She is the book’s picture of what a good teacher does: she gives ambition an examination timetable.

Miss Josephine Barry

Diana’s wealthy great-aunt from Charlottetown; first appears Chapter 19

Two children jump into her bed in the dark and she becomes, improbably, one of Anne’s allies, the first adult outside Green Gables to be won over by talk. She provides the tea-parties, the concert tickets and the second home in town that the last chapters need.
