Gulliver’s Travels · Jonathan Swift

What happens

Prefatory documents The publisher’s note and Gulliver’s letter

Before the first voyage the reader is given a publisher’s note vouching for Gulliver’s character and a furious letter from Gulliver complaining that his cousin Sympson pressed him into publishing and then let the text be tampered with. The satire is under way before the travels begin: the apparatus that guarantees a true account is the first thing shown to be unreliable.

The Publisher to the Reader
A cousin certifies Gulliver’s plain style, his reputation for truth in Redriff, and the cuts he has made to the manuscript.
A Letter from Captain Gulliver to His Cousin Sympson
Gulliver complains of interpolations, of the failure of his book to reform anybody in six months, and of the sea Yahoos who fault his sea-language.

Part I Lilliput

Shipwrecked and pegged to the ground by people a twelfth his size, Gulliver is fed, inventoried, made a Nardac and used as a naval weapon. Court office is won by rope-dancing, national identity rests on which end of an egg is broken, and the reward for putting out a palace fire is a charge of high treason. He leaves before his eyes can be put out.

Part I, Chapters I–II
The shore, the cords, the first meal, and an official inventory of his pockets in which a watch is filed as a possible god.
Part I, Chapters III–IV
Preferment by acrobatics; Reldresal explains the Tramecksan and Slamecksan, and the war with Blefuscu over the larger end of an egg.
Part I, Chapters V–VI
Gulliver hauls away the enemy navy, refuses to help enslave Blefuscu, and extinguishes a fire in the empress’s apartment in the most efficient available way.
Part I, Chapters VII–VIII
A friend reads him the charges and the merciful sentence of blinding; he walks to Blefuscu and sails home in a real boat.

Part II Brobdingnag

Left on a beach where the corn is forty feet high, Gulliver becomes a farm curiosity, then a queen’s pet, then a lecturer on England to a king. Everything he is proud of in England (the constitution, the law, finance, gunpowder) is laid before a listener who neither flatters him nor interrupts, and who is appalled when he has finished.

Part II, Chapters I–II
A giant reaper picks him up; the farmer exhibits him in market towns until he is nearly worked to death, cared for by the daughter he calls Glumdalclitch.
Part II, Chapters III–IV
The court scholars classify him as a freak of nature, and he begins to see European faces as skin and pores.
Part II, Chapters V–VI
Wasps, apples, a monkey, a dwarf and the maids of honour: the body is the subject of the middle chapters.
Part II, Chapters VII–VIII
Gulliver offers the recipe for artillery as a gift and is refused with horror; an eagle carries his box out to sea and he is picked up by an English ship.

Part III Laputa, Balnibarbi and the Struldbrugs

The loosest and most various of the voyages: a flying island whose philosophers must be struck with bladders to attend to anything, a ruined country improved into famine by its academy, a governor who can raise the dead, and the Struldbrugs, who are immortal but not young. The target throughout is reason cut off from ordinary life.

Part III, Chapters I–III
Pirates, then Laputa: music, mathematics, terror of comets, and a king who suppresses rebellion by lowering the island onto the town.
Part III, Chapters IV–VI
Munodi’s prosperous estate is a scandal to his neighbours; the projectors extract sunbeams from cucumbers and build houses from the roof down.
Part III, Chapters VII–VIII
The dead are summoned and modern history is corrected; the honours of the last century turn out to have been bought.
Part III, Chapters IX–XI
Gulliver plans a glorious immortal life, then meets people who have had one for two hundred years.

Part IV The land of the Houyhnhnms

Set ashore by his own mutinous crew, Gulliver finds rational horses and filthy human animals, and spends the voyage explaining England to a master who has no word for falsehood. The account convinces him: he learns to speak in whinnies, is expelled by an assembly that cannot see why a Yahoo should be kept, and comes home unable to be in a room with his family.

Part IV, Chapters I–III
Mutiny, the shore, the Yahoos, and the slow acquisition of a language with no term for saying the thing which is not.
Part IV, Chapters IV–VII
War, law, money, medicine and ministers, set out plainly and heard by someone with no reason to make allowances.
Part IV, Chapters VIII–X
Houyhnhnm virtue, education and marriage; Gulliver keeps house, imitates their gait and voice, and calls these the happiest years of his life.
Part IV, Chapters XI–XII
The assembly exhorts his master to send him away; Don Pedro’s kindness is unbearable, and Gulliver ends among horses in his own stable.
