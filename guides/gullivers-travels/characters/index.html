Gulliver’s Travels · Jonathan Swift

Characters

Lemuel Gulliver

Ship’s surgeon, then captain, and the book’s narrator; first appears Part I, Chapter I

A competent, incurious man who measures everything and understands almost nothing, and whose plainness makes the satire work. He is not a fixed point the reader can stand on: each voyage adjusts what he admires, and by the fourth he faints when his wife kisses him.

The Emperor of Lilliput

Sovereign of a country six inches high; first appears Part I, Chapter II

Taller than his courtiers by the breadth of a fingernail, and described in the vocabulary of universal monarchy. He is generous while Gulliver is useful and merciful in the technical sense afterwards: the sentence of blinding is presented at court as an act of clemency.

The King of Brobdingnag

A ruler who asks questions and waits for the answers; first appears Part II, Chapter III

Swift’s one straightforwardly admirable figure, and a deliberately limited one: he is learned, he keeps a small army, and he cannot conceive of a state secret worth a massacre. His verdict on English history is delivered after several audiences of careful notes, and so it is hard to dismiss.

Glumdalclitch

The farmer’s daughter, nine years old and forty feet tall; first appears Part II, Chapter II

She names him, sews his shirts, teaches him the language and is the only person in four voyages who loves him. Swift lets her stand outside the satire entirely, and when an eagle carries his box out to sea, Gulliver’s first thought is of her grief.

Lord Munodi

A nobleman of Balnibarbi with a working estate; first appears Part III, Chapter IV

His fields are full, his house stands up and his neighbours despise him for it, because his methods are the old ones. Munodi keeps the Academy funny as well as grotesque: the country has failed because ingenuity has been put in charge of farming, and it never lacked ingenuity.

The Struldbrugs

The immortals of Luggnagg; first appears Part III, Chapter X

Born with a spot over the eyebrow, they never die and they also never stop ageing. Swift uses them to separate two wishes that are usually confused, and the chapter’s real cruelty is that Gulliver delivers his rapturous plan for an immortal life before he is taken to see any of them.

Gulliver’s Houyhnhnm master

The horse who teaches him the language and hears his account of England; first appears Part IV, Chapter I

Patient, literal and entirely without a concept of deception, he can only call a lie “the thing which was not”, and Gulliver has to explain a lawsuit to him from first principles. His difficulty in believing the account is the engine of Part IV: everything sounds worse when explained to someone who cannot supply the usual excuses.

The Yahoos

The human animals of Houyhnhnmland; first appears Part IV, Chapter I

Filthy, greedy, tribal and recognisably human, they are the satire’s hardest device because Gulliver accepts the identification completely and the book does not obviously stop him. They hoard coloured stones they cannot use, which is the one detail that makes the resemblance impossible to argue with.

Don Pedro de Mendez

The Portuguese captain who takes Gulliver home; first appears Part IV, Chapter XI

He has a raving man brought aboard for his own safety, clothes him, takes him into his own house at Lisbon and asks nothing in return, and Gulliver can barely stand to breathe near him. Swift plants an entirely good human being in the last chapters, and lets his narrator fail to notice, which is the strongest evidence that Swift does not share Gulliver’s final opinion of mankind.
