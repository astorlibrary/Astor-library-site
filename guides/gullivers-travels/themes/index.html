Gulliver’s Travels · Jonathan Swift

Themes

Scale and perspective

Size is an argument here. At twelve to one Gulliver finds Lilliputian politics comic; at one to twelve he finds Brobdingnagian skin disgusting and hears the king condemn English politics. The same facts about Europe are funny in Part I and monstrous in Part II, and only the vantage point has moved.

Giant, then miniature, then a tourist at the ordinary human scale in Part III, and finally an animal being classified by someone else in Part IV.

they shouted for joy, and danced upon my breast, repeating several times as they did at first, “Hekinah degul.”

Part I, Chapter I

I cannot but conclude the bulk of your natives to be the most pernicious race of little odious vermin that nature ever suffered to crawl upon the surface of the earth.”

Part II, Chapter VI

Pride

The book ends on pride: in his last paragraph Gulliver can put up with a lawyer, a pickpocket or a politician, but not a Yahoo smitten with pride. The target is the claim to be a reasoning creature, and the Houyhnhnm master offers a humbler description: animals to whose share some small pittance of reason has fallen.

National pride in Part I, personal vanity in Part II, intellectual pride in Part III, and species pride in Part IV, where Gulliver catches it himself in the act of despising Yahoos.

I do, in the next place, complain of my own great want of judgment, in being prevailed upon by the entreaties and false reasoning of you and some others, very much against my own opinion, to suffer my travels to be published.

Part II, Chapter VI

Reason and its uses

The Laputans calculate beautifully and cannot build a house that stands; the Academy improves agriculture into famine; the perfectly rational Houyhnhnms debate in their general assembly whether to exterminate the Yahoos. Reason cut off from use, and reason cut off from mercy, are both examined, and neither is spared.

Munodi’s working farm sets the standard in Part III; the general assembly in Part IV shows what the standard costs when applied without feeling.

we conjecture it is either some unknown animal, or the god that he worships; but we are more inclined to the latter opinion

Party, office and faction

High heels and low, big ends and little: Swift reduces confessional and party conflict to distinctions with no content and then counts the dead. Office in Lilliput goes to the best rope-dancer, and the coloured silks to whoever leaps over or creeps under the emperor’s stick most nimbly: a joke about preferment that explains a good deal of it.

Local and topical in Part I, generalised into a European history of war in Part II, and restated to a creature with no politics at all in Part IV, Chapter V.

It is allowed on all hands, that the primitive way of breaking eggs, before we eat them, was upon the larger end

Part I, Chapter IV

his majesty has determined to make use only of low heels in the administration of the government

Part I, Chapter IV

Travel, discovery and possession

Gulliver measures, classifies and reports every people he meets, which is exactly what the voyage literature of the period did. The last chapter turns the habit inside out by explaining how a new country is normally taken, and then exempting his own discoveries on the grounds that they are not worth the trouble.

The Lilliputian inventory of his pockets in Part I is the first survey; his refusal to help enslave Blefuscu is the first refusal; Part IV, Chapter XII states the case plainly.

Part I, Chapter I

Bodies and disgust

Swift will not let the reader forget that a human being is a body that eats, sweats and smells. Gulliver’s excretions are a practical problem in Lilliput, the giant nurses are unbearable at close range, and home from Part IV he finds the very smell of his wife and children intolerable and talks to his horses four hours a day.

Comic in Part I, magnified and clinical in Part II, and finally moralised in Part IV, where disgust at the body becomes disgust at the species.

They were the most mortifying sight I ever beheld; and the women more horrible than the men.

Part III, Chapter X

when I behold a lump of deformity and diseases, both in body and mind, smitten with pride, it immediately breaks all the measures of my patience
