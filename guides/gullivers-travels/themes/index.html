Scale and perspective

Size is an argument here. At twelve to one Gulliver finds Lilliputian politics comic; at one to twelve he finds Brobdingnagian skin disgusting and hears the king condemn English politics. The same facts about Europe are funny in Part I and monstrous in Part II, and only the vantage point has moved.

Through the book Giant, then miniature, then a tourist at the ordinary human scale in Part III, and finally an animal being classified by someone else in Part IV.

In the text

they shouted for joy, and danced upon my breast, repeating several times as they did at first, “Hekinah degul.”

Part I, Chapter I

The foreign words are left untranslated, so the reader is put exactly where Gulliver is: watching a ceremony and guessing at its meaning. Swift also gets the scale into the verb (they dance on his chest), which makes the wonder physical before it is political.

I cannot but conclude the bulk of your natives to be the most pernicious race of little odious vermin that nature ever suffered to crawl upon the surface of the earth.”

Part II, Chapter VI

The sentence is built to sound judicial: he has recapitulated, compared question with answer, and only then delivers. The king is stroking Gulliver as he gives the verdict, so contempt and gentleness arrive in the same gesture.

Pride

The book ends on pride: in his last paragraph Gulliver can put up with a lawyer, a pickpocket or a politician, but not a Yahoo smitten with pride. The target is the claim to be a reasoning creature, and the Houyhnhnm master offers a humbler description: animals to whose share some small pittance of reason has fallen.

Through the book National pride in Part I, personal vanity in Part II, intellectual pride in Part III, and species pride in Part IV, where Gulliver catches it himself in the act of despising Yahoos.

In the text

The king was struck with horror at the description I had given of those terrible engines, and the proposal I had made.

Part II, Chapter VII

The reversal is complete and Gulliver misses it entirely: he files the refusal under the king’s narrow principles and nice scruples. Swift lets the reader hold two readings of the same scene, and the satire lives in the gap between them.

“Happy nation, where every child hath at least a chance for being immortal! Happy people, who enjoy so many living examples of ancient virtue, and have masters ready to instruct them in the wisdom of all former ages!

Part III, Chapter X

Swift makes him deliver the whole eloquent programme (wealth, learning, a record of the ages) and only then takes him to see the Struldbrugs. The chapter separates two things the wish for immortality usually keeps welded together, and Gulliver has already committed himself in public before the distinction arrives.

Reason and its uses

The Laputans calculate beautifully and cannot build a house that stands; the Academy improves agriculture into famine; the perfectly rational Houyhnhnms debate in their general assembly whether to exterminate the Yahoos. Reason cut off from use, and reason cut off from mercy, are both examined, and neither is spared.

Through the book Munodi’s working farm sets the standard in Part III; the general assembly in Part IV shows what the standard costs when applied without feeling.

In the text

whoever could make two ears of corn, or two blades of grass, to grow upon a spot of ground where only one grew before, would deserve better of mankind, and do more essential service to his country, than the whole race of politicians put together.”

Part II, Chapter VII

The standard offered here is the one the whole book measures learning against, and it is agricultural: does it feed anybody? Part III is built to fail this test, which is why the Academy of Lagado comes a hundred pages after this sentence.

He has been eight years upon a project for extracting sunbeams out of cucumbers, which were to be put in phials hermetically sealed, and let out to warm the air in raw inclement summers.

Part III, Chapter V

In principle the project is a reasonable one, a scheme to store summer heat, which is why it is dangerous: the man is eight years in and asking for funds. Swift attacks the institution that pays for eight years of curiosity while the country outside has stopped growing food.

Party, office and faction

High heels and low, big ends and little: Swift reduces confessional and party conflict to distinctions with no content and then counts the dead. Office in Lilliput goes to the best rope-dancer, and the coloured silks to whoever leaps over or creeps under the emperor’s stick most nimbly: a joke about preferment that explains a good deal of it.

Through the book Local and topical in Part I, generalised into a European history of war in Part II, and restated to a creature with no politics at all in Part IV, Chapter V.

In the text

It is allowed on all hands, that the primitive way of breaking eggs, before we eat them, was upon the larger end

Part I, Chapter IV

The phrase ‘allowed on all hands’ is the vocabulary of settled doctrine applied to breakfast. Swift then supplies casualty figures, eleven thousand who chose death over submission, so the cause stays trivial while the consequences are at full historical size.

in recounting the numbers of those who have been killed in battle, I cannot but think you have said the thing which is not.”

Part IV, Chapter V

The Houyhnhnm phrase for lying is used, for once, against Gulliver, and it is used because the truth is too large to be credible. Swift gets the sharpest effect available to satire here: the honest report of Europe is indistinguishable, to a reasonable listener, from an invention.

Travel, discovery and possession

Gulliver measures, classifies and reports every people he meets, which is what the voyage literature of the period did. The last chapter turns the habit inside out by explaining how a new country is normally taken, and then exempting his own discoveries on the grounds that they are not worth the trouble.

Through the book The Lilliputian inventory of his pockets in Part I is the first survey; his refusal to help enslave Blefuscu is the first refusal; Part IV, Chapter XII states the case plainly.

In the text

we conjecture it is either some unknown animal, or the god that he worships; but we are more inclined to the latter opinion

Part I, Chapter II

The officers reason correctly from what they can observe: the object is consulted before every action, so it governs him. Swift’s joke about a nation that worships the clock is delivered entirely inside a customs document, and the form of the passage, a numbered search of a man’s body, is as pointed as its content.

wherein I have not been so studious of ornament as of truth.

Part IV, Chapter XII

The claim to plainness is the oldest guarantee in travel writing and Swift has spent four voyages making it worthless. Gulliver closes the account with the same promise Sympson opened it with, and by now the reader has seen what his plain reporting can be made to carry.

Bodies and disgust

Swift will not let the reader forget that a human being is a body that eats, sweats and smells. Gulliver’s excretions are a practical problem in Lilliput, the giant nurses are unbearable at close range, and home from Part IV he finds the very smell of his wife and children intolerable and talks to his horses four hours a day.

Through the book Comic in Part I, magnified and clinical in Part II, and finally moralised in Part IV, where disgust at the body becomes disgust at the species.

In the text

They were the most mortifying sight I ever beheld; and the women more horrible than the men.

Part III, Chapter X

The correction is flat and brief after the rapture, and the second clause, that the women are worse, is the period’s ordinary misogyny surfacing in a passage about the horror of not dying. Swift’s satire and Swift’s prejudices are in the same sentence, and an honest reading has to hold both.

when I behold a lump of deformity and diseases, both in body and mind, smitten with pride, it immediately breaks all the measures of my patience

Part IV, Chapter XII

He will forgive humanity its natural vices and not its pride, which is the very vice he is displaying while he says so, and Swift has him admit as much a few lines earlier. The book ends by catching its narrator in the fault the book exists to attack.
