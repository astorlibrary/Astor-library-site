As You Like It · William Shakespeare

Characters

Rosalind

Daughter of the banished duke; in the forest, Ganymede; first appears 1.2

She has more lines than any other woman in Shakespeare and uses most of them to conduct her own courtship from behind a disguise. The wit is not decoration: playing Ganymede playing Rosalind lets her ask Orlando the questions a betrothed woman could not, and hear the answers before she commits.

Orlando

Youngest son of Sir Rowland de Boys; first appears 1.1

Kept unschooled by his brother and still, in his own account, unable to explain why. He is brave, courteous and a truly terrible poet, and the play is fond of him without pretending he is clever; his best moment is drawing a sword on strangers to get food for an old servant.

Celia

Duke Frederick’s daughter; in the forest, Aliena; first appears 1.2

She gives up a dukedom for a cousin without a moment’s discussion, which is the most decisive act anyone performs in the play. As the disguise takes over she is edged out of the dialogue, and her dry interruptions in Acts III and IV are the closest the play comes to noticing the cost.

Touchstone

The court fool, who follows the cousins into exile; first appears 1.2

He is the first character to say plainly that the forest is cold and the food is poor, and he spends the play testing pastoral sentiment against what things actually cost. His marriage to Audrey is the one in the final scene that nobody pretends will last.

Jaques

A lord attending the banished duke; first appears 2.5

He has attached himself to the exiles in order to have something to be sad about, and he is the only person in the play who is not in love, in service or in danger. The seven ages speech is superb and immediately disproved on stage by the entrance of an old man being carried by a young one.

Duke Senior

The banished duke, Rosalind’s father; first appears 2.1

He runs a court in the woods with all the ceremony and none of the power, and his first speech is an argument that the cold is honest. Whether this is philosophy or a way of making exile bearable, the play does not say, and Jaques is there to keep the question open.

Duke Frederick

The usurping duke, Celia’s father; first appears 1.2

He banishes his niece because she is well liked, which is the only reason he gives and probably the true one. He never sets foot in Arden: the play sends him to its edge, has him meet a religious man, and converts him offstage in a single reported sentence.

Oliver

Orlando’s eldest brother; first appears 1.1

He admits in his first scene that he hates his brother for being better liked and cannot say why. His rescue from a lioness and his conversion happen offstage and he reports them himself, which asks a good deal of an audience, the kind of demand the last act makes again and again.

Corin

An old shepherd; first appears 2.4

He is a working shepherd, and he answers a courtier’s philosophical needling with a list of things he knows: rain wets, fire burns, good pasture makes fat sheep. His account of being a hired man on another’s land keeps the pastoral honest.

Phebe

A shepherdess; first appears 3.5

She refuses Silvius in the language of a Petrarchan mistress and then falls for a boy who has just told her she is nothing special. Her instant love at the sight of Ganymede is the play’s demonstration that the conventions it laughs at are also how people actually behave.
