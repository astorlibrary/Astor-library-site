Pastoral and work

The forest is introduced as freedom from flattery and immediately shown to contain cold, hunger and wage labour. Shakespeare keeps a real shepherd on stage throughout, one who does not own his flock and cannot offer hospitality, so the idyll never closes over completely.

Through the play Duke Senior’s praise in 2.1 is answered in 2.4, by Touchstone’s ‘now am I in Arden, the more fool I’ and by Corin’s explanation of who owns the sheep.

In the text

Sweet are the uses of adversity, Which, like the toad, ugly and venomous, Wears yet a precious jewel in his head;

Duke Senior, 2.1.12

The toadstone was a piece of folklore that had been doubted for a century, so the consolation rests on an example the audience may not believe. That is characteristic of him: the speech is beautiful, sincere and delivered by a man who will propose killing deer for dinner six lines later.

Truly, shepherd, in respect of itself, it is a good life, but in respect that it is a shepherd’s life, it is naught. In respect that it is solitary, I like it very well; but in respect that it is private, it is a very vile life.

Touchstone, 3.2.13

Six clauses, each cancelling the last, and the joke is that the form is a parody of scholastic argument aimed at a man who has never been to school. It also happens to describe pastoral itself fairly: charming in the abstract, poor in the particular.

Disguise and gender

Rosalind’s doublet and hose start as a practical measure and become a method: as Ganymede she can teach, tease and question a man she means to marry. The layering was doubled in the original playhouse, where a boy played a woman playing a young man playing a woman.

Through the play Adopted for safety in 1.3, used as a tool in 3.2 and 4.1, and beginning to fail in 4.3, when she faints at the sight of blood and has to claim she counterfeited it.

In the text

and—in my heart Lie there what hidden woman’s fear there will— We’ll have a swashing and a martial outside, As many other mannish cowards have That do outface it with their semblances.

Rosalind, 1.3.113

She describes manhood as a costume other men are also wearing, which makes her disguise a general observation about men. ‘Outface it with their semblances’ is a courtier’s phrase about courtiers, and it comes before she has entered the wood: the play’s doubts about performed identity are there from the start.

Do you not know I am a woman? when I think, I must speak.

Rosalind, 3.2.215

She is wearing a doublet and hose and claiming a woman’s licence to talk, which is the disguise’s whole comedy in one sentence. The line is also a conventional joke about women’s speech, put in the mouth of the character who has more lines than any other woman Shakespeare wrote.

Love and its conventions

The play sets four courtships side by side (Rosalind and Orlando, Silvius and Phebe, Touchstone and Audrey, Oliver and Celia) and lets each expose the others. Rosalind’s cure is a course in the difference between the poetry of love and the twenty years that follow it.

Through the play Verses on trees in 3.2, a demolition of dying for love in 4.1, and a formal round in 5.2 in which four people say the same thing and mean four different things.

In the text

Love is merely a madness, and, I tell you, deserves as well a dark house and a whip as madmen do: and the reason why they are not so punished and cured is, that the lunacy is so ordinary that the whippers are in love too.

Rosalind, 3.2.337

The dark house and the whip were the real treatment for madness in 1599, so the joke has a cold floor under it. The turn comes in the last clause: nobody is treated because everybody, including the people who would do the whipping, has the disease, which makes love a condition of the whole society.

But these are all lies: men have died from time to time and worms have eaten them, but not for love.

Rosalind, 4.1.83

She has just spent ten lines rewriting two famous love-deaths as a club to the head and an attack of cramp, and the summary sentence is flat, unornamented and final. The word that matters is ‘worms’: it puts the plain physical fact where the literature was, and it is spoken by someone who is very much in love.

Court and exile

Two brothers have usurped two other brothers, and both crimes are motivated by nothing more explicable than dislike of someone better liked. The forest is not an alternative political order; it is a place where people wait, and the play returns them all to the court it criticised.

Through the play Usurpation stated in 1.1 and 1.2, a second court set up in 2.1, and in 5.4 a restoration announced by a messenger, with no fight.

In the text

And this our life exempt from public haunt Finds tongues in trees, books in the running brooks, Sermons in stones and good in every thing.

Duke Senior, 2.1.15

Each clause puts a court accomplishment (speech, learning, preaching) into a natural object, so the forest is praised for being a better version of what he has lost. The last phrase, ‘good in every thing’, is the kind of generalisation the other characters spend five acts testing.

You to your former honour I bequeath; Your patience and your virtue well deserves it

Jaques, 5.4.173

He distributes futures to each couple in turn, like a man reading a will, and keeps nothing for himself. ‘I am for other than for dancing measures,’ he says a few lines later, and he is allowed to go. A comedy that ends with one guest walking out admits that its settlement does not cover everyone.

Time

There is no hurry in Arden at all, and the play makes that its subject: Touchstone’s dial marks ripening and rotting in the same sentence, Rosalind lectures a stranger on how differently time moves for different people, and Jaques cuts a life into seven scenes and an exit.

Through the play A fool’s dial in 2.7, the seven ages in the same scene, and Rosalind’s divers paces in 3.2: three separate accounts of time within an act of each other.

In the text

And so, from hour to hour, we ripe and ripe, And then, from hour to hour, we rot and rot; And thereby hangs a tale.

Jaques, 2.7.26

Jaques is reporting Touchstone, so the play’s bleakest formulation reaches us at second hand from a professional joker. The repeated verbs make growth and decay the same mechanical process, and the tag at the end, ‘thereby hangs a tale’, draws no moral at all.

men are April when they woo, December when they wed: maids are May when they are maids, but the sky changes when they are wives.

Rosalind, 4.1.114

The warning is aimed at both parties, so it is more than a joke about husbands. She then promises to be jealous as a cock-pigeon and to weep for nothing, which puts herself in the prediction: she is letting him see the whole marriage before he agrees to it.

Melancholy and folly

Jaques and Touchstone are the play’s two professionals: one is paid to be foolish and is frequently wise, the other is sad by choice and frequently ridiculous. Between them they supply a running commentary that the lovers neither hear nor need.

Through the play Jaques wants the fool’s coat in 2.7, catalogues his own sadness in 4.1, and in 5.4 leaves the wedding for a cave.

In the text

All the world’s a stage, And all the men and women merely players: They have their exits and their entrances; And one man in his time plays many parts, His acts being seven ages.

Jaques, 2.7.139

The idea comes from the duke, who has just used the theatre image; Jaques takes it off him and runs. ‘Merely’ means entirely here, and the seven parts that follow are all masculine and all solitary: there is no scene in his account in which two people are together.

it is a melancholy of mine own, compounded of many simples, extracted from many objects, and indeed the sundry’s contemplation of my travels

Jaques, 4.1.13

The vocabulary is a pharmacist’s (simples, compounded, extracted), so he presents his own temperament as a recipe he has made up himself. Rosalind’s reply prices it exactly: a traveller who has sold his lands to see other men’s has rich eyes and poor hands.
