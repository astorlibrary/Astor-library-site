As You Like It · William Shakespeare

Themes

Pastoral and work

The forest is introduced as freedom from flattery and immediately shown to contain cold, hunger and wage labour. Shakespeare keeps a real shepherd on stage throughout, one who does not own his flock and cannot offer hospitality, so the idyll never closes over completely.

Duke Senior’s praise in 2.1 is answered in 2.4, by Touchstone’s ‘now am I in Arden, the more fool I’ and by Corin’s explanation of who owns the sheep.

Sweet are the uses of adversity, Which, like the toad, ugly and venomous, Wears yet a precious jewel in his head;

Duke Senior, 2.1.12

And this our life exempt from public haunt Finds tongues in trees, books in the running brooks, Sermons in stones and good in every thing.

Duke Senior, 2.1.15

Disguise and gender

Rosalind’s doublet and hose start as a practical measure and become a method: as Ganymede she can teach, tease and question a man she means to marry. The layering was doubled in the original playhouse, where a boy played a woman playing a young man playing a woman.

Adopted for safety in 1.3, used as a tool in 3.2 and 4.1, and beginning to fail in 4.3, when she faints at the sight of blood and has to claim she counterfeited it.

and--in my heart Lie there what hidden woman's fear there will-- We'll have a swashing and a martial outside, As many other mannish cowards have That do outface it with their semblances.

Rosalind, 1.3.113

I'll have no worse a name than Jove's own page; And therefore look you call me Ganymede.

Rosalind, 1.3.119

Love and its conventions

The play sets four courtships side by side (Rosalind and Orlando, Silvius and Phebe, Touchstone and Audrey, Oliver and Celia) and lets each expose the others. Rosalind’s cure is a course in the difference between the poetry of love and the twenty years that follow it.

Verses on trees in 3.2, a demolition of dying for love in 4.1, and a formal round in 5.2 in which four people say the same thing and mean four different things.

Do you not know I am a woman? when I think, I must speak.

Rosalind, 3.2.215

Love is merely a madness, and, I tell you, deserves as well a dark house and a whip as madmen do: and the reason why they are not so punished and cured is, that the lunacy is so ordinary that the whippers are in love too.

Rosalind, 3.2.337

Court and exile

Two brothers have usurped two other brothers, and both crimes are motivated by nothing more explicable than dislike of someone better liked. The forest is not an alternative political order; it is a place where people wait, and the play returns them all to the court it criticised.

Usurpation stated in 1.1 and 1.2, a second court set up in 2.1, and in 5.4 a restoration announced by a messenger, with no fight.

Time

There is no hurry in Arden at all, and the play makes that its subject: Touchstone’s dial marks ripening and rotting in the same sentence, Rosalind lectures a stranger on how differently time moves for different people, and Jaques cuts a life into seven scenes and an exit.

A fool’s dial in 2.7, the seven ages in the same scene, and Rosalind’s divers paces in 3.2: three separate accounts of time within an act of each other.

And so, from hour to hour, we ripe and ripe, And then, from hour to hour, we rot and rot; And thereby hangs a tale.

Jaques, 2.7.26

All the world's a stage, And all the men and women merely players: They have their exits and their entrances; And one man in his time plays many parts, His acts being seven ages.

Jaques, 2.7.139

Melancholy and folly

Jaques and Touchstone are the play’s two professionals: one is paid to be foolish and is frequently wise, the other is sad by choice and frequently ridiculous. Between them they supply a running commentary that the lovers neither hear nor need.

Jaques wants the fool’s coat in 2.7, catalogues his own sadness in 4.1, and in 5.4 leaves the wedding for a cave.

Ay, now am I in Arden; the more fool I; when I was at home, I was in a better place: but travellers must be content.

Touchstone, 2.4.12

And so, from hour to hour, we ripe and ripe, And then, from hour to hour, we rot and rot; And thereby hangs a tale.

Jaques, 2.7.26
