As You Like It · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Prose

Unmetred speech used for argument, wit and social ease as well as low comedy.

Rosalind conducts the courtship almost entirely in prose while Orlando writes verse at her, and the play’s intelligence is largely on the prose side of that divide.

Ay, now am I in Arden; the more fool I; when I was at home, I was in a better place: but travellers must be content.

Touchstone, 2.4.12

Disguise and dramatic irony

A concealed identity that lets the audience hear two conversations at the same time.

Every exchange between Orlando and Ganymede is double: he is practising, she is being courted, and only the audience is in both scenes at the same time.

and--in my heart Lie there what hidden woman's fear there will-- We'll have a swashing and a martial outside, As many other mannish cowards have That do outface it with their semblances.

Rosalind, 1.3.113

Song

Lyric set into the action, marking time or supplying a mood the dialogue withholds.

There are more songs here than in any other Shakespeare comedy, and Jaques attaches a mocking verse to one of them, so even the music is examined.

Blow, blow, thou winter wind. Thou art not so unkind As man's ingratitude; Thy tooth is not so keen, Because thou art not seen, Although thy breath be rude.

Amiens, 2.7.173

The formal set piece

A self-contained speech that pauses the action to survey a subject.

Duke Senior on adversity, Jaques on the seven ages and Rosalind on the paces of time are all detachable, and all are answered by the stage business around them.

Sweet are the uses of adversity, Which, like the toad, ugly and venomous, Wears yet a precious jewel in his head;

Duke Senior, 2.1.12

Pastoral convention

The inherited literary world of shepherds, scornful shepherdesses and lovesick swains.

Silvius and Phebe are the convention played straight, Corin is what a shepherd is actually like, and Touchstone stands between them totalling up the difference.

Truly, shepherd, in respect of itself, it is a good life, but in respect that it is a shepherd's life, it is naught. In respect that it is solitary, I like it very well; but in respect that it is private, it is a very vile life.

Touchstone, 3.2.13

Metatheatre

The play stepping outside itself to address the audience directly.

Rosalind ends the play by noting that a lady epilogue is unusual and then offering to kiss the men in the audience if she were a woman, which, in 1599, she was not.

All the world's a stage, And all the men and women merely players: They have their exits and their entrances; And one man in his time plays many parts, His acts being seven ages.

Jaques, 2.7.139
