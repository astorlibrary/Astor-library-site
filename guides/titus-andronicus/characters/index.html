Titus Andronicus · William Shakespeare

Characters

Titus Andronicus

Roman general, back from ten years of war; first appears 1.1

He has buried twenty-one sons for Rome and cannot imagine an argument that Rome’s forms will not settle. He gives away the empire, sacrifices a prisoner because the rite requires it, and kills his own son for standing in a doorway. When the forms stop working he goes to the stones, then to the gods by arrow, and finally does the arithmetic himself with a knife and a pie dish.

Lavinia

Titus’s daughter, betrothed to Bassianus; first appears 1.1

She is bargained over by two men in the first scene and has her tongue and hands cut off in the second act so that she can name nobody. What follows is the hardest thing in Shakespeare to stage: forty lines in which her uncle describes her, scene after scene in which her family guesses at her meaning, and then the moment she takes a staff in her mouth and writes two names in the sand.

Tamora

Queen of the Goths, then Empress of Rome; first appears 1.1

She begins the play kneeling in the street to beg for her son’s life and is refused in the name of piety. Within two hundred lines she is empress, and she spends the rest of the play making Rome pay. The play lets her be right about the sacrifice and monstrous about everything after it, and she never pretends the two are connected by anything but appetite.

Aaron

A Moor, Tamora’s lover, and the engineer of the plot; first appears 1.1

He buries the gold, writes the letter, guides the brothers to the pit, talks Titus out of his hand and watches through a crack in the wall while the heads come back. He is also the only parent in the play who protects a child: when his black son is born he kills the nurse, refuses the empress and carries the baby off. On the ladder he confesses everything and regrets one good deed.

Saturninus

The late emperor’s elder son, and Rome’s new emperor; first appears 1.1

He is given the empire by the man he immediately insults, and loses Lavinia to his brother within a hundred lines of claiming her. Everything he does afterwards is reaction: he marries Tamora on the spot, condemns Titus’s sons on evidence Aaron arranged for him, orders a clown hanged for carrying a letter, and dies at his own banquet without ever understanding who was moving him.

Lucius

Titus’s eldest surviving son, exile, and the last emperor standing; first appears 1.1

He asks for the sacrifice in the first scene, which makes him the cause of the revenge as much as his father. Banished for trying to rescue his brothers, he comes back at the head of the army of the people whose prince he had burned. He swears by a god Aaron does not believe in to keep a baby alive, and keeps the promise while sentencing its father to starve in the ground.

Marcus Andronicus

Tribune of the People, and Titus’s brother; first appears 1.1

He offers Rome to Titus, argues him into burying Mutius, and finds Lavinia in the wood. His long speech over her has been the play’s most argued-about passage for two centuries: it is beautiful, it is about Ovid, and it does nothing for her. He kills the fly, thinks of the staff in the sand, and is left at the end trying to talk a city back into one piece.

Demetrius

Tamora’s elder surviving son; first appears 1.1

He and his brother open Act II with swords drawn over Lavinia and are still finishing each other’s sentences when Titus cuts their throats. Demetrius supplies the reasoning: she is a woman, therefore may be won; she is Lavinia, therefore must be loved. He puts the first knife into Bassianus, talks Tamora out of killing Lavinia on the spot (thrash the corn first, burn the straw after) and jeers at her to go and tell.

Chiron

Tamora’s younger son; first appears 1.1

The younger and touchier of the pair, mocked by his brother for the dancing-rapier their mother gave him in the quarrel that opens Act II. He orders Bassianus’s body dragged to a secret hole, then tells Lavinia to write her meaning down if her stumps will let her, a joke the play turns into the instrument of his death. Watching Alarbus led away in 1.1, he says Scythia was never half so barbarous, and he is not counting himself.

Young Lucius

Lucius’s son, a boy; first appears 3.2

He carries the books his aunt chases him for, and his copy of Ovid is what lets the family finally learn who attacked her. He offers to stab Chiron and Demetrius with a dagger and is told there is a better way. At the end he cannot get a word out for crying, which in this play is the only honest response anyone manages to the sight of the dead.
