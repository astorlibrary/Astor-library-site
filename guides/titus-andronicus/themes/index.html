Revenge

Nobody in the play invents revenge; they all inherit it. Alarbus dies because Lucius asks for a sacrifice, Bassianus dies because he mocked Tamora in the forest, Chiron and Demetrius die because Lavinia was attacked, and Tamora dies at a table. Each killing is presented by its author as the settling of an account, which is why the play can keep going after the point at which grief should have stopped it.

Through the play The sacrifice at 1.1 sets the rate; Tamora’s aside at 1.1.452 promises a day; Titus takes his vow over two severed heads in 3.1; and 5.3 collects three of the debts in four lines.

In the text

I’ll find a day to massacre them all, And raze their faction and their family, The cruel father and his traitorous sons, To whom I sued for my dear son’s life

Tamora, 1.1.452

The double voice is the whole of her method: aloud she is reconciling the court, under her breath she is naming a date. Notice what she lists as the grievance. It is not the war, the captivity or the yoke; it is the refusal of a kneeling woman in the street. She is exact about the debt, and she collects on that account.

Why dost thou laugh? It fits not with this hour.

Marcus, 3.1.266

Marcus speaks for the part of the audience still trying to keep the genre steady. Titus’s answer is that he has no tears left, which is a kind of accountancy: grief has a supply, and it has run out. The laugh is the moment the play stops being a tragedy of suffering and becomes a tragedy of planning.

Law

Rome has elections, tribunes, judges and a senate, and every one of them fails inside two acts. Titus pleads in a street the tribunes have already left, then tells his sorrows to the stones because stones at least do not interrupt. By Act IV he is digging for Justice in the underworld and shooting petitions to the gods over the palace wall.

Through the play The election in 1.1 is the last working civic act; the trial of Quintus and Martius happens offstage on planted evidence; 3.1 has Titus pleading to nobody; 4.3 sends letters to Jove because there is nowhere else to send them.

In the text

Why, foolish Lucius, dost thou not perceive That Rome is but a wilderness of tigers? Tigers must prey, and Rome affords no prey But me and mine.

Titus, 3.1.53

Titus gave this city his sons, his arm and the empire, and here he sees it plainly for the first time. Wilderness is the sharp word: it means no Rome at all, a wood with animals in it, far worse than a corrupt Rome to be reformed. He has just come from the forest where his daughter was attacked, and he no longer distinguishes the two places.

And sith there’s no justice in earth nor hell, We will solicit heaven and move the gods To send down Justice for to wreak our wrongs.

Titus, 4.3.50

He has already sent men to dig for Justice underground and to drag the sea for her. The logic is perfectly sound and the method is ridiculous, which is how this play does madness: a rational man following a failed system to its last address, with no confusion at all. The arrows land in the palace, and Saturninus reads them as libel.

Evidence

Rome keeps convicting on objects. A bag of gold under a tree and a letter dropped where it will be found are enough to condemn two men for a murder they did not commit. The one piece of true testimony in the play has to be written in sand with a staff held in a mouth, and even then Marcus has to swear the family to believe it.

Through the play Aaron buries the gold and writes the letter in 2.3; the pit makes two innocent men look like murderers on sight; Lavinia writes Stuprum and two names at 4.1.78; Aaron confirms all of it from a ladder in 5.1.

In the text

Stuprum. Chiron. Demetrius.

Titus, 4.1.78

Three words, one of them Latin, and the play’s only piece of direct testimony. ‘Stuprum’ is the legal term: she names the crime in the language of the courts that failed her, then names the men. After two acts of other people describing her, this is Lavinia speaking, and it takes a stick, a patch of sand and both her stumps.

But I have done a thousand dreadful things As willingly as one would kill a fly, And nothing grieves me heartily indeed But that I cannot do ten thousand more.

Aaron, 5.1.143

The comparison reaches back to Act III, where a killed fly reduced Titus to fury and then to knife-work. Aaron uses the same insect to measure indifference. He has bought his son’s life with this confession and spends the rest of it refusing the remorse that would make the bargain look like repentance.

Lavinia

The play cuts out its heroine’s tongue at the end of Act II and then spends three acts on the problem of what she still means. Her family talk at her, over her and for her. Titus claims he can read every sign she makes and promises to wrest an alphabet out of her stumps, which is devotion and also another way of speaking on her behalf.

Through the play She argues fluently with Tamora at 2.3; Marcus describes her for forty lines at 2.4; Titus interprets her at 3.2; she writes for herself at 4.1, and her father kills her at 5.3 without asking.

In the text

So, now go tell, an if thy tongue can speak, Who ’twas that cut thy tongue and ravished thee.

Demetrius, 2.4.1

The play opens its most notorious scene with a taunt made out of the mechanics of the crime. Philomela in Ovid was silenced and still wove the truth; Demetrius thinks he has closed that loophole by taking the hands as well. The rest of the act, and most of the next two, is the play proving him wrong by a staff and a sandy plot.

Thou shalt not sigh, nor hold thy stumps to heaven, Nor wink, nor nod, nor kneel, nor make a sign, But I of these will wrest an alphabet

Titus, 3.2.42

The tenderest speech he makes, and it is also a claim of ownership over her meaning. Wrest is a violent verb for a loving promise: he will force a language out of her body whether the signs are offered or not. The play gives her a father who will not stop attending to her, yet it is Marcus who thinks of giving her a staff to write with.

Race

Everyone in Rome describes Aaron by his colour, and so does he. The difference is that he makes an argument of it: coal-black scorns to take another hue, while a white face betrays its owner by blushing. When his son is born he will not let the baby be called a devil, and the scene gives him the only unconditional love anyone in the play shows a child.

Through the play He is a silent prisoner through 1.1; he walks out of 3.1 with a hand he has just cut off and a couplet rhyming his soul to his face; 4.2 has him defending the baby against its own mother and brothers; 5.3 sentences him to be buried breast-deep and starved.

In the text

Let fools do good, and fair men call for grace, Aaron will have his soul black like his face.

Aaron, 3.1.205

He takes Rome’s insult and makes it a rhyme and a policy. Fair men call for grace pins the hypocrisy: the same city that equates whiteness with virtue has just cheated an old man out of his hand. Aaron takes the identity Rome has assigned him and makes it his own, which is not the same as agreeing with it.

Coal-black is better than another hue In that it scorns to bear another hue; For all the water in the ocean Can never turn the swan’s black legs to white

Aaron, 4.2.100

The argument is that black keeps its own colour and white cannot. Fifteen lines later Chiron says he blushes at the family shame, and Aaron pounces: a treacherous hue that betrays the counsels of the heart. It is the one speech in the play where blackness is a boast, made by a man holding a newborn in one arm and a scimitar in the other.

Ritual

Titus does nothing in Act I that Rome would call wrong. He buries his sons with the proper rite, refuses the empire out of modesty, offers the sacrifice the dead are owed, and kills Mutius for dishonouring the family. The play then follows each of those correct acts to its consequence, and leaves the audience wondering whether ceremony was ever doing the work everyone believed it was doing.

Through the play The tomb opens at 1.1 and Alarbus burns; Mutius is refused burial and then granted it after a scene of kneeling; the final act distributes burial as a verdict, with Tamora thrown to the birds.

In the text

Wilt thou draw near the nature of the gods? Draw near them then in being merciful. Sweet mercy is nobility’s true badge.

Tamora, 1.1.117

She argues in Rome’s own vocabulary, and she argues well: nobility, the gods, mercy as a mark of rank. Titus answers with the word religiously and burns the boy anyway. Everything Tamora does for the rest of the play is licensed by the fact that she asked properly first and was refused on a technicality of rite.

Why, there they are, both baked in this pie, Whereof their mother daintily hath fed, Eating the flesh that she herself hath bred.

Titus, 5.3.60

The line is delivered as information, in answer to a polite request, by a man dressed as a cook. Daintily does the damage: it makes Tamora a guest with manners while she finishes her sons. Three lines later he stabs her, and the revenge that began with a boy burned on a tomb is settled over dinner in under thirty seconds.

Hands, tongues and writing

The play is obsessed with the body parts that make meaning. Hands are cut off, bargained for, sent back in scorn, carried between teeth and used to write in sand. Tongues are cut out, bitten, and wished away. When Marcus says the word hands at dinner, Titus cannot stop talking about it, and nor can the play.

Through the play Lavinia loses both at 2.4; Titus gives his away at 3.1 and gets it back with two heads; in 3.2 the word hands becomes a wound; in 4.1 a staff stands in for the hand and sand for the tongue.

In the text

These words are razors to my wounded heart.

Titus, 1.1.316

The first blade in a play full of them, and it is only a figure of speech. By Act III the metaphors have all come true: hands actually come off, tongues actually go, and the razor is a real knife held by a father over two bound men. Again and again Titus’s language predicts the stage business that arrives to replace it.

Write down thy mind, bewray thy meaning so, An if thy stumps will let thee play the scribe.

Chiron, 2.4.3

The play takes the joke literally: it becomes the instrument of his death. Two acts later Lavinia does play the scribe, guiding a staff with her stumps and her mouth, and writes his name. Chiron names the method by which he will be identified, bound, gagged and bled over a basin. Few taunts in Shakespeare are repaid so exactly.
