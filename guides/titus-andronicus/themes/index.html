Titus Andronicus · William Shakespeare

Themes

Revenge

Nobody in the play invents revenge; they all inherit it. Alarbus dies because Lucius asks for a sacrifice, Bassianus dies because he mocked Tamora in the forest, Chiron and Demetrius die because Lavinia was attacked, and Tamora dies at a table. Each killing is presented by its author as the settling of an account, which is why the play can keep going after the point at which grief should have stopped it.

The sacrifice at 1.1 sets the rate; Tamora’s aside at 1.1.452 promises a day; Titus takes his vow over two severed heads in 3.1; and 5.3 collects three of the debts in four lines.

Wilt thou draw near the nature of the gods? Draw near them then in being merciful. Sweet mercy is nobility's true badge.

Tamora, 1.1.117

I'll find a day to massacre them all, And raze their faction and their family, The cruel father and his traitorous sons, To whom I sued for my dear son's life

Tamora, 1.1.452

Law

Rome has elections, tribunes, judges and a senate, and every one of them fails inside two acts. Titus pleads in a street the tribunes have already left, then tells his sorrows to the stones because stones at least do not interrupt. By Act IV he is digging for Justice in the underworld and shooting petitions to the gods over the palace wall.

The election in 1.1 is the last working civic act; the trial of Quintus and Martius happens offstage on planted evidence; 3.1 has Titus pleading to nobody; 4.3 sends letters to Jove because there is nowhere else to send them.

Wilt thou draw near the nature of the gods? Draw near them then in being merciful. Sweet mercy is nobility's true badge.

Tamora, 1.1.117

O cruel, irreligious piety!

Tamora, 1.1.130

Evidence

Rome keeps convicting on objects. A bag of gold under a tree and a letter dropped where it will be found are enough to condemn two men for a murder they did not commit. The one piece of true testimony in the play has to be written in sand with a staff held in a mouth, and even then Marcus has to swear the family to believe it.

Aaron buries the gold and writes the letter in 2.3; the pit makes two innocent men look like murderers on sight; Lavinia writes Stuprum and two names at 4.1.78; Aaron confirms all of it from a ladder in 5.1.

So, now go tell, an if thy tongue can speak, Who 'twas that cut thy tongue and ravished thee.

Demetrius, 2.4.1

Write down thy mind, bewray thy meaning so, An if thy stumps will let thee play the scribe.

Chiron, 2.4.3

Lavinia

The play cuts out its heroine’s tongue at the end of Act II and then spends three acts on the problem of what she still means. Her family talk at her, over her and for her. Titus claims he can read every sign she makes and promises to wrest an alphabet out of her stumps, which is devotion and also another way of speaking on her behalf.

She argues fluently with Tamora at 2.3; Marcus describes her for forty lines at 2.4; Titus interprets her at 3.2; she writes for herself at 4.1, and her father kills her at 5.3 without asking.

So, now go tell, an if thy tongue can speak, Who 'twas that cut thy tongue and ravished thee.

Demetrius, 2.4.1

Write down thy mind, bewray thy meaning so, An if thy stumps will let thee play the scribe.

Chiron, 2.4.3

Race

Everyone in Rome describes Aaron by his colour, and so does he. The difference is that he makes an argument of it: coal-black scorns to take another hue, while a white face betrays its owner by blushing. When his son is born he will not let the baby be called a devil, and the scene gives him the only unconditional love anyone in the play shows a child.

He is a silent prisoner through 1.1; he walks out of 3.1 with a hand he has just cut off and a couplet rhyming his soul to his face; 4.2 has him defending the baby against its own mother and brothers; 5.3 sentences him to be buried breast-deep and starved.

Let fools do good, and fair men call for grace, Aaron will have his soul black like his face.

Aaron, 3.1.205

Pardon me, sir; 'twas a black ill-favoured fly, Like to the empress' Moor; therefore I killed him.

Marcus, 3.2.66

Ritual

Titus does nothing in Act I that Rome would call wrong. He buries his sons with the proper rite, refuses the empire out of modesty, offers the sacrifice the dead are owed, and kills Mutius for dishonouring the family. The play then follows each of those correct acts to its consequence, and leaves the audience wondering whether ceremony was ever doing the work everyone believed it was doing.

The tomb opens at 1.1 and Alarbus burns; Mutius is refused burial and then granted it after a scene of kneeling; the final act distributes burial as a verdict, with Tamora thrown to the birds.

Wilt thou draw near the nature of the gods? Draw near them then in being merciful. Sweet mercy is nobility's true badge.

Tamora, 1.1.130

Hands, tongues and writing

The play is obsessed with the body parts that make meaning. Hands are cut off, bargained for, sent back in scorn, carried between teeth and used to write in sand. Tongues are cut out, bitten, and wished away. When Marcus says the word hands at dinner, Titus cannot stop talking about it, and nor can the play.

Lavinia loses both at 2.4; Titus gives his away at 3.1 and gets it back with two heads; in 3.2 the word hands becomes a wound; in 4.1 a staff stands in for the hand and sand for the tongue.

Thou art a Roman; be not barbarous.

Marcus, 1.1.380

These words are razors to my wounded heart.

Titus, 1.1.316
