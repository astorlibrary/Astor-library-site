Titus Andronicus · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Symbolic objects

A physical thing on stage doing work that speech would otherwise have to do.

A bag of gold, a letter, a book of Ovid, a staff, a basin, a severed hand, two heads and a pie. The play moves from one object to the next, and an audience always knows exactly what is being carried on and what it will cost.

Stuprum. Chiron. Demetrius.

Titus, 4.1.78

Classical allusion

Naming a Greek or Roman story so that it explains, or supplies a pattern for, the action on stage.

Philomela is not decoration here. Lavinia turns the pages of the Metamorphoses to the tale of Tereus because it is the nearest thing she has to a sentence, and Titus tops it at 5.2: worse than Philomel, and worse than Procne in return.

So, now go tell, an if thy tongue can speak, Who 'twas that cut thy tongue and ravished thee.

Demetrius, 2.4.1

Antithesis

Two opposed terms set against each other inside a single line or sentence.

Roman against barbarous, fair against black, white-limed walls against coal-black. The play thinks in pairs, and Aaron handles the figure better than anyone in Rome, because the pairs are the ones the city has used to place him and he turns each of them over.

Thou art a Roman; be not barbarous.

Marcus, 1.1.380

Personification

An abstract idea given a body, a costume and lines to speak.

Revenge, Rape and Murder knock at Titus’s study door in 5.2, which is what an old morality play would do in earnest. They are a queen and her two sons in costume, and Titus has already named her to her face by the evidence of his own stump, so the allegory is a lie both sides keep up until he cuts two of its throats.

Know thou, sad man, I am not Tamora; She is thy enemy, and I thy friend. I am Revenge, sent from th' infernal kingdom

Tamora, 5.2.28

Hyperbole

Exaggeration held so long that it stops being ornament and becomes the state of the speaker.

Titus offers to water the earth until eternal spring grows on it, declares himself the sea and Lavinia the weeping sky, and says his sorrow has no bottom. Marcus keeps asking him to speak with possibility, and he cannot.

These words are razors to my wounded heart.

Titus, 1.1.316

The set piece

A long formal speech that halts the action and asks to be admired on its own terms.

Marcus’s forty lines over the mutilated Lavinia at 2.4 stop the play dead. Lutes, conduits, Titan’s face, Cerberus: the verse is exquisite and it leaves her standing there bleeding while he finishes.

Fair Philomela, why she but lost her tongue, And in a tedious sampler sewed her mind; But, lovely niece, that mean is cut from thee

Marcus, 2.4.38

Black comedy

Jokes placed where an audience expects horror, so that laughter and disgust arrive together.

Titus laughs on hearing he has no tears left, Marcus explains that the fly resembled the empress’s Moor, and a clown is hanged for delivering a letter with two pigeons. Productions discover the play is funny and then have to decide what to do about it.

So, now go tell, an if thy tongue can speak, Who 'twas that cut thy tongue and ravished thee.

Demetrius, 2.4.1
