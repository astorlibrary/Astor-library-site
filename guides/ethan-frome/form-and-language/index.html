Ethan Frome · Edith Wharton

Form and language

Frame narrative

A story enclosed within another story, often several layers deep.

The prologue and epilogue belong to an outsider who admits his sources contradicted each other; the nine chapters between them are his reconstruction, given with a confidence about Ethan’s private thoughts that no informant could have supplied.

I had the story, bit by bit, from various people, and, as generally happens in such cases, each time it was a different story.

The narrator, Prologue

Landscape

Using terrain, weather and scale to act on the characters.

The hill, the snow, the distance to Corbury Flats and the elm at the bottom of the run are all load-bearing: each of them limits what a character is physically able to do next.

it seemed to produce no change except that of retarding still more the sluggish pulse of Starkfield

The narrator, Prologue

Dialect

Regional speech written phonetically.

The villagers’ speech is set down as it sounds (“wust kind”, “’cept”, “ha’ died”), while the narrator’s prose stays formal, so the gap between the observer and the observed is audible on every page he quotes.

He’s looked that way ever since he had his smash-up; and that’s twenty-four years ago come next February

Harmon Gow, Prologue

Objects and omens

Things in the narrative that carry a meaning the characters half-register.

A wedding-present pickle-dish taken down for one supper and broken by a cat carries the whole marriage; so do a hanging ribbon, a dead cucumber-vine and a gravestone that already bears Ethan’s name.

We’ll always go on living here together, and some day she’ll lie there beside me.

Ethan Frome, Chapter II

Omission

Leaving out the part of a story the reader most expects to be shown.

Chapter IX breaks off at the elm and resumes with Ethan lying in the snow beside Mattie; the epilogue then jumps to a kitchen twenty-four years later without narrating the injuries or the decisions that followed, so the reader reconstructs the worst of it from a woman’s shape in a chair.

A slatternly calico wrapper hung from her shoulders and the wisps of her thin grey hair were drawn away from a high forehead and fastened at the back by a broken comb.

Epilogue

Understatement

Reporting extreme events in language deliberately too mild for them.

The village calls a catastrophe a smash-up, a driver says Ethan will likely touch a hundred, and the book’s cruellest judgement is delivered by a kindly landlady, who admits it once shocked her minister.

He’s looked that way ever since he had his smash-up; and that’s twenty-four years ago come next February

Harmon Gow, Prologue
