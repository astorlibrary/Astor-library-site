Ethan Frome · Edith Wharton

Themes

Class and money

The book gives exact figures and lets them do the work of fate: fifty dollars asked for and refused, lumber paid for three months in arrears, a mortgage that cannot be cleared, a girl with no saleable skill. Wharton’s tragedy turns on a household that cannot raise the fare out.

The poverty is scenery in the early chapters and machinery by Chapter VIII, where the plan to run away collapses on the price of the tickets.

Guess he’s been in Starkfield too many winters. Most of the smart ones get away.

Harmon Gow, Prologue

I’ve got complications

Zeena Frome, Chapter VII

Duty and the sick wife

Ethan is held by illness: first his father’s, then his mother’s, then Zeena’s, in an unbroken line of people who need nursing. The novel never says whether Zeena is ill or has discovered that illness is the only authority open to her.

Marriage is presented as the consequence of a funeral, and the epilogue completes the pattern by putting the invalid on her feet to nurse the girl she sent away.

He looks as if he was dead and in hell now!

Harmon Gow, Prologue

There the silence had deepened about him year by year.

Chapter IV

Silence

Almost nothing in this book is spoken at the moment it matters. Ethan rehearses sentences he does not deliver and writes a letter he does not send, Zeena lets Mattie leave without a goodbye, and Mrs Ned Hale keeps her verdict for the last page. Wharton makes the unspoken sentence a physical event, with a place in the scene.

The narrator names the silence in the prologue as something frozen; by Chapter IX the two people involved can reach each other only by what they do.

I had the story, bit by bit, from various people, and, as generally happens in such cases, each time it was a different story.

The narrator, Prologue

He’s looked that way ever since he had his smash-up; and that’s twenty-four years ago come next February

Harmon Gow, Prologue

Winter

Cold in this book is economics: it decides what work can be done, which roads are passable, how long it takes to reach the station and what a farm can earn. The village’s phrase for a man ruined by staying, too many winters, is a piece of local economics.

The narrator sits out one winter and comes to understand the phrase; the epilogue shows what twenty-four more have done since the smash-up, confining all three survivors to a single heated kitchen.

He’s looked that way ever since he had his smash-up; and that’s twenty-four years ago come next February

Harmon Gow, Prologue

Love and harm

The feeling between Ethan and Mattie is made of very small things (a ribbon, a chair moved, a shared supper), and the novel is precise about how little it would have taken to satisfy it and how completely that little is unavailable. What it produces is an attempt at a double suicide that leaves both of them alive.

It begins as company in Chapter I, becomes a domestic fantasy over one supper, and by the top of the sledding hill has turned into the conviction that there is no version of being together except not surviving.

We’ll always go on living here together, and some day she’ll lie there beside me.

Ethan Frome, Chapter II

backed into the pickle-dish, which fell to the floor with a crash

Chapter IV

Isolation

Starkfield is described as a place where the clever leave and the rest are snowed in, and the Frome farm stands a mile beyond even that. Ethan has had to take down the house’s ‘L’, the wing that joins it to the woodshed and barn, and the narrator sees in the diminished dwelling an image of his shrunken body; the epilogue shuts three people into one kitchen that almost nobody visits.

Geographic at first, then social (only the doctor and Mrs Hale still call), and finally absolute, when the only stranger to enter the kitchen in years is a man waiting out a storm.

He looks as if he was dead and in hell now!

Harmon Gow, Prologue
