Class and money

The book gives exact figures and lets them do the work of fate: fifty dollars asked for and refused, lumber paid for three months in arrears, a mortgage that cannot be cleared, a girl with no saleable skill. Wharton’s tragedy turns on a household that cannot raise the fare out.

Through the book The poverty is scenery in the early chapters and machinery by Chapter VIII, where the plan to run away collapses on the price of the tickets.

In the text

He was a prisoner for life, and now his one ray of light was to be extinguished.

Chapter VIII

The sentence comes straight after a page of arithmetic, the price of tickets, the mortgage, three months’ credit, so the prison metaphor is being checked against a ledger. Wharton is precise that the walls are financial, and that the door was shut by a kind man who was also short of money.

A slatternly calico wrapper hung from her shoulders and the wisps of her thin grey hair were drawn away from a high forehead and fastened at the back by a broken comb.

Epilogue

The description is given before the name, so the reader spends a paragraph deciding which of the two women this is and gets it wrong. The broken comb is the telling detail: everything in the room has been mended once and is now simply being used broken.

Duty and the sick wife

Ethan is held by illness: first his father’s, then his mother’s, then Zeena’s, in an unbroken line of people who need nursing. The novel never says whether Zeena is ill or has discovered that illness is the only authority open to her.

Through the book Marriage is presented as the consequence of a funeral, and the epilogue completes the pattern by putting the invalid on her feet to nurse the girl she sent away.

In the text

Sacred to the memory of Ethan Frome and Endurance his wife, who dwelled together in peace for fifty years.

Chapter IV

The wife’s name is a virtue, and the epitaph is about how long they lasted: fifty years, in peace, together. Wharton then has Ethan wonder whether the same words will be written over him and Zeena, and the epilogue answers the question without needing a stone.

I’ve got complications

Zeena Frome, Chapter VII

Three words, delivered with the air of somebody singled out for a great fate. In a village where everybody has troubles, the word is a promotion, and Wharton has Zeena win the only argument that matters by producing a noun her husband cannot contradict.

Silence

Almost nothing in this book is spoken at the moment it matters. Ethan rehearses sentences he does not deliver and writes a letter he does not send, Zeena lets Mattie leave without a goodbye, and Mrs Ned Hale keeps her verdict for the last page. Wharton makes the unspoken sentence a physical event, with a place in the scene.

Through the book The narrator names the silence in the prologue as something frozen; by Chapter IX the two people involved can reach each other only by what they do.

In the text

I had the story, bit by bit, from various people, and, as generally happens in such cases, each time it was a different story.

The narrator, Prologue

The first sentence of a tragedy is a warning about evidence. ‘Bit by bit’ concedes that the pieces arrived separately, and the last clause concedes that they contradicted each other. Everything the reader is about to be shown in close-up third person has been assembled by a man who has just told us his materials do not agree.

There the silence had deepened about him year by year.

Chapter IV

Silence is given a depth and a rate of increase, like snow or debt. The sentence also supplies the motive for the marriage twenty pages before the book states it: Ethan asks Zeena to stay because she has been speaking in a house where nobody spoke.

Winter

Cold in this book is economics: it decides what work can be done, which roads are passable, how long it takes to reach the station and what a farm can earn. The village’s phrase for a man ruined by staying, too many winters, is a piece of local economics.

Through the book The narrator sits out one winter and comes to understand the phrase; the epilogue shows what twenty-four more have done since the smash-up, confining all three survivors to a single heated kitchen.

In the text

He’s looked that way ever since he had his smash-up; and that’s twenty-four years ago come next February

Harmon Gow, Prologue

The village’s word for the event is a word for a cart overturning, and the sentence dates it with the precision people use for a wedding or a death. The whole book is contained in the gap between the mildness of ‘smash-up’ and the fact that somebody has been counting the Februaries.

There was in him a slumbering spark of sociability which the long Starkfield winters had not yet extinguished.

Chapter IV

‘Not yet’ is the word that makes a kind sentence threatening. The metaphor is a fire going out slowly in a cold house, and the chapter that contains it is the only one in which Ethan whistles, sings and behaves like a man of twenty-eight.

Love and harm

The feeling between Ethan and Mattie is made of very small things (a ribbon, a chair moved, a shared supper), and the novel is precise about how little it would have taken to satisfy it and how completely that little is unavailable. What it produces is an attempt at a double suicide that leaves both of them alive.

Through the book It begins as company in Chapter I, becomes a domestic fantasy over one supper, and by the top of the sledding hill has turned into the conviction that there is no version of being together except not surviving.

In the text

We’ll always go on living here together, and some day she’ll lie there beside me.

Ethan Frome, Chapter II

This is the novel’s love scene, and it consists of a man imagining a shared grave. In one sentence the graves that stood for everything he could not escape become a promise, and that shows how narrow the available happiness is.

backed into the pickle-dish, which fell to the floor with a crash

Chapter IV

The agent of the disaster is a cat, which lets Wharton destroy the marriage’s most valued object without giving anybody the blame or the satisfaction. The dish was a wedding present never used; taking it down for one meal is the only extravagance in the book, and it is punished within the hour.

Isolation

Starkfield is described as a place where the clever leave and the rest are snowed in, and the Frome farm stands a mile beyond even that. Ethan has had to take down the house’s ‘L’, the wing that joins it to the woodshed and barn, and the narrator sees in the diminished dwelling an image of his shrunken body; the epilogue shuts three people into one kitchen that almost nobody visits.

Through the book Geographic at first, then social (only the doctor and Mrs Hale still call), and finally absolute, when the only stranger to enter the kitchen in years is a man waiting out a storm.

In the text

He looks as if he was dead and in hell now!

Harmon Gow, Prologue

Gow has just said the Fromes are tough and will last, and he immediately spoils the compliment. Putting hell in the present tense makes the rest of the book an explanation: the reader knows the outcome and is waiting to be told what led to it.

I don’t see’s there’s much difference between the Fromes up at the farm and the Fromes down in the graveyard; ’cept that down there they’re all quiet, and the women have got to hold their tongues.

Mrs Ned Hale, Epilogue

The joke is that the dead are quieter, and the sting is in what it concedes about the living: the kitchen is a grave with the arguing left in. Ending a novel about a man who could not speak on a line about women who cannot stop is Wharton’s final adjustment to who, in Starkfield, has actually been silenced.
