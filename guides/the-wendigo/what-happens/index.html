The Wendigo · Algernon Blackwood

What happens

Sections I-II The hunting party sets out

Blackwood assembles five men with five different ways of explaining things: a Scottish psychologist who has written a book on collective hallucination, his nephew who is training for the ministry, two guides, and an Indian cook. The party splits, and Défago takes Simpson towards a lake he has reasons for avoiding that he does not give.

Section I
A season with no moose in it, and a doctor who comes home with something better than a trophy.
Section I
A guide who sings in the canoe, knows the bush better than anyone, and has fits of melancholy nobody asks about.
Section II
Simpson goes with Défago towards Fifty Island Water; his uncle and Hank take the other direction.

Sections II-III The camp, and Défago’s fear

They make camp on the lake, and the guide’s nerve goes. He smells something on the air, cannot say what, and by nightfall he is frightened of the dark in a country he has worked in all his life. Simpson, who is twenty-something and reads everything as a spiritual exercise, notices and does not understand.

Section II
A lake that answers exactly to the description, and a guide who stops talking when they reach it.
Section IV
Faint, pungent and wrong for a forest: the odour Simpson will spend the rest of his life failing to describe.
Sections III–IV
Weeping in the tent, a man pressed against the canvas, and a voice outside calling his name.

Section IV Défago is taken

Défago is called out of the tent by something that uses his name, and goes at a speed no man runs. What Simpson hears as the sound recedes is the story’s most famous sentence, and it comes from high above, out of the sky.

Section IV
The guide kicks free of his blankets and is gone through the tent flaps, at a pace that takes him out of earshot in seconds.
Section IV
The cry about burning feet of fire, heard once, from above, and then silence over the whole wilderness.

Sections IV-V Following the tracks

Alone, in snow, Simpson follows what is left. Défago’s prints run beside marks that are not a moose’s and not a bear’s, and as the trail goes on the distance between the man’s footfalls grows until it can no longer be walked. Then his prints begin to resemble the other set.

Section IV
Big, round, ample, with no pointed outline: nothing in the bush leaves that.
Section IV
The gap between prints widening past any possible step, and the snow between them untouched.
Section V
The man’s tracks lose their shape and take on the other’s, and the story stops being a disappearance.

Sections VI-IX Cathcart’s explanation, and Défago’s return

The party reunites and Cathcart sets out the rational account: suggestion, hysteria, a legend planted in a young mind by a superstitious guide. While he is still explaining, something comes into the firelight that answers to Défago’s name and does not answer to anything else. The last pages give the facts of his death, the cook’s flight, and no explanation at all.

Section VI
The doctor’s theory, offered with real intelligence and no effect whatever on what happens next.
Section VIII
A shrunken figure answering to Défago’s name comes into the firelight, talks of a hellfire trip, and is met by a stream of Hank’s oaths.
Section IX
The real Défago, found back at the main camp with moss in his cheeks and frozen feet that ‘burn like fire’, lingers a few weeks.
Section IX
The cook catches the odour as the guide comes into camp and starts at once for home, a three days’ journey.
