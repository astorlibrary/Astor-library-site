Wilderness

The bush in this story is simply larger than the people in it, and the story gives the exact distances again and again: grub for two days, a lake named for its fifty islands, three days’ walk home.

Through the book It is beautiful in the canoe chapters, indifferent by the camp, and by the tracking section it is a surface that records what happened without commenting on it.

In the text

A considerable number of hunting parties were out that year without finding so much as a fresh trail

Section I

It opens on an absence, and on men going home with stories. The whole tale is about what one party brought back instead of a trophy, and the first line puts the reader among people who exaggerate.

Isolation

The party is divided in the second section so that the worst night can happen with only one witness, and Simpson spends the middle of the story entirely alone.

Through the book Being alone makes his testimony doubtful, and the last three sections are about that doubt.

In the text

Oh! oh! This fiery height! Oh, my feet of fire! My burning feet of fire

Section V

The most quoted sentence Blackwood wrote, and it does two things: it tells the reader that the man is airborne, and it puts the pain of it in his own mouth. The exclamations are the only moment the prose loses its composure.

bereft of mind, memory and soul, had gone with it

Section IX

Three nouns, in order of increasing loss, and then the flat verb. For a story that never describes its monster, this is as close as it comes to saying what the monster does.

Simpson’s faith

Simpson is training for the ministry and reaches instinctively for prayer, and the story neither mocks him nor rewards him. His religion gives him a way to feel awe and no way to act on it.

Through the book He prays in the tent and tracks in the snow, and by the end his account has lost its religious vocabulary altogether.

In the text

Défago had “seen the Wendigo.”

Section IX

It closes on the local phrase, in quotation marks, untranslated into the doctor’s language or the student’s. After a whole story of competing explanations it ends by using the one it started with and declining to gloss it.

Explanations

A psychologist, a theology student, a bushman and a cook each know something about what is happening, and the story lets them contradict each other without appointing a winner.

Through the book Cathcart’s hallucination theory is the most sophisticated and the least useful; the cook’s, which is never argued, is the one the plot obeys.

In the text

the vagaries of the human mind

Section I

His subject is named before the case arrives, so his diagnosis is available from the start. Blackwood is playing fair: the psychological reading is offered by the text itself and not smuggled in.

for the Wendigo is simply the Call of the Wild personified, which some natures hear to their own destruction

Section VII

The doctor’s neatest formulation, offered to fill a pause; he does not believe it settles anything. It is a good line, it is almost certainly wrong, and Blackwood gives it to the cleverest man present.

Tracks in snow

Everything supernatural in the story arrives as a physical trace: prints, a smell, a burnt smell on the skin, a distance between footfalls that can be paced out and measured.

Through the book The trail is the story’s argument: it starts as evidence of a man, becomes evidence of something else, and its most frightening stretch is the part where the two sets converge.

In the text

the “odor of lions” is the phrase with which he usually sums it all up

Section IV

The comparison is wrong for a Canadian forest, which is the trouble: the nearest thing he can reach for is an animal from another continent. Blackwood repeats the phrase three times across the story, so a smell becomes the creature’s signature.

They were big, round, ample, and with no pointed outline as of sharp hoofs

Section IV

Blackwood describes the tracks by elimination, ruling out anything with hoofs. The adjectives are so neutral that they frighten more than a scream would, because the one thing the reader wants to know, what made them, is what the sentence withholds.

Rational explanation

Cathcart is the reader’s proxy, and Blackwood treats him fairly: his explanation covers most of the facts, and he keeps offering it while the thing he is explaining is in the firelight with him.

Through the book The narrator’s one dry remark (that this case never appeared in his book on Collective Hallucination) is all the verdict the story delivers.

In the text

This particular story, however, found no mention in his book on Collective Hallucination

Section I

The rationalist is given his credentials and his one omission in the same sentence, on page one. Everything he says for the next eight sections is read against a fact the reader has been handed in advance.

But Défago surely had already told you all these details of the Wendigo legend, my dear fellow

Section VI

The suggestion theory, put politely and in the form of a question. It is the obvious explanation, and Blackwood has it stated by a professional so that nobody can accuse the story of ducking it.

Borrowing the Wendigo

The Wendigo comes from Ojibwe and Cree tradition, where it carries meanings about famine, winter and the danger of appetite. Blackwood takes the name and the terror and leaves the meaning behind, and his Indigenous character is given knowledge but almost no speech.

Through the book The story’s frame treats Indigenous belief as evidence of something real and its Indigenous characters as atmosphere, and both are true of the story.

In the text

Wendigo’s somewhere around, doin’ a bit of swift traveling

Section II

The name arrives casually, in a guide’s idiom, as a piece of weather. The creature is introduced as workaday local knowledge, without any sense of revelation, and the later sections are unbearable because of it.

several Indians had “seen the Wendigo” along the shores of Fifty Island Water

Section VI

The information that would have changed everything arrives after the event, from the man who talked his friend into going. Blackwood puts local knowledge in quotation marks; that is how the story works, and it is also what critics most fairly object to.
