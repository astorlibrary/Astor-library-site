The Wendigo · Algernon Blackwood

Themes

Wilderness

The bush in this story is simply larger than the people in it, and the story gives the exact distances again and again: grub for two days, a lake named for its fifty islands, three days’ walk home.

It is beautiful in the canoe chapters, indifferent by the camp, and by the tracking section it is a surface that records what happened without commenting on it.

A considerable number of hunting parties were out that year without finding so much as a fresh trail

Section I

Isolation

The party is divided in the second section so that the worst night can happen with only one witness, and Simpson spends the middle of the story entirely alone.

Being alone makes his testimony doubtful, and the last three sections are about that doubt.

Oh! oh! This fiery height! Oh, my feet of fire! My burning feet of fire

Section IX

Simpson’s faith

Simpson is training for the ministry and reaches instinctively for prayer, and the story neither mocks him nor rewards him. His religion gives him a way to feel awe and no way to act on it.

He prays in the tent and tracks in the snow, and by the end his account has lost its religious vocabulary altogether.

Section IX

Explanations

A psychologist, a theology student, a bushman and a cook each know something about what is happening, and the story lets them contradict each other without appointing a winner.

Cathcart’s hallucination theory is the most sophisticated and the least useful; the cook’s, which is never argued, is the one the plot obeys.

This particular story, however, found no mention in his book on Collective Hallucination

Section I

Tracks in snow

Everything supernatural in the story arrives as a physical trace: prints, a smell, a burnt smell on the skin, a distance between footfalls that can be paced out and measured.

The trail is the story’s argument: it starts as evidence of a man, becomes evidence of something else, and its most frightening stretch is the part where the two sets converge.

the “odor of lions” is the phrase with which he usually sums it all up

Section V

Rational explanation

Cathcart is the reader’s proxy, and Blackwood treats him fairly: his explanation covers most of the facts, and he keeps offering it while the thing he is explaining is in the firelight with him.

The narrator’s one dry remark (that this case never appeared in his book on Collective Hallucination) is all the verdict the story delivers.

Section VII

Borrowing the Wendigo

The Wendigo comes from Ojibwe and Cree tradition, where it carries meanings about famine, winter and the danger of appetite. Blackwood takes the name and the terror and leaves the meaning behind, and his Indigenous character is given knowledge but almost no speech.

The story’s frame treats Indigenous belief as evidence of something real and its Indigenous characters as atmosphere, and both are true of the story.

Wendigo’s somewhere around, doin’ a bit of swift traveling
