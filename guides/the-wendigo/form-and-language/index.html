The Wendigo · Algernon Blackwood

Form and language

Precise setting

Setting described with the precision of someone who has worked in it.

Distances, portages, the weight of a canoe, the behaviour of moose in a bad season: Blackwood had lived this country, and the accuracy lets the one impossible detail land.

Tracks as evidence

A physical record that the characters and the reader read together.

Simpson first takes the huge prints by the tent for a bull moose’s, then measures a stride of eighteen feet beside Défago’s own. From then on the snow is a document, and the horror is a matter of shape and spacing.

They were big, round, ample, and with no pointed outline as of sharp hoofs

Section IV

Refrain

A line repeated at intervals, each time in a different mouth.

The cry about burning feet of fire comes down from the sky when Défago is carried off in section IV, again over Simpson’s search in section V, twice more over the camp in sections VII and VIII, and at last as a frostbitten man’s complaint that his feet burn like fire. Each repetition takes something away from the possibility of hallucination.

the “odor of lions” is the phrase with which he usually sums it all up

Section IV

Dialect

Rendering pronunciation and idiom on the page.

Hank’s swearing and clipped consonants set him against Cathcart’s careful Aberdeen sentences, so that the argument about what happened is also an argument between two kinds of English.

Wendigo’s somewhere around, doin’ a bit of swift traveling

Section II

The unseen monster

Keeping the central figure off the page.

There is no description of the Wendigo anywhere in the story, only a smell, a voice, tracks, and what is left of a man. The reader assembles it and is responsible for the result.

the “odor of lions” is the phrase with which he usually sums it all up

Section IV

Open-air horror

The old machinery of dread moved out of doors.

A tent instead of a castle, snow instead of a crypt, and a guide instead of a servant. The pressure is gothic and the furniture is a canoe, a fire and a pair of moccasins.

Witnesses

An account assembled afterwards from several people’s testimony.

The narrator sorts what Simpson said, what Hank admitted, what Cathcart would concede and what Punk contributed. The story is presented as a case file, which is why the doctor’s rejection of it matters.

A considerable number of hunting parties were out that year without finding so much as a fresh trail
