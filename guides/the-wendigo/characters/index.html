The Wendigo · Algernon Blackwood

Characters

Simpson

Cathcart’s nephew, a divinity student on his first trip into the bush; first appears Section I

Young, devout, entirely unprepared, and the only member of the party who sees the whole thing. His training gives him a vocabulary for awe and none at all for what is in front of him, and his honesty about that makes the account usable: he reports his terror as data and does not dress it up.

Joseph Défago

A French-Canadian guide, the best woodsman in the party; first appears Section I

Cheerful in a canoe, subject to fits of gloom nobody enquires about, and frightened of one particular lake. Blackwood makes him competent first and terrified second, so that his collapse tells us about the place more than about his character. What comes back wearing his name is the story’s worst moment.

Dr Cathcart

A psychologist from Aberdeen, author of a book on collective hallucination; first appears Section I

He arrives with the explanation already written and spends the story applying it: suggestion, hysteria, a legend planted in an impressionable mind. He is never made a fool of (his reasoning is good and his nerve holds), but the narrator notes drily that this particular case never made it into his book.

Hank Davis

The other guide, and Défago’s old partner; first appears Section I

Profane, practical and the only man who talks about the Wendigo as a thing you might meet. He blames himself for talking his friend into the trip, and he is the one who swears at the figure by the fire until it leaves.

Punk

The party’s cook, an Indian who keeps the base camp; first appears Section I

He has almost no lines and the story’s clearest judgement. He recognises the smell before anyone else, and when the shape walks in he leaves, covering three days’ travel without stopping. Blackwood gives the one man with inherited knowledge of the country no argument and no explanation, only the decision to go.

The Wendigo

Whatever calls Défago out of the tent; first appears Section IV

Never described except by its effects: a smell, a voice that knows your name, tracks that are not an animal’s, and a man who comes back changed. Blackwood borrows an Algonquian figure and strips it of the cultural setting that gives it meaning, which is the source of the story’s power and also the fairest charge against it.
