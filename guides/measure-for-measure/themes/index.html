Measure for Measure · William Shakespeare

Themes

Justice, mercy and measure

The title is a rule of equivalence from the Sermon on the Mount, and the play spends five acts testing whether human courts can operate it. Angelo applies the statute exactly and produces monstrosity; the Duke suspends it entirely and produces a city where liberty plucks justice by the nose. Neither extreme is offered as the answer.

Escalus pleads proportion in 2.1, Isabella pleads mercy in 2.2, and in 5.1 the Duke pronounces an eye-for-an-eye sentence he has already arranged not to carry out.

Spirits are not finely touch'd But to fine issues, nor Nature never lends The smallest scruple of her excellence But, like a thrifty goddess, she determines Herself the glory of a creditor,

Duke Vincentio, 1.1.35

Mortality and mercy in Vienna Live in thy tongue and heart: old Escalus, Though first in question, is thy secondary.

Duke Vincentio, 1.1.44

Authority and substitution

Everyone in the play stands in for someone else: a deputy for a duke, a friar for a deputy’s judge, a woman for another woman, a dead man’s head for a living man’s. The play’s political question is what happens to power when it is lent out, and whether the lender can ever really put it down.

Angelo receives the commission in 1.1, exceeds it by 2.4, and in 5.1 is tried by the man whose authority he was using.

Spirits are not finely touch'd But to fine issues, nor Nature never lends The smallest scruple of her excellence But, like a thrifty goddess, she determines Herself the glory of a creditor,

Duke Vincentio, 1.1.35

Duke Vincentio, 1.1.44

Desire and the law

Vienna’s statute treats sex as a matter of licensing, and the play answers by showing desire arriving where it was least expected, in a man who had built an identity on not having any. Angelo’s horror is real; what he does with it is the crime.

Lucio calls his blood snow-broth in 1.4; by 2.2 Angelo is asking who sins most, and by 4.4 he is wishing Claudio had lived while still excusing himself: we would, and we would not.

a man whose blood Is very snow-broth; one who never feels The wanton stings and motions of the sense, But doth rebate and blunt his natural edge With profits of the mind, study and fast.

Lucio, 1.4.57

Our doubts are traitors And makes us lose the good we oft might win By fearing to attempt.

Lucio, 1.4.77

Reputation

Angelo’s most chilling line is a statement of fact about testimony: his unsoiled name will outweigh her accusation, and he is right. The last act is built to prove him right for as long as possible, with Isabella dismissed as mad in front of a crowd before the truth is allowed out.

The threat is made in 2.4 and executed in 5.1, where both women are publicly disbelieved by a judge who knows exactly what happened.

a man whose blood Is very snow-broth; one who never feels The wanton stings and motions of the sense, But doth rebate and blunt his natural edge With profits of the mind, study and fast.

Lucio, 1.4.57

But man, proud man, Dressed in a little brief authority, Most ignorant of what he’s most assured, His glassy essence, like an angry ape Plays such fantastic tricks before high heaven As makes the angels weep;

Isabella, 2.2.128

Chastity, the body and exchange

Bodies in this play are currency: a maidenhead against a life, a marriage against a dowry lost at sea, a head against a head. Isabella’s refusal is usually discussed as a moral choice, but the play frames it as a refusal to accept a rate of exchange that everyone around her treats as normal.

The offer is made in 2.4, turned down in 3.1, and then satisfied in 4.1 by a substitution that pays with somebody else’s body.

Our doubts are traitors And makes us lose the good we oft might win By fearing to attempt.

Lucio, 1.4.77

I do arrest your words. Be that you are, That is, a woman; if you be more, you're none; If you be one, as you are well express'd By all external warrants, show it now, By putting on the destined livery.

Angelo, 2.4.136

Silence and consent

The play’s loudest moment is a gap in the text. Isabella has more lines than almost anyone up to Act V, argues her way through two interviews with a judge, and then receives a proposal of marriage and says nothing, twice. Whatever a production does with that silence becomes its reading of the whole play.

She kneels for Angelo’s life at Mariana’s plea in 5.1, and then answers neither of the Duke’s proposals; the Folio gives her no more words after that plea.

Then, Isabel, live chaste, and, brother, die: More than our brother is our chastity.

Isabella, 2.4.186

O you beast! O faithless coward! O dishonest wretch! Wilt thou be made a man out of my vice? Is't not a kind of incest, to take life From thine own sister's shame?

Isabella, 3.1.139
