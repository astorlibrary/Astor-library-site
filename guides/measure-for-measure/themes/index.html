Justice, mercy and measure

The title is a rule of equivalence from the Sermon on the Mount, and the play spends five acts testing whether human courts can operate it. Angelo applies the statute exactly and produces monstrosity; the Duke suspends it entirely and produces a city where liberty plucks justice by the nose. Neither extreme is offered as the answer.

Through the play Escalus pleads proportion in 2.1, Isabella pleads mercy in 2.2, and in 5.1 the Duke pronounces an eye-for-an-eye sentence he has already arranged not to carry out.

In the text

We must not make a scarecrow of the law, Setting it up to fear the birds of prey, And let it keep one shape, till custom make it Their perch and not their terror.

Angelo, 2.1.1

It is the best defence of enforcement anybody makes, and it is made with an image of birds settling comfortably on the thing meant to frighten them. Angelo’s argument is about how the law looks: he is worried about being laughed at, the same anxiety the Duke admitted in 1.3.

‘An Angelo for Claudio, death for death!’ Haste still pays haste, and leisure answers leisure; Like doth quit like, and MEASURE still FOR MEASURE.

Duke Vincentio, 5.1.412

The title arrives as a judicial formula in the mouth of a judge who has already prevented the death he is avenging. Everything in the line is balanced (haste against haste, like against like), and the balance is a performance, since Claudio is alive behind the scenes and the Duke knows it.

Authority and substitution

Everyone in the play stands in for someone else: a deputy for a duke, a friar for a deputy’s judge, a woman for another woman, a dead man’s head for a living man’s. The play’s political question is what happens to power when it is lent out, and whether the lender can ever really put it down.

Through the play Angelo receives the commission in 1.1, exceeds it by 2.4, and in 5.1 is tried by the man whose authority he was using.

In the text

Spirits are not finely touch’d But to fine issues, nor Nature never lends The smallest scruple of her excellence But, like a thrifty goddess, she determines Herself the glory of a creditor,

Duke Vincentio, 1.1.35

Virtue is described as a loan advanced on interest, and the metaphor governs the rest of the play: nothing in Vienna is given, everything is lent, weighed or repaid. The Duke is also, in the same breath, telling Angelo that a reputation which is never spent is worthless, and he is about to enforce the advice as an experiment.

I do arrest your words. Be that you are, That is, a woman; if you be more, you’re none; If you be one, as you are well express’d By all external warrants, show it now, By putting on the destined livery.

Angelo, 2.4.136

He conducts a seduction in the vocabulary of his office (arrest, warrants, livery), so the extortion is phrased entirely in legal terms. The trap is grammatical: she conceded a general frailty, and with the word ‘now’ he makes it a personal obligation.

Desire and the law

Vienna’s statute treats sex as a matter of licensing, and the play answers by showing desire arriving where it was least expected, in a man who had built an identity on not having any. Angelo’s horror is real; what he does with it is the crime.

Through the play Lucio calls his blood snow-broth in 1.4; by 2.2 Angelo is asking who sins most, and by 4.4 he is wishing Claudio had lived while still excusing himself: we would, and we would not.

In the text

a man whose blood Is very snow-broth; one who never feels The wanton stings and motions of the sense, But doth rebate and blunt his natural edge With profits of the mind, study and fast.

Lucio, 1.4.57

Melted snow is water that was once solid, so even the insult admits that something was made cold. Lucio is usually unreliable; here he is exactly right about the diagnosis and exactly wrong about the prognosis, which is the play’s method with him throughout.

What’s this, what’s this? Is this her fault or mine? The tempter or the tempted, who sins most? Ha! Not she: nor doth she tempt: but it is I

Angelo, 2.2.176

The question is asked and answered inside four lines, and he gets it right: she does not tempt, and the fault is his. The play never lets him plead ignorance afterwards, which is why what he does in 2.4 is a decision.

Reputation

Angelo’s most chilling line is a statement of fact about testimony: his unsoiled name will outweigh her accusation, and he is right. The last act is built to prove him right for as long as possible, with Isabella dismissed as mad in front of a crowd before the truth is allowed out.

Through the play The threat is made in 2.4 and executed in 5.1, where both women are publicly disbelieved by a judge who knows exactly what happened.

In the text

Who will believe thee, Isabel? My unsoil’d name, the austereness of my life, My vouch against you, and my place i’ the state, Will so your accusation overweigh, That you shall stifle in your own report

Angelo, 2.4.156

He does not deny that she could prove it; he explains that proof is not what decides such cases. ‘Overweigh’ returns the play to its scales, and in Act V the Duke will stage a hearing designed to demonstrate, at length and in public, that Angelo’s calculation was accurate.

O, what may man within him hide, Though angel on the outward side!

Duke Vincentio, 3.2.236

The metre changes to the short, chanting line Shakespeare gives to choruses and spirits, which lifts the Duke out of the action and into the position of a commentator on it. The pun on Angelo’s name has been waiting since the first scene, and it is sprung only once the plot to trap him is already in motion.

Chastity, the body and exchange

Bodies in this play are currency: a maidenhead against a life, a marriage against a dowry lost at sea, a head against a head. Isabella’s refusal is usually discussed as a moral choice, but the play frames it as a refusal to accept a rate of exchange that everyone around her treats as normal.

Through the play The offer is made in 2.4, turned down in 3.1, and then satisfied in 4.1 by a substitution that pays with somebody else’s body.

In the text

Then, Isabel, live chaste, and, brother, die: More than our brother is our chastity.

Isabella, 2.4.186

The decision is taken in a rhymed couplet, the form Shakespeare uses to close a scene and settle a question, so the verse itself insists that there is nothing left to discuss. She addresses herself and her absent brother in the same line, which is as close as the play comes to showing her weigh the two.

Ay, but to die, and go we know not where; To lie in cold obstruction and to rot; This sensible warm motion to become A kneaded clod; and the delighted spirit To bathe in fiery floods, or to reside

Claudio, 3.1.120

The fear is entirely physical until the fourth line, and the move from ‘warm motion’ to ‘kneaded clod’ is the whole argument: what terrifies him is the loss of warmth and movement. The speech begins with ‘Ay, but’: a man concedes the philosophy and then finds it does not reach.

Silence and consent

The play’s loudest moment is a gap in the text. Isabella has more lines than almost anyone up to Act V, argues her way through two interviews with a judge, and then receives a proposal of marriage and says nothing, twice. Whatever a production does with that silence becomes its reading of the whole play.

Through the play She kneels for Angelo’s life at Mariana’s plea in 5.1, and then answers neither of the Duke’s proposals; the Folio gives her no more words after that plea.

In the text

O you beast! O faithless coward! O dishonest wretch! Wilt thou be made a man out of my vice? Is’t not a kind of incest, to take life From thine own sister’s shame?

Isabella, 3.1.139

Three vocatives in a line and a half, each one shorter in patience than the last, and then a legal-sounding question that is not really a question. ‘Made a man’ is the cruellest phrase: she tells him that buying his life at this price would cost him his manhood as surely as it would cost her her chastity.

If he be like your brother, for his sake Is he pardon’d; and, for your lovely sake, Give me your hand and say you will be mine.

Duke Vincentio, 5.1.495

A pardon and a proposal are delivered in the same sentence, with the grammar making the second conditional on the goodwill created by the first. The Folio gives Isabella no reply here or at the second asking forty lines later, and every production has to decide what a woman does with a hand held out in front of a crowd.
