The Red Badge of Courage · Stephen Crane

Themes

Courage

Henry wants a fact about himself that can be established once and then relied on, and the novel never supplies one. Courage in these chapters is produced by crowds, by rage at an insult, by fear of being seen, and it never becomes a property a man can own between engagements.

The unanswerable question of Chapters I–II, the borrowed courage of the first stand, the flight, and the final charges made in a temper about the words ‘mule drivers’.

He was a mental outcast.

Chapter II

And there were iron laws of tradition and law on four sides. He was in a moving box.

Chapter III

Self-deception

Every action Henry takes is followed by a version of it in which he acted rightly, and Crane keeps the narration close enough to make the versions persuasive. The book’s real subject is how quickly a mind can manufacture the account it needs, more than whether he is brave.

The squirrel as proof in Chapter VII, the private hope for a defeat in Chapter XI, the wound accepted as a battle scar, and the settled narrative of Chapter XXIV with the tattered man still in it.

He was a mental outcast.

Chapter II

He felt that he was a fine fellow. He saw himself even with those ideals which he had considered as far beyond him.

Chapter VI

Manhood and gender

Henry is afraid of being seen as the kind of man who runs, more than of dying, and near the end the book gives him the title he has been chasing. His mother will not supply the heroic language he wants, and her flat advice comes back to correct the story.

The refused send-off in Chapter I, the shame of the unwounded man among the wounded in Chapter IX, the epithet ‘war devil’ in Chapter XVII, and ‘He was a man’ in Chapter XXIV.

Where yeh hit, ol’ boy?

The tattered soldier, Chapter VIII

It was not well to drive men into final corners; at those moments they could all develop teeth and claws.

Chapter XVII

The regiment

Individual nerve counts for less in this novel than the state of the thing a man is standing in. The regiment is described in the vocabulary of animals and machinery, it acquires moods before its members do, and a general can decide to spend it in one line of dialogue overheard by two of its privates.

The moving box of Chapter III, the welded common personality of Chapter V, the mule-driver conversation of Chapter XVIII, and the collective rage that carries the last charges.

The cold passed reluctantly from the earth, and the retiring fogs revealed an army stretched out on the hills, resting.

Chapter I

He had grown to regard himself merely as a part of a vast blue demonstration.

Chapter I

Nature's indifference

Henry keeps asking the landscape for a verdict, and it never gives one. He reads a fleeing squirrel as a law of self-preservation and walks straight into a chapel of trees with a rotting corpse in it, and the sun goes on standing in the sky like a pasted wafer over the death of his friend.

The religion of peace in Chapter VII, the dead man among the pines, the red sun over Jim Conklin’s body, and the sunlight in the last paragraph, which means nothing in particular.

The cold passed reluctantly from the earth, and the retiring fogs revealed an army stretched out on the hills, resting.

Chapter I

There was the law, he said. Nature had given him a sign.

Chapter VII

Confusion in battle

No battle is named, no campaign explained, no cause argued. What the private knows is smoke, noise, shouted contradictions and the men within a few yards of him, and the novel’s method is to refuse the reader any information Henry does not have.

Rumour in Chapter I, the local and partial fighting of Chapters IV–VI, the wandering of Chapters VII–XII, and the absence at the end of any statement about who won.

He had grown to regard himself merely as a part of a vast blue demonstration.

To the youth it was an onslaught of redoubtable dragons.
