Courage

Henry wants a fact about himself that can be established once and then relied on, and the novel never supplies one. Courage in these chapters is produced by crowds, by rage at an insult, by fear of being seen, and it never becomes a property a man can own between engagements.

Through the book The unanswerable question of Chapters I–II, the borrowed courage of the first stand, the flight, and the final charges made in a temper about the words ‘mule drivers’.

In the text

Where yeh hit, ol’ boy?

The tattered soldier, Chapter VIII

It is the friendliest sentence in the novel and the one Henry cannot bear, because among these men the wound is the credential and he has none. The question is asked twice more in the next chapters, and it is what finally drives him to leave a badly hurt man alone in a field.

It was not well to drive men into final corners; at those moments they could all develop teeth and claws.

Chapter XVII

The proposition is offered as general wisdom and is in fact a threat made by a frightened man to nobody in particular. Crane has been describing armies as animals since Chapter II, and here the imagery finally arrives inside Henry’s own account of himself.

Self-deception

Every action Henry takes is followed by a version of it in which he acted rightly, and Crane keeps the narration close enough to make the versions persuasive. The book’s real subject is how quickly a mind can manufacture the account it needs, more than whether he is brave.

Through the book The squirrel as proof in Chapter VII, the private hope for a defeat in Chapter XI, the wound accepted as a battle scar, and the settled narrative of Chapter XXIV with the tattered man still in it.

In the text

He had fled, he told himself, because annihilation approached.

Chapter VII

‘He told himself’ is the whole technique in three words: the sentence reports a proposition without adopting it. The Latinate ‘annihilation’ is several registers above anything Henry says out loud, which marks the reasoning as a performance for an audience of one.

He found that he could look back upon the brass and bombast of his earlier gospels and see them truly.

Chapter XXIV

He congratulates himself on having seen through his own self-congratulation, which is the same operation one level up. The novel’s difficulty is concentrated here: nothing in the sentence is false, and nothing in it is evidence, since the man assessing the account is the man who wrote it.

Manhood and gender

Henry is afraid of being seen as the kind of man who runs, more than of dying, and near the end the book gives him the title he has been chasing. His mother will not supply the heroic language he wants, and her flat advice comes back to correct the story.

Through the book The refused send-off in Chapter I, the shame of the unwounded man among the wounded in Chapter IX, the epithet ‘war devil’ in Chapter XVII, and ‘He was a man’ in Chapter XXIV.

In the text

It was a woman, red and white, hating and loving, that called him with the voice of his hopes.

Chapter XIX

The flag gets the only romantic attachment in the novel, and Henry calls it invulnerable because he needs something in the field that cannot be killed. It is described as a woman, and by its own colours, red and white.

He had been to touch the great death, and found that, after all, it was but the great death. He was a man.

Chapter XXIV

The repetition of ‘the great death’ drains the phrase between its first appearance and its second, which is either maturity or the deflation of a boy’s word. Then four monosyllables award him the title he wanted in Chapter I, and Crane does not say whether he deserves it.

The regiment

Individual nerve counts for less in this novel than the state of the thing a man is standing in. The regiment is described in the vocabulary of animals and machinery, it acquires moods before its members do, and a general can decide to spend it in one line of dialogue overheard by two of its privates.

Through the book The moving box of Chapter III, the welded common personality of Chapter V, the mule-driver conversation of Chapter XVIII, and the collective rage that carries the last charges.

In the text

And there were iron laws of tradition and law on four sides. He was in a moving box.

Chapter III

The image makes a body of comrades into a container with walls, and repeating ‘law’ inside one clause makes the enclosure sound legal. It also sets up the chapters ahead: when he does escape the box, he finds that there is nowhere outside it to be.

He suddenly lost concern for himself, and forgot to look at a menacing fate. He became not a man but a member.

Chapter V

Courage arrives here as a loss: first of self-consciousness, then of a separate self. The pun on ‘member’ is meant: he is briefly a limb of something larger, which is the condition he cannot reproduce when he is alone in Chapter VI.

Nature’s indifference

Henry keeps asking the landscape for a verdict, and it never gives one. He reads a fleeing squirrel as a law of self-preservation and walks straight into a chapel of trees with a rotting corpse in it, and the sun goes on standing in the sky like a pasted wafer over the death of his friend.

Through the book The religion of peace in Chapter VII, the dead man among the pines, the red sun over Jim Conklin’s body, and the sunlight in the last paragraph, which means nothing in particular.

In the text

There was the law, he said. Nature had given him a sign.

Chapter VII

He has conducted an experiment designed to produce the result he wanted and calls the result a law. Crane lets the reasoning stand for a page and then walks him into a dead man’s face, which is nature’s other sign and is not addressed to him at all.

Over the river a golden ray of sun came through the hosts of leaden rain clouds.

Chapter XXIV

It is the shape of a consoling ending without the content of one: a ray of sun, a river, and no statement about what any of it means. ‘Hosts’ is the only word carrying any military weight, and it belongs to the clouds: the weather keeps the vocabulary of armies, and the armies have marched off the page.

Confusion in battle

No battle is named, no campaign explained, no cause argued. What the private knows is smoke, noise, shouted contradictions and the men within a few yards of him, and the novel’s method is to refuse the reader any information Henry does not have.

Through the book Rumour in Chapter I, the local and partial fighting of Chapters IV–VI, the wandering of Chapters VII–XII, and the absence at the end of any statement about who won.

In the text

He had grown to regard himself merely as a part of a vast blue demonstration.

Chapter I

A war is reduced to a colour and a show, as though the Union army were a piece of political theatre and he an extra in it. ‘Merely’ belongs to Henry’s point of view, and it registers the disappointment of a boy who enlisted for something with names in it.

To the youth it was an onslaught of redoubtable dragons.

Chapter VI

The phrase ‘to the youth’ matters: Crane marks the image as Henry’s own, so the dragons are a boy’s reading matter arriving on a battlefield. He will use the same creatures later to congratulate himself on having met them, which is how the novel measures what he has learned.
