The Red Badge of Courage · Stephen Crane

Form and language

Free indirect style

Third-person narration that takes on the vocabulary and bias of the character it follows.

The prose adopts Henry’s reasoning without endorsing it, so his case for running is made in his own inflated terms while the sentences leave the reader room to reject it.

He had grown to regard himself merely as a part of a vast blue demonstration.

Chapter I

Colour as feeling

Colour used to register emotional and physical states, more than to describe appearance.

Red eyes across the river, the red sickness of battle, the red badge itself, a blue demonstration, a dead man’s uniform faded to green: colour arrives before information does, which keeps the reader at the private’s eye level.

The cold passed reluctantly from the earth, and the retiring fogs revealed an army stretched out on the hills, resting.

Chapter I

Animal imagery

Men and formations described as creatures.

Dragons advance, the army is a worm, war is a red animal and a blood-swollen god, and men develop teeth and claws when cornered, and heroism drops out of the vocabulary altogether.

To the youth it was an onslaught of redoubtable dragons.

Chapter VI

Irony

Saying one thing in a form that invites the opposite judgement.

The wound that certifies Henry’s courage is given to him by a terrified man on his own side, and the regiment’s finest hour is arranged by a general who chose it as the unit he could best spare.

He felt that he was a fine fellow. He saw himself even with those ideals which he had considered as far beyond him.

Chapter VI

Naming by epithet

Characters identified by a single visible attribute instead of a name.

The youth, the tall soldier, the loud soldier, the tattered man: the labels are how an army sees its own people, and Crane withholds Henry’s name for most of the book so that the reader inherits that view.

Where yeh hit, ol’ boy?

The tattered soldier, Chapter VIII

Simile

An explicit comparison, usually brief, doing the work of a longer description.

The most famous sentence in the book compares the sun to a wafer pasted in the sky. It stands alone as a paragraph immediately after a death, and says nothing more.

The red sun was pasted in the sky like a wafer.
