The Red Badge of Courage · Stephen Crane

Characters

Henry Fleming

A private in the 304th New York, called ‘the youth’ for most of the book; first appears Chapter I

He enlisted for a war he had read about and finds one made of smoke, rumour and other men’s faces. Everything that happens to him happens twice: once as an event and again, shortly afterwards, as a version of the event in which he comes off better, and Crane keeps both on the page.

Jim Conklin

The tall soldier; first appears Chapter I

He starts the novel by carrying a rumour through the camp on the strength of a washed shirt, and he ends it in a field, refusing help and looking for a place to fall down. Crane gives him the one death in the book described in full, and no consoling language whatever.

Wilson

The loud soldier; first appears Chapter I

He begins by announcing his own courage and handing Henry a packet of letters for his family, and comes back from the first day quiet, useful and no longer interested in being watched. He is the novel’s evidence that a man can change without narrating the change to himself.

The tattered soldier

A wounded man in the column; first appears Chapter VIII

He is friendly, badly hurt and losing his grip, and he keeps asking Henry the one question Henry cannot answer: where are you hit? Henry leaves him wandering in a field, and the memory is the only thing in the last chapter that the new self-account cannot absorb.

Henry's mother

A farm widow in New York State; first appears Chapter I

She refuses to give her son the send-off he has imagined, keeps peeling potatoes while he announces his enlistment, and tells him to watch out and to remember that he is nothing special in an army of thousands. Her advice is the most accurate assessment of his situation in the novel.

Lieutenant Hasbrouck

The regiment’s officer; first appears Chapter IV

He swears at his men, drags them forward by the collar, and is the first person to see Henry fight. He is also the one who passes Henry’s name upward, which is how a private’s private crisis becomes a matter of official record.

The 304th New York

Henry’s regiment, treated throughout as a single creature; first appears Chapter I

Crane describes it as a box, a machine, a worm and a dragon, and gives it moods of its own that run ahead of any individual’s decision. It is the thing that makes Henry brave in Chapter V and the thing he cannot escape in Chapter III, and a general disposes of it in a sentence as the unit he can best spare.
