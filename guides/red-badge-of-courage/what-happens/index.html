The Red Badge of Courage · Stephen Crane

What happens

Chapters I–III Waiting in camp

The regiment has been camped for months and lives on contradictory reports of a coming move. Henry Fleming, who enlisted against his mother’s advice and expecting something Homeric, discovers that the only question that interests him is whether he will run, and that he cannot answer it by reasoning or by watching other men.

Chapter I
A washed shirt brings news of a march; Henry remembers enlisting, and his mother peeling potatoes instead of making a speech.
Chapter II
He tries to find another man as frightened as he is, and concludes that he is alone in it.
Chapter III
Knapsacks are thrown away along the road, and Henry realises the regiment cannot be left.

Chapters IV–VI The first battle, and Henry runs

The first assault comes and Henry fires with everybody else, absorbed into the line and briefly free of himself. When a second wave arrives and men on either side begin to go, he goes too. He runs fast, before any argument has taken place in him, and looks back to find that the regiment held.

Chapters IV–V
Henry loses his separate self in the regiment and discovers that courage can be produced by a crowd.
Chapter VI
He runs like an insane sprinter, and learns from an officer’s shouting that the position was not lost.

Chapters VII–XII The forest, the wounded column, the blow

Alone in the woods, Henry builds a case for his own wisdom out of a squirrel and a quiet glade, and then walks into a chapel of pine boughs containing a corpse. He joins a column of wounded men, watches Jim Conklin die, abandons the tattered soldier, and is finally struck on the head by a panicking man from another regiment.

Chapter VII
A squirrel runs from a thrown pine cone, which Henry takes as a law; the dead man in the chapel is the reply.
Chapters VIII–IX
The procession of the wounded, the tattered man’s questions, and the tall soldier’s death in a field.
Chapters X–XII
Henry deserts the tattered soldier, hopes privately for a defeat that would vindicate him, and is clubbed with a rifle in the retreat.

Chapters XIII–XVIII Back with the regiment

A cheerful stranger walks him back to his own fires, where the regiment assumes his head wound is a graze from a bullet and Wilson nurses him. Henry keeps the account, finds that the loud soldier has become a serious man, fires on after the enemy has gone until the others call him a war devil, and overhears an officer tell a general that the regiment fights like mule drivers.

Chapters XIII–XIV
Wilson binds his head and gives up his blanket; nobody asks how the wound was made.
Chapters XV–XVII
Henry holds Wilson’s envelope of last words over him, and then fights like a wild man while the regiment watches.
Chapter XVIII
Two officers discuss the regiment as an expendable unit within earshot of two of its men.

Chapters XIX–XXIV Henry carries the flag

Henry takes the colours from a dying sergeant and carries them through two charges, and hears that the colonel and his lieutenant have praised him by name. In the last chapter he assembles all of it, including the tattered soldier, into an account of himself he can live with.

Chapters XIX–XXI
He seizes the flag, the charge falls short, and the regiment is jeered and then defended.
Chapters XXII–XXIII
A stand under fire, a captured flag, and four prisoners.
Chapter XXIV
Marching away in the rain, Henry reviews his deeds, flinches at the memory of the tattered man, and decides he is a man.
