The War of the Worlds · H. G. Wells

Themes

Imperial reversal

Wells puts readers at the centre of the largest empire on Earth in the other position. Wells makes the argument explicit in the first chapter, naming the extermination of the Tasmanians before a single cylinder has landed, so that everything the Martians do afterwards has already been described as something Europeans did first.

Stated as history in Book I, Chapter I; enacted as crowded roads, refugees and abandoned homes in the exodus chapters; and left unresolved at the end, because the deliverance is bacterial and has nothing to do with merit.

Are we such apostles of mercy as to complain if the Martians warred in the same spirit?

Book I, Chapter I

It was the beginning of the rout of civilisation, of the massacre of mankind.

Book I, Chapter XVII

Dethronement

The invasion’s real damage is to a belief, more than a city: the belief that human beings are the point of the planet. The narrator dates his own loss of it exactly, standing among the red weed and realising that he has become an animal among animals, and the epilogue shows the feeling has not lifted.

Complacency in Chapter I, terror through most of the book, and then a settled and permanent change of position in Book II, Chapters VI and X.

No one would have believed in the last years of the nineteenth century that this world was being watched keenly and closely by intelligences greater than man’s and yet as mortal as his own; that as men busied themselves about their various concerns they were scrutinised and studied, perhaps almost as narrowly as a man with a microscope might scrutinise the transient creatures that swarm and multiply in a drop of water.

Book I, Chapter I

Yet across the gulf of space, minds that are to our minds as ours are to those of the beasts that perish, intellects vast and cool and unsympathetic, regarded this Earth with envious eyes, and slowly and surely drew their plans against us.

Book I, Chapter I

Technological asymmetry

No act of courage changes the course of this war. The Heat-Ray kills before it is understood, the Black Smoke is applied like a weedkiller, and the human successes are a lucky shell at Shepperton and the Thunder Child, a ship that runs straight at the machines and blows up doing it.

Artillery at Horsell, a lucky shell at Shepperton, an ironclad off the Essex coast: three attempts, and none of them alters the outcome.

It was sweeping round swiftly and steadily, this flaming death, this invisible, inevitable sword of heat.

Book I, Chapter V

A monstrous tripod, higher than many houses, striding over the young pine trees, and smashing them aside in its career; a walking engine of glittering metal, striding now across the heather; articulate ropes of steel dangling from it, and the clattering tumult of its passage mingling with the riot of the thunder.

Book I, Chapter X

Witness and report

Everything in the book reaches the reader through somebody who was there and did not understand it at the time. Telegraph, newspaper, rumour and eyewitness are all shown failing in different ways, and the narrator’s authority comes from admitting how much of his account is reconstruction.

A paragraph in the Daily Telegraph, then contradictory special editions, then no papers at all; and behind them the brother’s narrative, which the narrator can only have received second-hand.

Evolution and the humblest things

The novel’s science is Darwinian before it is mechanical. Mars is old and cooling, its inhabitants have specialised themselves into brains with hands, and Earth’s advantage turns out to be an immune system bought by a billion deaths nobody remembers.

The cooling planet in Chapter I, the red weed rotting in Book II, and the Martians dead in the redoubt without a shot fired at them.

Yet across the gulf of space, minds that are to our minds as ours are to those of the beasts that perish, intellects vast and cool and unsympathetic, regarded this Earth with envious eyes, and slowly and surely drew their plans against us.

Book I, Chapter I

A big greyish rounded bulk, the size, perhaps, of a bear, was rising slowly and painfully out of the cylinder. As it bulged up and caught the light, it glistened like wet leather.

Book I, Chapter IV

Faith, explanation and nerve

Three ways of meeting a catastrophe are set against each other: the curate’s apocalypse, the artilleryman’s grand programme and the narrator’s habit of writing down what he can see. Two of them are exposed, and the third is not offered as heroism, only as the one that leaves a record.

The curate breaks in Book I, Chapter XIII and is silenced in Book II, Chapter IV; the artilleryman is believed for a morning and abandoned that night.
