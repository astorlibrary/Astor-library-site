Imperial reversal

Wells puts readers at the centre of the largest empire on Earth in the other position. Wells makes the argument explicit in the first chapter, naming the extermination of the Tasmanians before a single cylinder has landed, so that everything the Martians do afterwards has already been described as something Europeans did first.

Through the book Stated as history in Book I, Chapter I; enacted as crowded roads, refugees and abandoned homes in the exodus chapters; and left unresolved at the end, because the deliverance is bacterial and has nothing to do with merit.

In the text

Are we such apostles of mercy as to complain if the Martians warred in the same spirit?

Book I, Chapter I

The argument of the whole novel is made in a rhetorical question before the first cylinder has landed. It disarms the reader in advance: any outrage felt over the next two hundred pages has already been booked against an account the reader is on the wrong side of.

It was the beginning of the rout of civilisation, of the massacre of mankind.

Book I, Chapter XVII

Rout is a military word applied to a population that was never an army, and the sentence puts civilisation and mankind in the same grammatical position as a broken regiment. Wells reaches this judgement only after six pages of specific roads and named suburbs, so the abstraction has been earned.

Dethronement

The invasion’s real damage is to a belief, more than a city: the belief that human beings are the point of the planet. The narrator dates his own loss of it exactly, standing among the red weed and realising that he has become an animal among animals, and the epilogue shows the feeling has not lifted.

Through the book Complacency in Chapter I, terror through most of the book, and then a settled and permanent change of position in Book II, Chapters VI and X.

In the text

No one would have believed in the last years of the nineteenth century that this world was being watched keenly and closely by intelligences greater than man’s and yet as mortal as his own; that as men busied themselves about their various concerns they were scrutinised and studied, perhaps almost as narrowly as a man with a microscope might scrutinise the transient creatures that swarm and multiply in a drop of water.

Book I, Chapter I

The sentence is built so that the reader is inside the microscope before they know there is one: men are the subject of the verbs for half its length and the object of them by the end. Wells also dates his own book from the future, which guarantees that somebody survived to write it.

I was no longer a master, but an animal among the animals, under the Martian heel. With us it would be as with them, to lurk and watch, to run and hide; the fear and empire of man had passed away.

Book II, Chapter VI

The realisation is triggered by the most ordinary sight in the book, men building a house, and the tenses give it away: it would be as with them, the future of a man who has already accepted the position. Empire is the exact word, and it is the same empire the first chapter warned about.

Technological asymmetry

No act of courage changes the course of this war. The Heat-Ray kills before it is understood, the Black Smoke is applied like a weedkiller, and the human successes are a lucky shell at Shepperton and the Thunder Child, a ship that runs straight at the machines and blows up doing it.

Through the book Artillery at Horsell, a lucky shell at Shepperton, an ironclad off the Essex coast: three attempts, and none of them alters the outcome.

In the text

It was sweeping round swiftly and steadily, this flaming death, this invisible, inevitable sword of heat.

Book I, Chapter V

The weapon is named with a word from an older war, sword, because nothing in the narrator’s vocabulary fits it. Every adjective is about motion and none about appearance, which is accurate: he is describing something he cannot see, only infer from what catches fire.

A monstrous tripod, higher than many houses, striding over the young pine trees, and smashing them aside in its career; a walking engine of glittering metal, striding now across the heather; articulate ropes of steel dangling from it, and the clattering tumult of its passage mingling with the riot of the thunder.

Book I, Chapter X

The machine is assembled in front of the reader out of participles, each clause adding one moving part, because the narrator sees it only in flashes of lightning. A page later he reaches for a milking stool to explain it, and the drop in register is the most honest thing in the chapter.

Witness and report

Everything in the book reaches the reader through somebody who was there and did not understand it at the time. Telegraph, newspaper, rumour and eyewitness are all shown failing in different ways, and the narrator’s authority comes from admitting how much of his account is reconstruction.

Through the book A paragraph in the Daily Telegraph, then contradictory special editions, then no papers at all; and behind them the brother’s narrative, which the narrator can only have received second-hand.

In the text

The chances against anything manlike on Mars are a million to one

Ogilvy, Book I, Chapter I

The professional is being reasonable with the evidence he has, and that is why the line frightens instead of amusing. Wells gives expertise one confident sentence and then kills the man who says it four chapters later.

I go to London and see the busy multitudes in Fleet Street and the Strand, and it comes across my mind that they are but the ghosts of the past, haunting the streets that I have seen silent and wretched, going to and fro, phantasms in a dead city, the mockery of life in a galvanised body.

Book II, Chapter X

The tenses have gone to the present: this is something that goes on happening, more than a memory. The living crowd is described as the ghost and the emptied street as the real one, which inverts the usual arrangement of a haunting.

Evolution and the humblest things

The novel’s science is Darwinian before it is mechanical. Mars is old and cooling, its inhabitants have specialised themselves into brains with hands, and Earth’s advantage turns out to be an immune system bought by a billion deaths nobody remembers.

Through the book The cooling planet in Chapter I, the red weed rotting in Book II, and the Martians dead in the redoubt without a shot fired at them.

In the text

A big greyish rounded bulk, the size, perhaps, of a bear, was rising slowly and painfully out of the cylinder. As it bulged up and caught the light, it glistened like wet leather.

Book I, Chapter IV

The first alien in English fiction to be described this closely is measured against a bear and a piece of wet leather, both things a Woking crowd would have handled. Wells withholds the eyes for another sentence, so the thing is an object before it is a gaze.

We’re eatable ants.

The artilleryman, Book II, Chapter VII

Three words, and the adjective is the horror: ants are cleared out of the way, but these ones are also food. It is the only moment in the novel where the human position is stated as a plain biological fact.

Faith, explanation and nerve

Three ways of meeting a catastrophe are set against each other: the curate’s apocalypse, the artilleryman’s grand programme and the narrator’s habit of writing down what he can see. Two of them are exposed, and the third is not offered as heroism, only as the one that leaves a record.

Through the book The curate breaks in Book I, Chapter XIII and is silenced in Book II, Chapter IV; the artilleryman is believed for a morning and abandoned that night.

In the text

This must be the beginning of the end

The curate, Book I, Chapter XIII

He has a framework ready and reaches for it immediately, which spares him the work of looking. The novel sets his apocalypse against the narrator’s habit of counting cylinders and noting times, and only one of the two men is capable of learning anything new.

Cities, nations, civilisation, progress⁠—it’s all over. That game’s up. We’re beat.

The artilleryman, Book II, Chapter VII

Everything the nineteenth century believed in is listed and dismissed in a sporting idiom. The energy of the speech is what persuades the narrator for a night, and Wells then shows the same man unable to finish a trench, so that the rhetoric and the labour can be compared directly.
