The War of the Worlds · H. G. Wells

What happens

Book I, Chapters I-IV A light on Mars, and a cylinder in a sandpit

Ten flashes are seen on Mars and reported as a curiosity. The first cylinder lands at night on Horsell Common, a crowd gathers round the pit as it would round a beached whale, and the lid begins to unscrew while the narrator watches from the heather.

Book I, I
The narrator, writing afterwards, describes a world being studied by minds it has never thought about.
Book I, II
The cylinder comes down in the small hours and Ogilvy is the first man to reach it.
Book I, III
A holiday crowd assembles round the pit and the newspapers get a paragraph.
Book I, IV
The first Martian hauls itself out and the watchers discover what they have come to see.

Book I, Chapters V-VIII The Heat-Ray, and the evening papers

A deputation walks towards the pit under a white flag and is burned where it stands. The narrator gets home, tells his wife, and finds that the rest of England is still treating the matter as local news; just after midnight the second cylinder falls into the pine woods to the north-west.

Book I, V
An invisible beam kills the deputation and sets the common alight.
Book I, VI
The crowd on the road is caught in the open.
Book I, VII
The narrator walks back through burning heather and finds his own dining room unchanged.
Book I, VIII
London reads a short paragraph and goes to bed.

Book I, Chapters IX-XIV The fighting-machines, and the brother’s story begins

The fighting-machines rise and the military answer is over almost before it begins. The narrator drives his wife to Leatherhead, comes back into the storm, drifts down the river from burning Weybridge to meet the curate near Walton, and the narrative opens a second front with his brother in London.

Book I, IX
Guns are dug in on the common; the narrator takes his wife out of Woking.
Book I, X
A tripod overtakes the dog cart in the lightning.
Book I, XI
Soldiers pass the house and the artilleryman tells what happened to his battery.
Book I, XII
A Martian is brought down by artillery, and the river boils.
Book I, XIII
A clergyman reads the invasion as the day of judgement and cannot stop talking.
Book I, XIV
The narrator’s brother finds the capital calm, then reads the morning papers.

Book I, Chapters XV-XVII Six million people on the roads

The Black Smoke clears the ground in front of the machines and London empties north and east. With the brother’s chapters the novel widens from one man’s escape to a whole population in motion, and the Thunder Child gives the book its last moment of successful resistance.

Book I, XV
The Black Smoke, heavier than any smoke, kills all that breathes it; only roofs and upper storeys escape.
Book I, XVI
The brother joins the stampede and gets two women out through Barnet to the coast.
Book I, XVII
A torpedo ram rams a fighting-machine and covers the escaping steamers.

Book II, Chapters I-V Trapped with the curate

A fifth cylinder buries the narrator and the curate in a half-collapsed house at Sheen, with a Martian pit on the other side of the wall. For a fortnight the two men watch the invaders feed, ration a dwindling larder and quarrel, until the curate’s noise forces the narrator to stop him.

Book II, I
The narrator is hunted through Sheen and takes shelter in an empty house.
Book II, II
Through a crack in the wall the Martians are seen taking human blood.
Book II, III
Hunger, thirst and the curate’s voice become the immediate danger.
Book II, IV
The narrator strikes him with the handle of a chopper, and a Martian takes the body.
Book II, V
The pit is abandoned and the narrator crawls out into a red landscape.

Book II, Chapters VI-VII The red weed, and the artilleryman

Walking through the red weed, the narrator works out that he is no longer a master but an animal among animals. On Putney Hill he meets the artilleryman again, listens for a night to a magnificent scheme for living underground and rebuilding the species, then looks at the trench the man has actually dug.

Book II, VI
The red weed has taken the Thames valley and the narrator understands what has happened to his species.
Book II, VII
The artilleryman’s underground future, his champagne, and his unfinished tunnel.

Book II, Chapters VIII-X Dead London, and the epilogue

The narrator walks into an empty capital following a howling that stops mid-note, climbs Primrose Hill and finds the Martians dead in their redoubt. The last chapter is written years later by a man who cannot walk down a road without seeing it silent.

Book II, VIII
The cry of Ulla, ulla ends, and the invaders are found killed by bacteria.
Book II, IX
The narrator breaks down, is taken in by strangers in St John’s Wood, and gets home to Woking.
Book II, X
Unfinished science, a warning about the next opposition, and a man who wakes cold in the night.
