Antony and Cleopatra · William Shakespeare

Characters

Mark Antony

Triumvir of Rome, general; first appears 1.1

A soldier whose reputation is largely made of other people’s memories of him, and who keeps being asked to live up to a version of himself that is already historical. He is generous, careless and consistently late, and the play’s tenderness towards him comes from watching a man lose the shape he is supposed to hold.

Cleopatra

Queen of Egypt; first appears 1.1

She is never off duty: every mood is real and also calculated, and Shakespeare never separates the two. Romans put her political intelligence down to caprice, because they cannot see that testing a messenger, staging a faint and declining a triumph are all the same skill.

Octavius Caesar

Triumvir, later sole ruler of Rome; first appears 1.4

Young, dry and entirely without appetite, he wins because he is the only person in the play who never mistakes a gesture for an achievement. Shakespeare gives him no soliloquy at all, so the audience is never invited inside a mind that runs the world.

Domitius Enobarbus

Antony’s lieutenant and closest follower; first appears 1.2

He talks to the audience in prose, sees through everyone including himself, and produces the one speech in the play that makes Cleopatra indisputable. His defection is a reasoned decision that kills him, which is the play’s sharpest comment on reason.

Octavia

Caesar’s sister, Antony’s second wife; first appears 2.3

A treaty in human form, and the play knows it: she is praised for holy, cold and still conversation by men deciding what to do with her. Her few speeches are attempts to be a person in an arrangement that needs her not to be.

Charmian

Cleopatra’s attendant; first appears 1.2

She jokes with a soothsayer in the second scene and closes the queen’s eyes in the last, and the play gives her the final judgement on Cleopatra’s life. Straightening the crown on a dead woman’s head before dying herself is the only stage direction the ending needs.

Sextus Pompeius

Commander of the fleet, the triumvirs’ opponent at sea; first appears 2.1

He has the strongest navy and the weakest stomach for what winning would require. Offered the world by a pirate who only needs permission to cut a rope, he explains that the deed would have been honourable if he had not been told about it.

Lepidus

The third triumvir; first appears 1.4

His job is to agree with whoever is speaking and to keep begging the others not to quarrel. He is carried drunk off Pompey’s galley and deposed offstage a few scenes later, which is how the play measures the value of a share in power without the means to defend it.
