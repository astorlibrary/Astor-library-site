Rome and Egypt

Philo’s opening speech sets out the opposition (discipline against appetite, measure against excess), and the play then spends five acts making it unusable. Rome is as theatrical as Alexandria; it has simply trained its performance to look like plain fact.

Through the play Philo states the Roman case before Antony has spoken; by Act V Caesar is arranging a procession and Cleopatra is declining a part in it, and both are staging.

In the text

Nay, but this dotage of our general’s O’erflows the measure: those his goodly eyes, That o’er the files and musters of the war Have glow’d like plated Mars, now bend, now turn, The office and devotion of their view Upon a tawny front

Philo, 1.1.1

The audience is given the Roman verdict before it is given a single fact, and the verb is the charge: measure is the Roman unit of everything, and Antony is accused of exceeding it. The criticism is grammatically a compliment gone wrong: the eyes still glow, but they have been turned elsewhere.

the quick comedians Extemporally will stage us, and present Our Alexandrian revels; Antony Shall be brought drunken forth, and I shall see Some squeaking Cleopatra boy my greatness I’ the posture of a whore.

Cleopatra, 5.2.240

The line was first spoken by a boy actor playing Cleopatra, so the theatre is required to admit its own limits at the very moment she rejects them. What she fears is a bad performance, and the fear drives her to write a better final scene.

Measure and excess

Counting is the Roman virtue: ships, legions, kingdoms, the terms of a treaty. Antony and Cleopatra answer in a vocabulary of boundlessness (new heavens, melting empires, an autumn that grew the more by reaping), and the play will not say which language is lying.

Through the play Antony refuses to have love reckoned in 1.1; Caesar ends by counting the pair into a single grave and a solemn show.

In the text

There’s beggary in the love that can be reckon’d.

Mark Antony, 1.1.16

Her question is a piece of accounting and his answer throws the ledger away, which sets the terms of the whole play. ‘Reckon’d’ carries both counting and being called to account, so the line turns down two different Roman demands in one word.

The crown o’ the earth doth melt. My lord! O, wither’d is the garland of the war, The soldier’s pole is fall’n: young boys and girls Are level now with men; the odds is gone, And there is nothing left remarkable Beneath the visiting moon.

Cleopatra, 4.15.67

Grief is expressed as the collapse of every scale of difference: with the pole gone, children and men measure the same. ‘The odds is gone’ is flat, almost colloquial, in the middle of the grandest lament in the play, and it tells you what she thinks she has lost: a standard to measure by, as well as a lover.

Performance and self-presentation

Everyone here is managing an image: Cleopatra openly, Caesar invisibly, Antony badly. The most dangerous thing Cleopatra imagines in the last act is a bad performance of herself, by a squeaking boy actor in Rome.

Through the play Play-acting is flirtation in Act I, propaganda in Act III, and by Act V the only remaining way of winning.

In the text

My salad days, When I was green in judgment: cold in blood, To say as I said then!

Cleopatra, 1.5.76

She rewrites her own past on the spot before she will lose an argument, and the phrase has been borrowed so often since that its edge is easy to miss: green is unripe, and cold in blood is an accusation of having been not fully alive. The revision is the performance.

The barge she sat in, like a burnish’d throne, Burn’d on the water: the poop was beaten gold; Purple the sails, and so perfumed that The winds were love-sick with them

Domitius Enobarbus, 2.2.213

The placing is the argument: the treaty has just been signed and Enobarbus immediately tells the signatories what they have bargained against. Shakespeare keeps much of North’s prose almost word for word but breaks it across the verse line, so that the sentence never rests where the metre does.

News, messengers and report

Almost everything important arrives through somebody else’s mouth: Fulvia’s death, the marriage, Actium, Antony’s suicide. The play is built so that events reach the stage already interpreted, and the interpreter always has something at stake.

Through the play A messenger is beaten in 2.5, a messenger is whipped in 3.13, and in 4.13 Cleopatra sends a false report of her own death to see what it will do.

In the text

But let the world rank me in register A master-leaver and a fugitive

Domitius Enobarbus, 4.9.22

A man who has spent the play writing other people’s reputations asks for his own to be filed under two words. ‘Register’ is a clerk’s word, and he asks for no mitigation at all: he has understood that the record is the only judgement there will be.

He words me, girls, he words me, that I should not Be noble to myself

Cleopatra, 5.2.209

Making the noun a verb is the whole judgement: Caesar’s courtesy is a technique, and she has seen how it works. Her word for suicide here is ‘noble to myself’, which converts a Roman virtue into something a queen can do alone and in private.

Melting and dissolving identity

Antony’s imagery is full of things losing their edges: Rome melting in Tiber, a cloud that was a horse becoming indistinct as water in water, a crown of the earth that melts at his death. The tragedy is about whether a self must keep one shape to be worth anything.

Through the play The images begin as extravagance in Act I and become a description of his actual condition by 4.14, where he says outright that he cannot hold this visible shape.

In the text

Let Rome in Tiber melt, and the wide arch Of the ranged empire fall! Here is my space.

Mark Antony, 1.1.33

The first of the play’s melting images arrives as bravado and returns in Act IV as diagnosis. ‘Here is my space’ is four monosyllables set against a collapsing empire, spoken by a man standing on a stage: the smallest possible claim, made in the largest possible terms.

That which is now a horse, even with a thought The rack dislimns, and makes it indistinct, As water is in water.

Mark Antony, 4.14.11

‘Dislimns’, meaning unpaints, is rare enough that Shakespeare may have coined it, and it treats the sky as a canvas being wiped. The simile then takes away the comfort of a comparison: water in water is dissolution itself, and there is nothing left to see it against.

Loyalty and defection

The play is full of people deciding when to leave, and none of the decisions is simple. Enobarbus’s desertion is reasonable, Antony’s response to it is generous, and the generosity makes the reasonable decision unbearable.

Through the play Ventidius declines to win too much in 3.1, Enobarbus resolves to leave at the end of 3.13, the Egyptian fleet yields in 4.12, and Charmian follows her mistress in 5.2.

In the text

Mine honesty and I begin to square.

Domitius Enobarbus, 3.13.46

‘Square’ means to quarrel, and the line splits a man from his own integrity as though they were two soldiers falling out. Everything Enobarbus says for the rest of the scene is an argument with himself conducted in the third person, and he loses it.

Now boast thee, death, in thy possession lies A lass unparallel’d. Downy windows, close;

Charmian, 5.2.339

After five acts of Egypt, empire and infinite variety, the summing-up word is ‘lass’, the smallest, plainest, most domestic noun available. Charmian then straightens the crown, which is the only correction anyone in the play manages to make to how Cleopatra will be seen.
