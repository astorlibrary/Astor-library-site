Antony and Cleopatra · William Shakespeare

Themes

Rome and Egypt

Philo’s opening speech sets out the opposition (discipline against appetite, measure against excess), and the play then spends five acts making it unusable. Rome is as theatrical as Alexandria; it has simply trained its performance to look like plain fact.

Philo states the Roman case before Antony has spoken; by Act V Caesar is arranging a procession and Cleopatra is declining a part in it, and both are staging.

Nay, but this dotage of our general's O'erflows the measure: those his goodly eyes, That o'er the files and musters of the war Have glow'd like plated Mars, now bend, now turn, The office and devotion of their view Upon a tawny front

Philo, 1.1.1

There's beggary in the love that can be reckon'd.

Mark Antony, 1.1.16

Measure and excess

Counting is the Roman virtue: ships, legions, kingdoms, the terms of a treaty. Antony and Cleopatra answer in a vocabulary of boundlessness (new heavens, melting empires, an autumn that grew the more by reaping), and the play will not say which language is lying.

Antony refuses to have love reckoned in 1.1; Caesar ends by counting the pair into a single grave and a solemn show.

Nay, but this dotage of our general's O'erflows the measure: those his goodly eyes, That o'er the files and musters of the war Have glow'd like plated Mars, now bend, now turn, The office and devotion of their view Upon a tawny front

Philo, 1.1.1

There's beggary in the love that can be reckon'd.

Mark Antony, 1.1.16

Performance and self-presentation

Everyone here is managing an image: Cleopatra openly, Caesar invisibly, Antony badly. The most dangerous thing Cleopatra imagines in the last act is a bad performance of herself, by a squeaking boy actor in Rome.

Play-acting is flirtation in Act I, propaganda in Act III, and by Act V the only remaining way of winning.

My salad days, When I was green in judgment: cold in blood, To say as I said then!

Cleopatra, 1.5.76

The barge she sat in, like a burnish'd throne, Burn'd on the water: the poop was beaten gold; Purple the sails, and so perfumed that The winds were love-sick with them

Domitius Enobarbus, 2.2.213

News, messengers and report

Almost everything important arrives through somebody else’s mouth: Fulvia’s death, the marriage, Actium, Antony’s suicide. The play is built so that events reach the stage already interpreted, and the interpreter always has something at stake.

A messenger is beaten in 2.5, a messenger is whipped in 3.13, and in 4.13 Cleopatra sends a false report of her own death to see what it will do.

he fishes, drinks, and wastes The lamps of night in revel; is not more man-like Than Cleopatra; nor the queen of Ptolemy More womanly than he

Octavius Caesar, 1.4.4

Melting and dissolving identity

Antony’s imagery is full of things losing their edges: Rome melting in Tiber, a cloud that was a horse becoming indistinct as water in water, a crown of the earth that melts at his death. The tragedy is about whether a self must keep one shape to be worth anything.

The images begin as extravagance in Act I and become a description of his actual condition by 4.14, where he says outright that he cannot hold this visible shape.

Let Rome in Tiber melt, and the wide arch Of the ranged empire fall! Here is my space.

Mark Antony, 1.1.33

Egypt, thou knew'st too well My heart was to thy rudder tied by the strings, And thou shouldst tow me after

Mark Antony, 3.11.56

Loyalty and defection

The play is full of people deciding when to leave, and none of the decisions is simple. Enobarbus’s desertion is reasonable, Antony’s response to it is generous, and the generosity makes the reasonable decision unbearable.

Ventidius declines to win too much in 3.1, Enobarbus resolves to leave at the end of 3.13, the Egyptian fleet yields in 4.12, and Charmian follows her mistress in 5.2.

Mine honesty and I begin to square.

Domitius Enobarbus, 3.13.46

Egypt, thou knew'st too well My heart was to thy rudder tied by the strings, And thou shouldst tow me after

Mark Antony, 3.11.56
