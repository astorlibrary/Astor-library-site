Antony and Cleopatra · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Hyperbole

Deliberate overstatement, used to claim a scale that ordinary language cannot reach.

Cleopatra’s dream of an emperor whose legs bestrid the ocean is the play’s largest overstatement and its hardest question: Dolabella says no such man existed, and she wonders whether that settles anything.

Let Rome in Tiber melt, and the wide arch Of the ranged empire fall! Here is my space.

Mark Antony, 1.1.33

Scene fragmentation

Building an act from many brief scenes instead of a few long ones.

Act III changes place thirteen times, and the battle of Actium is split into four scenes of which none shows the fighting. Political space is made to feel unmanageable by the form itself.

The messenger scene

A convention in which offstage events are reported to a listener who reacts in front of us.

Shakespeare makes a convention into a subject: the messenger who is beaten in 2.5 and called back in 3.3 shows that the listener decides what the news means.

Choric commentary

A character who stands slightly outside events and interprets them, without being exempt from them.

Enobarbus supplies the barge speech, the jokes and the running assessment of his own commander, and then discovers that commentary is not a safe place to stand.

Nay, but this dotage of our general's O'erflows the measure: those his goodly eyes, That o'er the files and musters of the war Have glow'd like plated Mars, now bend, now turn, The office and devotion of their view Upon a tawny front

Philo, 1.1.1

Run-on verse

Blank verse in which sense spills past the line-ending instead of resting on it.

The late style is at its loosest here: sentences begin mid-line, break off and start again, so even the grandest speeches sound like someone thinking aloud.

My good knave Eros, now thy captain is Even such a body: here I am Antony: Yet cannot hold this visible shape, my knave.

Mark Antony, 4.14.15

Elemental imagery

Fire, air, earth and water used as a vocabulary for what a person is made of.

Cleopatra’s ‘I am fire and air’ discards the two heavier elements at the moment of dying, having earlier called kingdoms clay through Antony’s mouth.

That which is now a horse, even with a thought The rack dislimns, and makes it indistinct, As water is in water.

Mark Antony, 4.14.11
