Antony and Cleopatra · William Shakespeare

Context

Dates

31 BC
The battle of Actium Octavian’s fleet defeats Antony and Cleopatra off the western coast of Greece. Shakespeare keeps the sea-fight offstage and stages the reaction to it instead.
30 BC
The deaths of Antony and Cleopatra Alexandria falls to Octavian, and within the year both are dead. Plutarch’s account of the manner of Cleopatra’s death is the source Shakespeare follows most closely.
1579
North’s translation of Plutarch published Thomas North’s English Plutarch, made from Amyot’s French, gives Shakespeare both his narrative and some of his phrasing.
1592
Mary Sidney’s Antonius printed A translation of Robert Garnier’s French tragedy, and part of an English fashion for closet drama about the Egyptian queen.
1594
Samuel Daniel’s Cleopatra published Written as a companion to Mary Sidney’s play, and evidence that the subject was already established before Shakespeare took it up.
1599
Julius Caesar first performed Antony’s oration over the body belongs to the same Plutarchan material, seven or eight years earlier in Shakespeare’s career.
1606
Antony and Cleopatra most likely written Usually dated 1606–07, between King Lear and Coriolanus.
1608
Entered in the Stationers’ Register The entry records an intention to print that produced no surviving quarto.
1623
First Folio The only early text, printed among the tragedies with no act or scene divisions of the kind modern editions supply.
1678
Dryden’s All for Love published A rewriting of the story in neoclassical form, keeping the unities Shakespeare ignores; it held the stage for well over a century.

Places

Alexandria
Cleopatra’s palace and the monument in which both lovers die.
Rome
Caesar’s house, the triumvirs’ quarrel and the marriage to Octavia.
Actium
The sea battle of Act III, fought offstage and reported by two officers watching from the shore.
Misenum
Where the triumvirs come to terms with Pompey and then get drunk on his galley.
Athens
Antony and Octavia’s household, and the scene in which the treaty begins to fail.
Tarsus
On the river Cydnus, where Enobarbus places the barge and the first meeting.

Critics

Samuel Taylor Coleridge

Literary Remains (1836)

In notes from his lectures Coleridge wondered whether the play was not a formidable rival to Macbeth, Lear, Hamlet and Othello, and praised the boldness of its style, which he called feliciter audax, happily daring.

T. S. Eliot

“Hamlet and His Problems”, in The Sacred Wood (1920)

In an essay mainly about Hamlet, Eliot named Coriolanus and Antony and Cleopatra as Shakespeare’s most assured artistic successes.
