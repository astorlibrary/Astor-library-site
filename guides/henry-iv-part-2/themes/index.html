Age, sickness and time

The play is full of old and ill men: a dying king, a gouty knight, a thin justice counting his dead friends. The Chief Justice tells Falstaff his age to his face in the first act, and others go on reminding him of it until the new king’s rejection in the last scene.

Through the play It moves from Falstaff’s jokes about his youth in 1.2 to Shallow’s list of the dead, the King’s deathbed and the new king’s order to fall to his prayers.

In the text

His effect of gravy, gravy, gravy.

Falstaff, 1.2.138

Falstaff reduces dignity to dripping with one pun. Falstaff’s answer to every mention of his age is to change the subject to his body and his appetite.

I know thee not, old man. Fall to thy prayers.

King Henry V, 5.5.39

These are the new reign’s first words to its old companion. It picks up the Chief Justice’s list of Falstaff’s signs of age in the first act, and Hal now speaks as the law.

Legitimacy

Henry IV took the throne from Richard II, and the play sends in the bill again and again: rebels invoke Richard’s blood, the King remembers Richard’s prophecy, and on his deathbed he admits to by-paths and indirect crooked ways.

Through the play The crown passes to Hal with, as his father says, better quiet; Hal’s answer is that his possession must be plain and right because his father won it and gave it him.

In the text

Uneasy lies the head that wears a crown.

King Henry, 3.1.31

The line closes a long speech envying the poor and the ship-boy their sleep. The crown the King has won keeps him awake: that is the price of how he won it.

O polish’d perturbation! golden care!

Prince Hal, 4.3.161

Two oxymorons: the crown is bright and troubling, precious and a burden. Hal’s first words to the crown already know what his father learned by wearing it.

Fathers and sons

Northumberland failed his son, the King fears his son, and the Chief Justice once punished the King’s son on his father’s behalf. Hal has to choose between a real father, a false one in Falstaff, and a legal one in the Chief Justice.

Through the play The choice is made in two scenes: Hal names the Chief Justice a father to his youth in 5.2, and turns away Falstaff in 5.5.

In the text

my heart bleeds inwardly that my father is so sick

Prince Hal, 2.2.38

He admits real grief, then explains why he cannot show it: in his company, weeping would look like hypocrisy. The line shows how much his reputation now weighs on him.

Not Amurath an Amurath succeeds, But Harry Harry.

King Henry V, 5.2.49

Amurath was a Turkish sultan whose successor had his brothers killed. Hal promises an English succession instead, and the doubled name insists on continuity with his father.

The law and the tavern

The Lord Chief Justice stands for the law that Falstaff expects the new reign to set aside. Falstaff’s cry that the laws of England are at his commandment meets the justice who sends him to the Fleet.

Through the play Justice is tested in the middle too: at Gaultree Prince John keeps the letter of his promise and breaks its meaning.

In the text

He hath eaten me out of house and home.

Hostess, 2.1.58

The phrase became proverbial. Here it is literal: she means the money he owes her for years of food and drink, and within the scene she agrees to lend him more.

God, and not we, hath safely fought today.

Prince John, 4.1.357

Rhyme and piety dress up a trick. John has kept to the words of his promise and broken its sense, and he gives the credit to God.

Rumour and report

Rumour opens the play with false news, and false or exaggerated reports keep arriving: Northumberland’s messengers contradict each other, Warwick says rumour doubles the rebels’ numbers, Colevile surrenders to Falstaff’s name.

Through the play By the end the question is Hal’s reputation, and he promises to frustrate the prophecies and raze out rotten opinion.

In the text

Rumour is a pipe Blown by surmises, jealousies, conjectures

Rumour, Induction.15

The image makes rumour an instrument that anyone can blow and the crowd can play upon. The list of three nouns says where false news comes from: guesses, suspicions and fears, with no facts among them.

Yea, this man’s brow, like to a title-leaf, Foretells the nature of a tragic volume.

Northumberland, 1.1.65

Northumberland already knows his son is dead, and says so through a simile from a bookshop. He reads the news in Morton’s face and still tells him, ‘say not that Percy’s dead’: the play’s first case of someone believing and disbelieving a report in the same breath.

Remembering the past

Shallow lives in his memories of Clement’s Inn, the King in his memory of Richard, Lady Percy in her memory of Hotspur. Falstaff says Shallow’s are lies.

Through the play The new king asks whether the wrongs done to him can be washed in Lethe and forgotten, and decides instead to keep the man who did them.

In the text

Past and to come seems best; things present, worst.

Archbishop, 1.3.109

Both halves of the line balance, and together they describe a country that cannot be satisfied with its present king. The Archbishop is describing the crowd, but the sentiment runs through the play: Shallow’s past, the King’s fear of the future.

He was indeed the glass Wherein the noble youth did dress themselves.

Lady Percy, 2.3.21

The dead man is remembered as a mirror of fashion that others copied, down to his stammer. The praise is also an accusation: this is the son his father failed to support.
