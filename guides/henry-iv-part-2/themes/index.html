Henry IV, Part 2 · William Shakespeare

Themes

Age, sickness and time

The play is full of old and ill men: a dying king, a gouty knight, a thin justice counting his dead friends. The Chief Justice tells Falstaff his age to his face in the first act, and others go on reminding him of it until the new king’s rejection in the last scene.

It moves from Falstaff’s jokes about his youth in 1.2 to Shallow’s list of the dead, the King’s deathbed and the new king’s order to fall to his prayers.

His effect of gravy, gravy, gravy.

Falstaff, 1.2.138

A good wit will make use of anything. I will turn diseases to commodity.

Falstaff, 1.2.209

Legitimacy

Henry IV took the throne from Richard II, and the play sends in the bill again and again: rebels invoke Richard’s blood, the King remembers Richard’s prophecy, and on his deathbed he admits to by-paths and indirect crooked ways.

The crown passes to Hal with, as his father says, better quiet; Hal’s answer is that his possession must be plain and right because his father won it and gave it him.

Past and to come seems best; things present, worst.

Archbishop, 1.3.109

Uneasy lies the head that wears a crown.

King Henry, 3.1.31

Fathers and sons

Northumberland failed his son, the King fears his son, and the Chief Justice once punished the King’s son on his father’s behalf. Hal has to choose between a real father, a false one in Falstaff, and a legal one in the Chief Justice.

The choice is made in two scenes: Hal names the Chief Justice a father to his youth in 5.2, and turns away Falstaff in 5.5.

Yea, this man’s brow, like to a title-leaf, Foretells the nature of a tragic volume.

Northumberland, 1.1.65

my heart bleeds inwardly that my father is so sick

Prince Hal, 2.2.38

The law and the tavern

The Lord Chief Justice stands for the law that Falstaff expects the new reign to set aside. Falstaff’s cry that the laws of England are at his commandment meets the justice who sends him to the Fleet.

Justice is tested in the middle too: at Gaultree Prince John keeps the letter of his promise and breaks its meaning.

He hath eaten me out of house and home.

Hostess, 2.1.58

God, and not we, hath safely fought today.

Prince John, 4.1.357

Rumour and report

Rumour opens the play with false news, and false or exaggerated reports keep arriving: Northumberland’s messengers contradict each other, Warwick says rumour doubles the rebels’ numbers, Colevile surrenders to Falstaff’s name.

By the end the question is Hal’s reputation, and he promises to frustrate the prophecies and raze out rotten opinion.

Rumour is a pipe Blown by surmises, jealousies, conjectures

Rumour, 1.0.15

Yea, this man’s brow, like to a title-leaf, Foretells the nature of a tragic volume.

Northumberland, 1.1.65

Remembering the past

Shallow lives in his memories of Clement’s Inn, the King in his memory of Richard, Lady Percy in her memory of Hotspur. Falstaff says Shallow’s are lies.

The new king asks whether the wrongs done to him can be washed in Lethe and forgotten, and decides instead to keep the man who did them.

Past and to come seems best; things present, worst.

Archbishop, 1.3.109

He was indeed the glass Wherein the noble youth did dress themselves.

Lady Percy, 2.3.21
