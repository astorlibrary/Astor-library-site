E. F. Benson Ghost Stories · E. F. Benson

Characters

Julia Stone

The hostess of the narrator’s dream in The Room in the Tower; first appears The Room in the Tower

In the narrator’s dream she is Mrs Stone, silent at tea on the lawn until she tells him that Jack will show him the room in the tower. In the waking story she is a portrait, signed Julia Stone by Julia Stone, whose face is alive with secret mirth. She had killed herself in the tower room; three burials failed to keep her coffin in the ground at West Fawley, and when it was dug up again it was full of blood.

Charles Linkworth

The hanged murderer of The Confession of Charles Linkworth; first appears The Confession of Charles Linkworth

Keeper of a small stationery shop in Sheffield, he strangled his mother for the five hundred pounds she had drawn from the bank, buried her in the back garden and went to the gallows without confessing. The prison doctor had never seen a man cling so hard to life. After the execution his whisper comes down the doctor’s telephone, and at last he confesses to the chaplain and hears the words of absolution.

Madge Dalrymple

The girl who pities the twins in How Fear Departed from the Long Gallery; first appears How Fear Departed from the Long Gallery

Blanche Peveril’s cousin hurts her knee on the ice on New Year’s Eve, stays behind in the long gallery while the others skate, and falls asleep there at dusk, the hour when the burned twins walk. Everyone who had seen them before had feared or mocked them. Waking to find them coming towards her, she is only sorry for them, and her pity dissolves the curse.

Arthur Inglis

The artist at the Villa Cascana in Caterpillars; first appears Caterpillars

An artist with a bedroom and studio at the Italian villa where the narrator sees a heap of luminous caterpillars in an empty room. He finds a small one crawling on his counterpane, keeps it in a pill-box and, because its feet are like a crab’s, christens it with the Latin for crab and his own name. Six months later the narrator hears that he has incurable cancer.

Hugh Grainger

The narrator’s friend in The Bus-Conductor, and again in The Gardener; first appears The Bus-Conductor

A steady man who had long ago decided he would never see a ghost, Hugh explains why his nerves once went to pieces: an empty hearse stopping outside his friend’s house before dawn, and a driver with a mole on his cheek who, a month later, is the conductor of a bus. The name returns in The Gardener of 1923, where Hugh Grainger and his wife Margaret take a house in which a dead man searches the rooms, and Hugh admits he has never felt less comfortable in his life.

The bus conductor

The figure in The Bus-Conductor, first seen driving a hearse; first appears The Bus-Conductor

He comes up an empty street before dawn driving a glass hearse drawn by black horses, dressed in grey trousers, brown boots and a straw hat, with a strap over his shoulder, and when he looks up his long thin face has a hairy mole on the left cheek. A month later the same face appears on the step of a crowded bus in Sloane Square, with the same words. Hugh Grainger calls him a most useful and friendly ghost.

Frank Halton

The man who went too far; first appears The Man Who Went Too Far

Thirty-five but looking like a boy, he has left London to live in the New Forest village of St Faith’s and devote himself to joy, and he believes that nature is beginning to answer him with the sound of Pan’s pipes. He waits eagerly for a final revelation. It comes in a thunderstorm, with a scream to God and a little bleating laugh, and leaves him dead with the marks of hoofs on his chest.

Darcy

Frank Halton’s friend in The Man Who Went Too Far; first appears The Man Who Went Too Far

An old friend who comes to St Faith’s after an illness and listens to Frank’s gospel of joy with growing unease. After some days he finds the flaw in it: all nature is crammed full of suffering, and a man who wants to be one with nature and leaves suffering out may find that the last revelation is a revelation of horror. He carries Frank in from the garden on the night of the storm.

Francis Assheton

The brain specialist whose statement makes up The Thing in the Hall; first appears The Thing in the Hall

Trained under Charcot and now a specialist in the brain, he dictated this account of his friend Louis Fielder’s experiments and heard it read back just before he died, insisting that he was completely sane and that the things he described happened in the external world. At the inquest on Fielder he had called the death suicide while of unsound mind; he now wants it known that he would give different evidence.

Louis Fielder

The experimenter of The Thing in the Hall; first appears The Thing in the Hall

Assheton’s friend since Cambridge, he takes up spiritualism impartially, learns to throw open the door of his soul and says that anyone may come in. What comes in, he suspects, is an Elemental, one of the servants of the evil principle in the world, and he wants to know what it is even in the name of Satan. He changes for the worse, and dies, like Assheton after him, with his throat torn.

Sir James Horton

The scientist of And the Dead Spake; first appears And the Dead Spake

A reclusive physicist of Newsome Terrace in Kensington, said to have made an artificial creature from living animal tissue, he thinks of the brain as a record office, where every vivid experience leaves a mark like the groove of a gramophone plate. He spends the war building a needle fine enough to play those marks back. When his housekeeper Mrs Gabriel dies, he puts the current through her dead brain, hears her confess to the murder of her husband, and is killed by his own apparatus a moment later.

John Aylsford

The painter of At the Farmhouse; first appears At the Farmhouse

Twenty years ago he fell in love with a fisher-girl who sat for him, married her against every warning about her family and bought the farmhouse where they met. She took to drink, and he came to believe in the spells he had once laughed at. One November night he walks out in a gale with whipcord and matches, strangles her and sets the house on fire, and finds himself trapped in the studio with a radiant young woman pointing at him.

Ellen Trenair

The wife in At the Farmhouse; first appears At the Farmhouse

Once a beautiful fisher-girl with a smile for the young painter who lodged at her uncle’s farm, she comes of a dark family whose women go mad or hang themselves, and in whose blood runs the Methodist preacher who learned a darker gospel one All-Hallows’ Eve. Drink leaves her bloated and withered, and her husband believes she has bound him by witchcraft. After he strangles her she comes back to him young again, radiant with revenge.

Sybil Rorke

The widow in Inscrutable Decrees; first appears Inscrutable Decrees

Tall, splendid and the queen of a London season, she dazzles everyone who meets her, yet the narrator keeps catching her enjoying pain: a dying thrush, a frightened child at the dentist’s, a newspaper sketch of an execution kept in her blotter. She plays with her husband’s cousin Archie while her husband is dying. A girl drowned in the lake at Lincote returns at a séance to say that Sybil watched her struggle and did nothing.

Archie Rorke

The teller of the story in Inscrutable Decrees; first appears Inscrutable Decrees

The narrator’s oldest friend, desperately in love with his cousin’s wife, inherits the title and the house at Lincote and becomes engaged to her. A week or two before the wedding the lake is drained and a missing village girl is found in the mud; at a séance he asks Sybil to hold, the girl’s phantom accuses her. He breaks off the engagement without a word, and tells the story only after Sybil’s death.

Mr Tilly

The dead man of Mr. Tilly’s Séance; first appears Mr. Tilly’s Séance

A fussy, good-natured little spiritualist, he slips at Hyde Park Corner, is flattened by a traction-engine, and goes on to Mrs Cumberbatch’s séance as a spirit, pleased to find himself on the newsboards. There he catches the medium reaching for the trumpet, agrees to speak through her, and finds that everything he says comes out as the stale talk of busy happy spirits.

Mrs Cumberbatch

The medium of Mr. Tilly’s Séance; first appears Mr. Tilly’s Séance

With her long spoon-shaped face and an antique chair that had belonged to Madame Blavatsky, she charges guineas for messages from Abibel, Sapphire and Napoleon, and hides Lord Rosebery’s book on Napoleon in an old cover. She is a fraud, but not entirely: she can see and hear the dead Mr Tilly when nobody else can, and begs him to make her fortune.

Mrs Amworth

The widow of Mrs. Amworth; first appears Mrs. Amworth

Big, energetic and jolly, the widow of a judge in the North-West Provinces who died at Peshawar, she settles in Maxley, where her ancestors the Chastons are buried, and wakes the village up with lunches, music and piquet. Everyone likes her except Francis Urcombe, who watches. A gardener’s boy begins to waste away, and the narrator dreams of her face floating outside his bedroom window.

Francis Urcombe

The retired professor of Mrs. Amworth; first appears Mrs. Amworth

He gave up his professorship of physiology at Cambridge to study the borderland between body and spirit, and wanted medical students examined in mesmerism, haunted houses and vampirism. Opposite the narrator’s house in Maxley he watches Mrs Amworth closely, moves the sick boy into his own house, bangs down a window on her finger, and ends the story in the cemetery with a pick.

Anthony Carling

The teller of In the Tube; first appears In the Tube

A generous host in Brompton Square with a great zest for life, he believes that time and space are conventions and that eternity is a quality, and he has the strangest clairvoyances. He tells a friend how he saw a man in an otherwise empty tube carriage, met him at lunch the next day, saw him in a vision throw himself in front of a train, and read of the suicide a few days later. The ghost of that man comes back to the fireside, and Anthony takes on its message.

Roderick Cardew

The dying friend of Roderick’s Story; first appears Roderick’s Story

Told by his doctor that his heart may stop without warning, he makes jokes about the bad manners of dying in other people’s houses, then accepts his friend’s invitation to Tilling when he learns it is the house where Margaret Alton died. He asks for her room, hits one last perfect drive on the golf links, calls his host’s ghost stories false, and tells what he saw at her deathbed.

Margaret Alton

The woman Roderick loved, in Roderick’s Story; first appears Roderick’s Story

Dazzling as a girl, she gave up Roderick to marry the heir to a barony, was treated with cruelty and then deserted, lost both her sons in France within a few days, and died of a disfiguring disease in the house at Tilling, veiled. When Roderick lifted the veil he saw her as she truly was, lovelier than ever, with her two boys come to fetch her.
