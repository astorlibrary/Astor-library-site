E. F. Benson Ghost Stories · E. F. Benson

Form and language

Frame narrative

A story set inside another, so that the reader hears it from a character telling it to someone else.

Many of these stories are told aloud after dinner or by the fire: Hugh Grainger’s bus conductor, Everard Chandler’s round room, Archie Rorke’s broken engagement, Anthony Carling’s night in the Tube, Roderick’s last visit to Margaret. The Thing in the Hall is a dead man’s dictated statement, read back to him before he died. The frames put the tellers in warm, lit rooms, and make the listener, often a sceptic, the reader’s stand-in.

I am quite certain as a brain specialist that I am completely sane, and that these things happened not merely in my imagination, but in the external world.

Francis Assheton, The Thing in the Hall

Refrain

A phrase brought back at intervals, so that its meaning changes with what has happened since.

Several stories hang on one repeated sentence. Jack will show you your room recurs through fifteen years of dreams before a waking hostess says it; Just room for one inside, sir is spoken first by a hearse-driver and then by a bus conductor; the planchette writes gardener, gardener; and the Latin under the Polearn panel is the last thing the first owner of the quarry-house screams. With each return the reader knows more of what it means.

Jack will show you your room: I have given you the room in the tower.

Julia Stone, The Room in the Tower

The unseen

Something left out on purpose, at a point where the reader will notice the gap and fill it.

For all his monsters, Benson knows the force of what is not seen. In fifteen years of dreams the narrator of The Room in the Tower never sees what waits in the room; in The Gardener an invisible man pushes past on a doorstep and leaves a rocking-chair moving in an empty kitchen; in In the Tube the narrator feels an ice-cold presence and hears only one side of Anthony’s conversation with it. Any vision, he says, would have been more tolerable than knowing the thing was there.

I have talked to a soul in the hell of remorse, which is the only possible hell.

Anthony Carling, In the Tube

The grotesque

Bodies distorted past the natural, so that disgust and fear arrive together.

When Benson does show his monsters he makes them fleshy and wrong: caterpillars a foot long covered in lumps and swellings, an Elemental like a legless slug with a seal’s head, the Polearn Thing with a puckered orifice that slavers at the edges, Ellen’s bloated crimson face set on a withered neck. Several are slug-like, and many suggest disease.

In colour these dreadful insects were yellowish-grey, and they were covered with irregular lumps and swellings.

Caterpillars

Analogy

Explaining something strange by comparing it with something familiar.

Theorists in these stories explain the supernatural with the newest machines. Mrs Aldwych compares a haunted room to a phonograph and passing feeling to a Marconi message; the narrator of The Terror by Night calls us all receivers of a kind of wireless telegraphy; Sir James Horton treats the brain as a gramophone plate waiting for its needle; Anthony Carling calls time a tunnel we believe we are travelling through. Hugh Grainger’s two revolving sheets of cardboard, each with a spy-hole, are homelier, and the narrator hopes his ghost will be more original than his theory.

these two holes, the one through which we are always looking, and the other in the spiritual plane, come opposite one another, we see through, and then only do the sights and sounds of the spiritual world become visible or audible to us.

Hugh Grainger, The Bus-Conductor

Dramatic irony

When the reader understands more than a character does, so that the character’s words carry a meaning they cannot hear.

Characters here often say more than they know. Arthur Inglis christens his caterpillar Cancer Inglisensis; Charles Alington jokes about how awful it would be to learn you had been Judas Iscariot; Mrs Amworth delights in having her blood curdled and advises the narrator to sleep with his windows open; Sir James Horton promises more from dead lips a moment before he dies himself. Readers who know the genre are usually a step ahead, and part of the pleasure is watching the characters catch up.

What’s the Latin for crab? Oh, yes, Cancer. So in case it is unique, let’s christen it: ‘Cancer Inglisensis.’

Arthur Inglis, Caterpillars

Comedy

Humour set beside horror, or used to deflate it.

Benson made his name with a comic novel, Dodo, and the comedy comes into the ghost stories too. The Peverils treat their ghosts like the post; Weston’s orange-peel disguise frightens a grave-robber backwards into the grave; Mr Tilly, flattened by a traction-engine, minds most about his new clothes and what his servants say of him. Even Roderick Cardew, dying, makes jokes about manners and hotels.

he may perhaps cast an occasional glance into the corners and dark places of the room where he sits, to make sure that nothing unusual lurks in the shadow … The author therefore fervently wishes his readers a few uncomfortable moments.

Preface, 1912

Haunted objects

An object that comes back again and again until it stands for the whole story.

Each story fixes on a thing: the portrait signed Julia Stone by Julia Stone, the hangman’s missing rope, the carved panel at Polearn and the altar used as a painter’s table, the whipcord in John Aylsford’s pocket, Margaret Alton’s veil. The telephone in Dr Teesdale’s study serves as a confessional, and the rocking-chair in The Gardener shows a presence better than a ghost could.

I killed her, I confess it. I want to be forgiven.

Charles Linkworth, The Confession of Charles Linkworth

The flat last line

Delivering an outrage or an absurdity in the same flat tone as everything else.

The endings are often a single plain sentence that states the worst without comment: the coffin was found to be full of blood; Bircham, sir; There is no rope, sir; Yes, that’s right. Roderick’s Story closes with a man who draws a couple of short breaths and falls back. The understatement leaves the reader to supply the shudder.

For to the Peverils the appearance of a ghost is a matter of hardly greater significance than is the appearance of the post to those who live in more ordinary houses.

How Fear Departed from the Long Gallery

Rival explanations

A natural and a supernatural account offered side by side, with the reader left to choose between them.

Almost every story offers the reader a way out. Machmout’s visions may be thought-reading, White Magic or Pure Coincidence; Everard Chandler says there are hundreds of ways to explain his vision; the narrator of The Outcast invites anyone who likes to recline on the cushion of coincidence; the surgeon in Machaon cannot believe in the cure he is watching. The endings usually lean one way, and The Gardener says outright that nobody is so obstinately unreasonable as a materialist.

Supposing you asked me, on oath, whether I believed in witches, I should say ‘No.’ But if you asked me again, on oath, whether I suspected I believed in them, I should, I think, say ‘Yes.’

Gavon’s Eve

Allusion

A reference to another text or story, which the reader is expected to recognise.

Educated narrators quote as they talk. The Polearn legend comes from the ninety-first Psalm; John Aylsford justifies murder with Thou shalt not suffer a witch to live; Sybil Rorke is kind to be cruel in a reversal of Hamlet, and John Evans means to hold the mirror up to Nature, as that old lunatic said; Anthony Carling quotes Browning on playing with souls. Machaon depends on the narrator’s knowledge of Greek myth and of the precinct of Asclepios in Athens, where, like Benson, he had been a student of archaeology.

It was, my dear Darcy, as the Greeks would have said, it was Pan playing on his pipes, the voice of Nature.

Frank Halton, The Man Who Went Too Far
