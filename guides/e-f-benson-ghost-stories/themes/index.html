E. F. Benson Ghost Stories · E. F. Benson

Themes

Dreams and premonitions

Several stories depend on seeing what has not happened yet. The narrator of The Room in the Tower dreams of a house for fifteen years before he sleeps in it; Hugh Grainger sees the face of a bus conductor a month before the bus crashes; Everard Chandler sees a round room full of dwarfish savages nine months before he stumbles into it; Anthony Carling watches a man throw himself under a train days before he does it. Benson’s characters argue over whether such things are coincidence or a glimpse down the tunnel of time.

Jack will show you your room; Just room for one inside, sir; the round room in the Picts’ castle; Time is a sort of tunnel.

Jack will show you your room: I have given you the room in the tower.

Julia Stone, The Room in the Tower

Just room for one inside, sir,

The bus conductor, The Bus-Conductor

Ghosts

Benson’s ghosts come back for reasons. Some repeat what was done to them, like the girl whose steps still run down the passage in Outside the Door; some are looking for something, like the gardener who searches the house for his wife; some want to confess or to be forgiven, like Charles Linkworth and Sir Henry Payle. The twins in How Fear Departed from the Long Gallery need only to be pitied, and in Roderick’s Story the dead return in love.

The appearance of a ghost and the appearance of the post; I am the gardener. I want to come in; a soul in the hell of remorse; two boys at the other side of the bed.

he may perhaps cast an occasional glance into the corners and dark places of the room where he sits, to make sure that nothing unusual lurks in the shadow … The author therefore fervently wishes his readers a few uncomfortable moments.

Preface, 1912

I knew you would come to the room in the tower … I have been long waiting for you. At last you have come. To-night I shall feast; before long we will feast together.

Julia Stone, The Room in the Tower

Psychical research

The Society for Psychical Research was founded in 1882, with Benson’s uncle Henry Sidgwick as its first president, to investigate thought-transference, apparitions, mediums and hypnotism, and Benson’s stories are full of its vocabulary: sensitives, planchette, trance, controls, materialisation, phantoms of the living. Weston in At Abdul Ali’s Grave is a member who once exposed a fraudulent medium; Dr Teesdale knows himself to be a sensitive; the control in Machaon talks platitudes until something else comes through. Mr. Tilly’s Séance mocks the whole business, down to investigators who reject the only genuine phenomena they ever meet, and yet most of the stories treat the evidence as if it might be real.

A “sensitive”; a member of the Society for Psychical Research; the analogy of wireless telegraphy; Starlight’s clap-trap; for once, the phenomena were absolutely genuine.

with Machmout I have to put it down to White Magic, which should be a very inclusive term, or to Pure Coincidence, which is even more inclusive, and will cover all the inexplicable phenomena of the world, taken singly.

At Abdul Ali’s Grave

these two holes, the one through which we are always looking, and the other in the spiritual plane, come opposite one another, we see through, and then only do the sights and sounds of the spiritual world become visible or audible to us.

Hugh Grainger, The Bus-Conductor

Guilt and confession

Murderers crowd these books, and their crimes do not stay hidden. Linkworth confesses by telephone after his hanging; Mrs Gabriel’s dead lips describe how she lathered her husband’s chin after cutting his throat; the gardener searches for the wife he strangled; Sybil Rorke is accused by the child she watched drown; Sir Henry Payle re-enacts his suicide until a living man will carry his message. Anthony Carling asks when a crime is really committed, in the planning or the act, and John Aylsford finds that the murder he planned for months does not set him free.

I killed her, I confess it. I want to be forgiven; a tale for dead lips to tell; you watched me struggling till I sank; the hell of remorse, which is the only possible hell.

I killed her, I confess it. I want to be forgiven.

Charles Linkworth, The Confession of Charles Linkworth

Fancy knowing you had been Judas Iscariot! You couldn’t get over it! you would commit suicide, or cause everybody who was connected with you to commit suicide from their horror of you.

The Outcast

Pity

Against the cruelty in these books Benson sets a few acts of pity that change what happens. Madge Dalrymple, sorry for the murdered twins, breaks a curse that fear and mockery had kept alive for three centuries; in The Outcast another Madge makes a wreath for a woman no one else mourns; Margaret Grainger, face to face with the gardener’s ghost, asks God to have mercy on him. Roderick’s Story ends the second book with a love that outlasts disfigurement and death.

I am only sorry for you; a little wreath of the spring-flowers; God have mercy on him!; I bless every pang of it.

It is not your fault that you must bring me what you must bring, but I am not afraid any longer. I am only sorry for you. God bless you, you poor darlings.

Madge Dalrymple, How Fear Departed from the Long Gallery

“Oh, Margaret, my dear....” he cried. He drew a couple of short breaths, and fell back.

Roderick’s Story

Motors, telephones and the Tube

Ghosts in these stories use the newest machinery. A dead man’s motor raises dust on a Norfolk road and sounds its siren; a hanged man speaks down the telephone; a scientist tries to play a dead brain like a gramophone record; a bus and a speeding motor crash at Sloane Square; a suicide happens at the Dover Street tube station. Benson’s theorists borrow their images from the same world, the phonograph, the Marconi apparatus and the receiver in tune, so that the supernatural sounds like the next invention.

The ghost of his motor-car; the telephone bell; your gramophone plate all ready; the analogy of wireless telegraphy; X-rays.

Yes, the ghost of his motor-car. Seems almost too up-to-date, doesn’t it?

The Dust-Cloud

I killed her, I confess it. I want to be forgiven.

Charles Linkworth, The Confession of Charles Linkworth

Nature and the old gods

Outside the drawing-rooms, older powers are still at work. Frank Halton seeks joy in the New Forest and hears Pan’s pipes, and Darcy warns him that nature is crammed full of suffering; Professor Ingram has seen half-human creatures on an Alpine peak, a remnant of the abyss out of which mankind crawled; the villagers of Achnaleish take hares for their neighbours, and at Polearn the storms and seasons have taught the people a spell. Benson sets many of these stories in wild country, the Highlands, Cornwall and the Engadine, and lets the landscape carry the menace.

Pan playing on his pipes, the voice of Nature; crammed full of suffering; the hoofs of some monstrous goat; a being more nearly a man than anything else.

It was, my dear Darcy, as the Greeks would have said, it was Pan playing on his pipes, the voice of Nature.

Frank Halton, The Man Who Went Too Far

All Nature from highest to lowest is full, crammed full of suffering; every living organism in nature preys on another, yet in your aim to get close to, to be one with nature, you leave suffering altogether out

Darcy, The Man Who Went Too Far

Cruelty and evil

Benson’s worst figures enjoy pain. The portrait of Julia Stone laughs with appalling mirth; Sybil Rorke licks her lips over a dying thrush and keeps a sketch of an execution in her blotter; Mrs Amworth feeds on a gardener’s son while bringing him delicate food. Evil can also be a force without a face, like the Elemental that Louis Fielder invites in, or the Thing on the Polearn panel, which the narrator’s uncle calls a minister of God’s wrath.

Evil beamed from the narrow, leering eyes; kind to be demoniacally cruel; the Thing is an Elemental; a minister of God’s wrath on the unrighteous.

I knew you would come to the room in the tower … I have been long waiting for you. At last you have come. To-night I shall feast; before long we will feast together.

Julia Stone, The Room in the Tower

All Nature from highest to lowest is full, crammed full of suffering; every living organism in nature preys on another, yet in your aim to get close to, to be one with nature, you leave suffering altogether out

Darcy, The Man Who Went Too Far

Illness and the body

Disease runs through the stories, and sometimes the ghost is a way of looking at it. The luminous caterpillars of the Villa Cascana are lumpy, crab-footed and malignant, and the man who names one Cancer Inglisensis is later found to have incurable cancer; in Machaon a medium’s message leads to X-ray treatment that cures a servant’s growth. Mrs Amworth’s victims waste away with anaemia, Roderick Cardew lives under sentence of sudden heart failure, and Margaret Alton hides the ravages of a disfiguring disease behind a veil.

Cancer Inglisensis; irregular lumps and swellings; X-rays; an anæmic pallor; heart-disease.

In colour these dreadful insects were yellowish-grey, and they were covered with irregular lumps and swellings.

Caterpillars

What’s the Latin for crab? Oh, yes, Cancer. So in case it is unique, let’s christen it: ‘Cancer Inglisensis.’

Arthur Inglis, Caterpillars

Superstition

Benson likes to set educated English visitors among people whose beliefs they laugh at, and then prove the people right. Hugh Graham half believes in witches; Jim Armytage calls the hare-lore of Achnaleish cock-and-bull stories; John Aylsford laughed at the spells of his wife’s family before he came to fear them. At Polearn the narrator’s aunt tells him he has been in London too long, and in Mrs. Amworth a Cambridge physiologist complains that the medical profession threw vampirism on the dust-heap without studying it.

Whether I suspected I believed in them; a belief as sober and commonplace as our own; you have been in London so long; Thou shalt not suffer a witch to live.

Supposing you asked me, on oath, whether I believed in witches, I should say ‘No.’ But if you asked me again, on oath, whether I suspected I believed in them, I should, I think, say ‘Yes.’

Gavon’s Eve

with Machmout I have to put it down to White Magic, which should be a very inclusive term, or to Pure Coincidence, which is even more inclusive, and will cover all the inexplicable phenomena of the world, taken singly.

At Abdul Ali’s Grave

Sacrilege and judgement

The son of an Archbishop of Canterbury, Benson gives the church a large place in his horrors. The Polearn panel shows a priest holding up a cross against the Thing, and the men who live in a house built from a ruined church, with its altar for a table, die drained of blood; the chaplain in The Confession of Charles Linkworth pronounces absolution into a telephone; The Outcast ends with a medieval treatise on the wandering spirit of Judas. Gavon’s Eve ends on the inscrutable designs of God, and Inscrutable Decrees closes with Archie Rorke saying its title.

Negotium perambulans in tenebris; the words of the Absolution; Judas Iscariot; the inscrutable designs of God; Inscrutable decrees.

I killed her, I confess it. I want to be forgiven.

Charles Linkworth, The Confession of Charles Linkworth

It is not your fault that you must bring me what you must bring, but I am not afraid any longer. I am only sorry for you. God bless you, you poor darlings.

Madge Dalrymple, How Fear Departed from the Long Gallery

Love and death

The second book ends with a story in which death is welcome. Roderick Cardew, told that his heart may stop at any moment, jokes that death is a train that always waits for you, and chooses the room where the woman he loved died, because he saw her there as she really was, with her dead sons come to fetch her. Elsewhere a death at Davos reaches a London room as a sudden cold, and at the end of At the Farmhouse the wife John Aylsford has murdered comes back as the girl he first loved.

That’s a train which we are all sure to catch; I couldn’t die without seeing you; Good-bye, just for a little while; Oh, Margaret, my dear.

Positively I look forward to death, for then I shall be buried and have the kind earth all round me. No leaden caskets for me--I have given explicit directions.

Mrs Amworth, Mrs. Amworth

No proper person fusses about death; that’s a train which we are all sure to catch. It always waits for you.

Roderick Cardew, Roderick’s Story
