E. F. Benson Ghost Stories · E. F. Benson

What happens

The Room in the Tower, stories 1–4 Dreams, motors and a confession

The title story is told by a man who has dreamed for fifteen years of a silent tea-party in a garden with a tower, where Mrs Stone tells him that Jack will show him his room. Staying with friends in the Ashdown Forest, he hears the same sentence, sleeps in the tower room under the portrait of Julia Stone, and wakes to find something at his bedside. In The Dust-Cloud a driver who ran over a child and then crashed into his own gates haunts a straight Norfolk road in a phantom motor. Gavon’s Eve takes a fishing party in Sutherland to the Picts’ pool, where a reputed witch calls a drowned girl up out of the water, and in The Confession of Charles Linkworth a hanged murderer’s whisper comes down the prison doctor’s telephone.

The Room in the Tower
A fifteen-year dream comes true in a house in the Ashdown Forest, and a suicide’s coffin will not stay buried.
The Dust-Cloud
A dust-cloud on the straight road by Bircham, a frightened siren, and a child who will not play.
Gavon’s Eve
On the night of 15 September a witch raises the drowned Catrine Gordon, and her lover goes into the river after her.
The Confession of Charles Linkworth
Whispers down the telephone, a missing rope, and the words of the Absolution.

The Room in the Tower, stories 5–8 Luxor, the Highlands, a Yorkshire house and an Italian villa

At Abdul Ali’s Grave is set at Luxor in June, when the tourists have gone: a boy’s ink-mirror shows a black magician preparing to rob a newly buried man, and the narrator and his friend Weston, a member of the Society for Psychical Research, go to the grave that night, Weston disguised in orange peel as the spirit of Black Magic. In The Shootings of Achnaleish the English tenants of a Highland shoot find that the villagers believe the black hares are their own neighbours. How Fear Departed from the Long Gallery visits Church-Peveril, a Yorkshire house whose family treat its ghosts as calmly as the post, except for two murdered twins whose appearance brings death, and Caterpillars sends the narrator to a villa on the Italian coast, where he sees luminous crab-footed caterpillars heaped on a bed in an empty room.

At Abdul Ali’s Grave
Machmout’s ink-mirror, Weston’s orange-peel disguise, and lips that move in an opened coffin.
The Shootings of Achnaleish
A hare killed on the road, a keeper’s dead mother, and a house wired up to be burned.
How Fear Departed from the Long Gallery
A family’s familiar ghosts, the twins burned in 1602, and Madge Dalrymple’s pity on New Year’s Eve.
Caterpillars
The Villa Cascana, a pill-box on a counterpane, and news from England six months later.

The Room in the Tower, stories 9–12 Portraits, premonitions and Pan

In The Cat a painter jilted by a woman he thinks of as feline paints the eyes of a big grey cat into her portrait, and is found dead in front of it in his garden, his throat mangled and his nails full of paint. The Bus-Conductor is Hugh Grainger’s account of an empty hearse that stopped outside his friend’s house before dawn, and of the driver’s face, which he saw again a month later on the step of a bus. In The Man Who Went Too Far, the longest story in the book, Frank Halton gives his life to joy in the New Forest and hears the pipes of Pan; his friend Darcy warns him that he has left suffering out, and that the last revelation will be one of horror. Between the Lights is told by the fire at Christmas, and concerns a vision that comes true in a ruined Pictish fort.

The Cat
Dick Alingham, Lady Madingley and a big grey cat among the sweet-peas.
The Bus-Conductor
An empty hearse before dawn, and the same face on a bus in Sloane Square a month later.
The Man Who Went Too Far
Frank Halton’s gospel of joy, Darcy’s objection, and the marks on a dead man’s chest.
Between the Lights
A vision on a croquet lawn, and the Picts’ castle in the Highland mist nine months later.

The Room in the Tower, stories 13–17 Places that remember

The last five stories of the 1912 book test theories about how strong feeling can outlast the people who felt it. In Outside the Door a hostess on the Norfolk coast compares a haunted room to a phonograph record, and the house answers with the steps of Helen Denison, murdered there in 1610. The Terror by Night likens the passing of emotion from mind to mind to wireless telegraphy, and follows a death at Davos that is felt in London as a sudden bitter cold. In The Other Bed a Swiss hotel room passes a suicide’s terror on to its next guest; The Thing in the Hall is a brain specialist’s statement, read back to him just before he died, about the slug-like Elemental his friend let into their house; and in The House with the Brick-Kiln two friends take a house near Lewes where a murderer’s pictures still hang.

Outside the Door
Table-turning on the Norfolk coast, and footsteps in the passage in the small hours.
The Terror by Night
A young wife dying at Davos, and a cold that comes into a London room.
The Other Bed
A razor in a dream, a waiter who brings whisky nobody ordered, and a newspaper paragraph about the last guest.
The Thing in the Hall
Dr Assheton’s dictated account of Louis Fielder’s experiments, and the shadow on the wall.
The House with the Brick-Kiln
Watercolours of a kiln, a smoking chimney, and the ghost of a man who burned his wife.

Visible and Invisible, stories 1–4 Science at the edge of death

The second book opens on the evening of 4 August 1914, with a reclusive physicist who believes the brain keeps a record of speech like a gramophone plate, and who builds a needle to play back the memories of the dead; his subject is his housekeeper, once acquitted of cutting her husband’s throat. In The Outcast a young widow whom every animal fears dies at sea, is washed ashore at the town that shunned her, and cannot be kept in her grave, and a medieval Latin treatise suggests whose spirit she carries. The Horror-Horn is set at a winter resort in the Engadine, where a physiologist reading about the Everest expedition says he has seen the half-human creatures that leave such footprints, and in Machaon a medium’s messages about a swallow-tail butterfly lead the narrator to a treatment that cures his servant’s cancer.

And the Dead Spake
Sir James Horton’s needle, a soldier’s memory of Tipperary, and a confession from dead lips.
The Outcast
A bulldog that bolts from her, flowers that wilt on her coffin, and a grave dug too short.
The Horror-Horn
Footprints on Everest, Professor Ingram’s story, and a hag in the pine woods.
Machaon
A medium in Tilling, the son of Asclepios, and a surgeon’s letter about X-rays.

Visible and Invisible, stories 5–8 Crimes and their avengers

Negotium Perambulans brings a London barrister back to the Cornish village of Polearn, where a carved panel in the church shows a priest facing a huge slug-like creature, and where the owners of a house built from the stones of an older church die drained of blood. At the Farmhouse follows a painter through a gale on his way to strangle his wife, a drunkard from a family reputed to be witches, and to burn the house over her. Inscrutable Decrees is Archie Rorke’s account of why he broke off his engagement to the brilliant Sybil, and in The Gardener a planchette writes that the gardener wants to come in, while a jealous man who killed his wife and himself searches a rented house for her.

Negotium Perambulans
The fourth panel of the altar-rails, the quarry-house, and the painter John Evans.
At the Farmhouse
John Aylsford’s walk, his wife Ellen, and the girl who stands in the burning doorway.
Inscrutable Decrees
Lady Rorke’s pleasure in suffering, the drained lake at Lincote, and a séance with one sitter.
The Gardener
An empty cottage by a foot-bridge, a rocking-chair still moving, and a man with a rust-coloured beard.

Visible and Invisible, stories 9–12 Mediums, a vampire and the borderland

Mr Tilly, run over by a traction-engine at Hyde Park Corner, goes on to his medium’s séance anyway, catches her cheating, and discovers how much of what the spirits say is the medium’s own twaddle. In Mrs. Amworth a jolly widow back from India brings dinner parties and piquet to a Sussex village on the Brighton road, and a retired Cambridge physiologist who has been watching her recognises the vampire. In the Tube is Anthony Carling’s story of a man he saw twice before the man threw himself under a train, and of the ghost that came to him afterwards for help. In Roderick’s Story a friend with a failing heart comes to stay in Tilling, reads the narrator’s spook stories, calls them false, and tells what he saw in the room he is sleeping in, years before, when the woman he loved lay dying there.

Mr. Tilly’s Séance
Mr Tilly’s servants, Miss Soulsby, and Mrs Cumberbatch’s trumpet.
Mrs. Amworth
Gnats in a hot summer, a sick boy, a face at a window twenty feet up, and a pick in the cemetery at dawn.
In the Tube
A passenger who vanishes between stations, a lunch party, and a message for Lady Payle.
Roderick’s Story
A golf shot, a criticism of ghost stories, and Margaret’s veil.
