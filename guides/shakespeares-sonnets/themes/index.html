Time

Time in these poems is an opponent with a body: it has a scythe, a hand, a pen, and it eats. Almost every poem measures something against it (a face, a season, a clock, waves reaching a beach), and the only defence ever offered is a sheet of paper.

Through the book The early poems propose a child as the answer; sonnet 15 replaces the child with the verse; by 60 and 65 the verse is a slim hope, no longer a victory; and 126 leaves the young man to Nature’s audit without an argument.

In the text

From fairest creatures we desire increase, That thereby beauty’s rose might never die

1.1–2

The sequence begins with a general law before any person appears: we want beautiful things to continue. ‘Increase’ is a farming and a banking word before it is a human one, which sets the terms for the next sixteen poems, in which the young man is treated as capital that is not being put to work.

Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day? Thou art more lovely and more temperate

18.1–2

The question is rhetorical and the answer is no: the poem spends its quatrains explaining why summer is the weaker term. What looks like the most conventional compliment in English is in fact a refusal of the comparison it offers.

Poetry and immortality

The sequence is unusually frank about writing as work: contracts, accounts, monuments, invoices, borrowed styles, the rival’s better-funded verse. The claim that the poems will preserve their subject is repeated so often that it stops being a boast and becomes a question.

Through the book Confidence rises to the monument of 55, is undercut by the rival poems at 78-86, and is dropped without comment in the last group, where nobody promises anybody immortality.

In the text

And all in war with Time for love of you, As he takes from you, I engraft you new.

15.13–14

The turn the whole sequence rests on. Up to here the answer to time was a son; from here it is the poem. ‘Engraft’ keeps the gardening language of the earlier argument, so the substitution sounds like the same plan.

So long as men can breathe or eyes can see, So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.

18.13–14

‘This’ is the poem, named twice and never described. The claim is a contract with the reader: the young man survives only as long as someone is doing what you are doing now, which is why the line still works.

Love

Love here is rarely mutual and never safe. It is argued for, defended in the language of the law courts, withdrawn without explanation, and defined by what it will not do.

Through the book The young man is addressed as a possession in the early poems, as an equal in the middle, and in 87 as an estate that was never really the speaker’s; 116 then defines love as the thing that does not move, which the surrounding poems keep disproving.

In the text

Haply I think on thee, and then my state, Like to the lark at break of day arising

29.10–11

‘Haply’ means by chance: the rescue is accidental, which is why it is believable. The line lifts on ‘arising’ and the verse follows it, the one place in the sequence where a change of mood is audible as a change of rhythm.

Farewell! thou art too dear for my possessing

87.1

‘Dear’ means beloved and expensive in the same breath, and the rest of the poem is conducted in contract law: estimate, charter, patent, bonds. A love affair is dissolved as though the deeds had been faulty from the start, which is dignified and also a way of not saying goodbye.

Desire and shame

The last group separates wanting from admiring and looks at what is left. Sonnet 129 describes lust in the three tenses of its own trap (before, during, after) and finds the same person in each.

Through the book Desire is decorative in the first hundred poems and physical in the dark lady poems; the shame arrives with the physical detail and does not leave.

In the text

A woman’s face with nature’s own hand painted, Hast thou the master mistress of my passion

20.1–2

Two categories in one phrase, and no punctuation in 1609 to separate them. The compliment works by taking the language kept for women and handing it to a man, and the poem’s feminine rhymes keep the doubleness audible through all fourteen lines.

The expense of spirit in a waste of shame Is lust in action

129.1–2

There is no ‘I’ and no ‘thou’ until the couplet says ‘none knows well’. Lust is described in its three tenses, sought, had and hated, and there is no speaker in it: this is a trap the poem says everybody is in.

Gender and beauty

Sonnet 20 gives the young man a woman’s face and a man’s body and makes a joke of nature changing her mind. The praise poems use the vocabulary reserved for women, and the sequence’s one extended piece of female portraiture is a refusal of that vocabulary.

Through the book The 1640 edition changed pronouns to make some of these poems address a woman, which is the clearest evidence of how uncomfortable they were; modern editions restore them.

In the text

In the old age black was not counted fair

127.1

The subject changes and so does the method: the poem argues about taste instead of praising. ‘Fair’ means pale, beautiful and just, and the sequence spends its last twenty-five poems pulling those meanings apart.

My mistress’ eyes are nothing like the sun

130.1

Every comparison the sonnet tradition had worn out is named and refused (sun, coral, snow, roses, perfume, music), and the couplet then claims that the refusal is the compliment. It works because the poem never actually describes her, only what she is not.

Rank

The speaker writes upwards. Patronage, service, disgrace with fortune, a public trade that has stained his name: the difference in station comes up often enough to be one of the sequence’s subjects.

Through the book It surfaces as envy in 29, as professional insecurity in the rival poems, and as anger at public judgement in 111 and 121.

In the text

When in disgrace with fortune and men’s eyes, I all alone beweep my outcast state

29.1–2

Two lines hold public judgement and private misery. ‘All alone’ is the important phrase: the shame is social, the weeping is not, and the poem’s recovery will also be private: a thought, with no change in circumstances.

They that have power to hurt, and will do none

94.1

Praise with no warmth in it: the admired people are stone, unmoved, slow to temptation. Whether the poem approves of them has been argued for two centuries, and the case for irony rests entirely on the last line.

Lies and self-deception

The poems are at their most modern when the speaker catches himself. He forgives what he has just described as a crime, praises what he has just called false, and swears to a beauty he can see is not there.

Through the book In 35 he argues his own case against himself; in 138 both people lie and both know; in 152 he closes the dark lady poems by declaring himself the worse perjurer of the two.

In the text

Roses have thorns, and silver fountains mud

35.2

A proverb offered as comfort, and it is the beginning of a defence that goes so far the speaker calls himself an accessory to the crime. Consolation and self-disgust arrive in the same poem, which is the sequence’s characteristic move.

Lilies that fester smell far worse than weeds.

94.14

Thirteen lines of cool admiration, then a smell. The line is a proverb Shakespeare also gave to a king in Edward III, and placed here it retracts the compliment without withdrawing it: the higher the beauty, the worse the rot.
