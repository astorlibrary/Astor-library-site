Shakespeare’s Sonnets · William Shakespeare

Themes

Time

Time in these poems is an opponent with a body: it has a scythe, a hand, a pen, and it eats. Almost every poem measures something against it (a face, a season, a clock, waves reaching a beach), and the only defence ever offered is a sheet of paper.

The early poems propose a child as the answer; sonnet 15 replaces the child with the verse; by 60 and 65 the verse is a slim hope, no longer a victory; and 126 leaves the young man to Nature’s audit without an argument.

From fairest creatures we desire increase, That thereby beauty’s rose might never die

1.1–2

When I do count the clock that tells the time

12.1

Poetry and immortality

The sequence is unusually frank about writing as work: contracts, accounts, monuments, invoices, borrowed styles, the rival’s better-funded verse. The claim that the poems will preserve their subject is repeated so often that it stops being a boast and becomes a question.

Confidence rises to the monument of 55, is undercut by the rival poems at 78-86, and is dropped without comment in the last group, where nobody promises anybody immortality.

And all in war with Time for love of you, As he takes from you, I engraft you new.

15.13–14

So long as men can breathe or eyes can see, So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.

18.13–14

Love

Love here is rarely mutual and never safe. It is argued for, defended in the language of the law courts, withdrawn without explanation, and defined by what it will not do.

The young man is addressed as a possession in the early poems, as an equal in the middle, and in 87 as an estate that was never really the speaker’s; 116 then defines love as the thing that does not move, which the surrounding poems keep disproving.

Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day? Thou art more lovely and more temperate

18.1–2

O! learn to read what silent love hath writ:

23.13

Desire and shame

The last group separates wanting from admiring and looks at what is left. Sonnet 129 describes lust in the three tenses of its own trap (before, during, after) and finds the same person in each.

Desire is decorative in the first hundred poems and physical in the dark lady poems; the shame arrives with the physical detail and does not leave.

A woman’s face with nature’s own hand painted, Hast thou the master mistress of my passion

20.1–2

In the old age black was not counted fair

127.1

Gender and beauty

Sonnet 20 gives the young man a woman’s face and a man’s body and makes a joke of nature changing her mind. The praise poems use the vocabulary reserved for women, and the sequence’s one extended piece of female portraiture is a refusal of that vocabulary.

The 1640 edition changed pronouns to make some of these poems address a woman, which is the clearest evidence of how uncomfortable they were; modern editions restore them.

A woman’s face with nature’s own hand painted, Hast thou the master mistress of my passion

20.1–2

In the old age black was not counted fair

127.1

Rank

The speaker writes upwards. Patronage, service, disgrace with fortune, a public trade that has stained his name: the difference in station comes up often enough to be one of the sequence’s subjects.

It surfaces as envy in 29, as professional insecurity in the rival poems, and as anger at public judgement in 111 and 121.

When in disgrace with fortune and men’s eyes, I all alone beweep my outcast state

29.1–2

Farewell! thou art too dear for my possessing

87.1

Lies and self-deception

The poems are at their most modern when the speaker catches himself. He forgives what he has just described as a crime, praises what he has just called false, and swears to a beauty he can see is not there.

In 35 he argues his own case against himself; in 138 both people lie and both know; in 152 he closes the dark lady poems by declaring himself the worse perjurer of the two.

Full many a glorious morning have I seen Flatter the mountain tops with sovereign eye

33.1–2

Roses have thorns, and silver fountains mud
