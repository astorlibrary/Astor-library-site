Form and language

The English sonnet

Fourteen lines of iambic pentameter in three quatrains and a closing couplet, rhyming abab cdcd efef gg.

The shape is an argument machine: three chances to state a case and two lines to settle it. Shakespeare uses the quatrains as three parallel images far more often than as three steps of logic (autumn, twilight, embers in 73), so the couplet has to do the reasoning alone.

When I do count the clock that tells the time

12.1

The turn

The point where a sonnet changes direction, traditionally at line nine in the Italian form.

The English shape allows the turn to move. In 29 it falls at ‘Haply I think on thee’ and lifts the whole poem; in 94 it comes late, with the summer’s flower of the third quatrain, and the festering lilies of the last line drive it home; in 116 there is no turn at all, which is part of why the poem sounds like legislation.

Haply I think on thee, and then my state, Like to the lark at break of day arising

29.10–11

The closing couplet

The final rhymed pair, which sums up, reverses or undercuts the twelve lines before it.

It is the sequence’s most variable instrument. Sometimes it seals the poem (18), sometimes it makes a joke that redistributes the property (20), sometimes it spoils the argument it is meant to finish (35), and in 126 it is missing altogether.

And all in war with Time for love of you, As he takes from you, I engraft you new.

15.13–14

The extended conceit

A single comparison pursued through a whole poem, often from an unpoetic field such as law, accounting or agriculture.

Sonnet 87 runs entirely on contract law (dear, estimate, charter, bonds, patent, misprision), so that the end of a love affair is conducted as the discovery of a faulty deed. The procreation poems do the same with usury and husbandry.

From fairest creatures we desire increase, That thereby beauty’s rose might never die

1.1–2

Antithesis

Setting two terms against each other inside a line or across a pair of lines.

Whole poems are built from it: “comfort and despair” in 144, the before-and-after of 129, the catalogue of what love is not in 116. The balance makes the couplets sound like conclusions even when they are not.

Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day? Thou art more lovely and more temperate

18.1–2

Personification of time

Treating an abstraction as a person who can act and be addressed.

Time gets a scythe, a hand, a pen, lion’s paws to be blunted and a fickle glass to hold. Making the enemy visible is what allows the poems to declare war on it in 15 and to negotiate with it in 126.

And all in war with Time for love of you, As he takes from you, I engraft you new.

15.13–14

Puns

Using several meanings of a word together, for wit and for argument.

Sonnets 135 and 136 turn on Will (desire, the young man, the poet’s own name and the thing a woman is said to have) until the joke becomes a demonstration of how little a name secures. Elsewhere the puns are quieter and harder: “lie” in 138 means deceive and also sleep with.

A woman’s face with nature’s own hand painted, Hast thou the master mistress of my passion

20.1–2

Overstatement

Deliberate exaggeration, used for emphasis.

Sonnet 19 gives time permission to blunt a lion and burn the phoenix; 55 expects to outlast the masonry of princes. The scale is part of the argument: only a claim this large is worth making against time at all.

Devouring Time, blunt thou the lion’s paws

19.1
