Shakespeare’s Sonnets · William Shakespeare

What happens

Sonnets 1–17 The procreation sonnets

Seventeen poems make one request in seventeen ways: the young man should marry and father a son, because beauty that is not copied is beauty thrown away. The reasoning is economic as often as it is romantic: usury, husbandry, accounts, the unused estate. It also establishes a speaker whose subject is time, and a listener who never answers.

Sonnet 1
The first line states the whole sequence’s problem, that we want beautiful things to continue, and the first accusation follows: the young man is at war with himself, feeding his own light and starving the world.
Sonnet 3
Look in the mirror and see your mother’s April. The argument makes family resemblance evidence that a face is not private property.
Sonnet 12
The first great time poem: clock, violet, sable curls, harvest. Nothing defends against time except breeding, and the couplet says so with the coldness of a proverb.
Sonnet 15
The turn that makes the rest possible. War with Time is declared, and for the first time the weapon is the poem itself.
Sonnet 17
The doubt inside the boast: future readers will call the poems liars sooner than believe in such a face. A child would prove it, and ‘you should live twice’.

Sonnets 18–60 The poems in place of a child

Once verse takes the child’s place, the writing changes: the praise gets stranger, the confidence louder, and the first damage appears. Sonnet 20 makes the addressee’s beauty a problem of gender; 33–35 forgive a betrayal the poems never describe; 55 raises the monument. The group ends on the sequence’s plainest statement of loss, waves moving up a beach.

Sonnet 18
The most famous poem in English begins as a comparison and ends as a contract: the lines breathe, therefore he does.
Sonnet 20
A woman’s face, a man’s body, nature changing her mind mid-poem. The couplet’s joke about what nature added is also a division of the property: love to the speaker, use to women.
Sonnet 29
Envy, self-hatred, then a single remembered person, and the lark. The volta is the clearest demonstration in the sequence of what a turn can do.
Sonnet 33
The sun flatters the mountains and then hides. The poem forgives the young man by making him weather, which is generous and not quite honest.
Sonnet 55
Marble and gilded monuments against ‘this powerful rhyme’. The boast is conventional; the survival of the poem is not.
Sonnet 60
Minutes as waves on a shingle beach, each taking the place of the one before. The couplet’s only defence is the verse, stated without triumph.

Sonnets 61–103 Absence, a rival, and a fault confessed

The middle of the sequence is where it stops being praise. A rival poet appears with better patronage and a louder style; the young man’s affection is withdrawn; the speaker is older than his subject and says so. The poems become defensive, ironic, and in places wounded. The best of them (73, 87, 94) are about being left.

Sonnet 73
Autumn, twilight, embers: three images of ending, each shorter than the last, so the poem itself runs out of time as it describes running out of time.
Sonnet 80
A better spirit uses the young man’s name. The speaker casts himself as a small boat beside a ship, which is modest and competitive.
Sonnet 87
A whole poem in the language of property law: dear, estimate, charter, patent, bonds. Love ends as a contract found void.
Sonnet 94
The most withholding poem in the sequence. Cold, unmoved people are praised for three quatrains, and the couplet drops a smell into the room.

Sonnets 104–126 Constancy argued, and the envoy

The last movement addressed to the young man argues for a love that does not alter, and knows the argument is hard to believe. Sonnet 116 states it as law; 121 answers the people who judge him; 126 stops after twelve lines in six couplets, and the 1609 quarto prints two empty pairs of brackets where the last two lines would be.

Sonnet 106
Old poems praising dead beauties were prophecies of this face. Praise becomes literary history, and the present tense is the one thing the old writers lacked.
Sonnet 116
Definition by negation: what love is not. Read at weddings; written, on the evidence of its neighbours, by a man being left.
Sonnet 126
Nature can delay Time but not cancel the account. The poem ends two lines early, with empty brackets in the quarto where the couplet should be.

Sonnets 127–154 The dark lady sonnets

The last group changes subject, tone and manners. The woman is desired and not admired; the praise is a running argument with the conventions of praise; and the poems about lust are the most self-disgusted in English. Several of them treat the triangle directly: she has the young man too. The final pair, about Cupid’s brand and a bath, translate a Greek epigram and read like a shrug.

Sonnet 127
The opening move of the group: beauty is now black, and the beauty industry of painted faces is the thing being mocked.
Sonnet 129
Fourteen lines with barely a verb of action and no pronoun until the couplet. Lust before, during and after, described as a trap everyone can see and nobody avoids.
Sonnet 130
A catalogue of everything she is not, which ends by mocking the poets’ comparisons and leaves her ‘as rare / As any she belied with false compare’.
Sonnet 138
Two people agreeing to believe each other’s lies about age and fidelity. An earlier version had appeared in print in 1599.
Sonnet 144
The triangle stated as a morality play: the better angel, the worse, and a speaker who will only know the outcome when one fires the other out.
Sonnet 152
The sequence’s last word to her is an admission that his own oaths were the worse ones. Eyes that swore against what they saw.
