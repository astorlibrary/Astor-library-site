Shakespeare’s Sonnets · William Shakespeare

Characters

The speaker

The poet of the sequence, writing in the first person; first appears Sonnet 1

An older man, publicly of lower rank than the person he writes to, who makes his living by writing and is ashamed of how. He is at his best when he is losing: the poems of praise are brilliant, the poems of abandonment are better. He lies to himself in the last group and says so in the same line.

The young man

The addressee of sonnets 1-126, never named; first appears Sonnet 1

Beautiful, well born, and silent for 126 poems. He is urged to breed, then promised immortality instead; he takes another poet’s attention, takes the speaker’s mistress, and is forgiven in language that cannot stop noticing it is forgiving. Nothing in the sequence is told from his side, which is why readers keep inventing one.

The woman

The subject of sonnets 127-152; first appears Sonnet 127

Black-haired, black-eyed, married, musical and unfaithful: that is the whole of what the poems establish. She is desired without being idealised, which makes the praise of her more convincing than the praise of the young man and the insults harder to forgive. The name readers use for her, the Dark Lady, comes from later critics; the poems never use it.

The rival poet

A competitor for the young man’s attention in sonnets 78-86; first appears Sonnet 78

A better-funded, grander-sounding writer whose verse the speaker describes as a full-sailed ship beside his own small boat. Whether he is Chapman, Marlowe or nobody at all, his function is exact: he makes praise a market, and makes the speaker admit that his poems have a price as well as a subject.

Time

The sequence’s antagonist, personified; first appears Sonnet 5

Given a scythe, a sickle, a pen, a hand and an appetite, Time is the one figure that appears in every group. It is addressed directly, insulted, bargained with and finally not defeated: the poems claim to outlast it, and the claim is the only weapon they have.
