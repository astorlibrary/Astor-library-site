The Call of the Wild · Jack London

Characters

Buck

A St Bernard and Scotch shepherd cross, latterly a sled dog; first appears Chapter 1

He begins as a well-fed aristocrat on a Californian estate and ends at the head of a wolf pack, and London insists that nothing in between is sudden. Each stage is a piece of learning under pressure (the club, the snow nest, the stolen bacon, the fight with Spitz), so that the wildest thing he does is also the best-trained.

Spitz

Lead dog of the mail team; first appears Chapter 1

A Spitzbergen dog with an experienced sense of where power lies and a habit of laughing with his tongue out as he kills. He is not a villain so much as an incumbent, and the book gives him the same qualities it admires in Buck, minus imagination.

John Thornton

A prospector with frozen feet, later Buck’s master; first appears Chapter 5

He is the only man in the book who treats the dogs as company where others treat them as equipment, and the book rewards him with a devotion so complete it becomes dangerous to him. Buck loses him to other men, the Yeehats who kill him, and so the ending is not a simple choice of the wild over man.

Perrault and François

Government couriers driving the mail team; first appears Chapter 1

Hard employers who know ice, pace and dogs, and who are set in the book as a standard against which later owners fail. They work Buck to the edge of his strength and are the only handlers who never waste him, which the novel counts as a kind of decency.

The man in the red sweater

A dog-breaker in Seattle; first appears Chapter 1

He appears for one chapter, hits Buck until Buck stops rising, and then talks to him in a genial voice. The book needs him to state its first principle in a form that cannot be argued with, and gives him no name, no history and no cruelty beyond the professional.

Hal and Charles

Gold-rush newcomers who buy the team; first appears Chapter 5

A young man with a Colt’s revolver and a hunting-knife and a middle-aged one with watering eyes, neither of whom can pack a sled or count a ration. Their failure is arithmetical before it is moral, and London lets the arithmetic do the condemning.

Mercedes

Charles’s wife and Hal’s sister; first appears Chapter 5

She weeps over the dogs while riding on the sled they are dragging, and her kindness is one of the book’s most exact pieces of cruelty. London uses her to show that sentiment about animals and knowledge of them are different things, and that in the North the difference kills.

Dave

Wheel dog; first appears Chapter 1

A morose animal who cares about nothing except the sled behind him, and who fights to be put back in harness when he is dying of an internal injury. His death is the book’s clearest statement that work can be an identity.

Curly

A good-natured Newfoundland; first appears Chapter 1

She makes an advance towards a husky in the way a Southland dog would, and is dead two minutes later with forty dogs looking on. Her death is the whole of the book’s argument compressed into one page, and Buck remembers it for the rest of the novel.
