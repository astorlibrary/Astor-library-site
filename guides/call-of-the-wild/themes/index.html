The law of club and fang

The book’s laws are demonstrated, never agreed: a club teaches obedience, a pack teaches that a dog who goes down does not get up, and a lead dog holds his rank only while he can hold it. London never calls this justice, but he does call it law, and the distinction is the novel’s hardest idea.

Through the book Stated in the beating of Chapter 1, named in the title of Chapter 2, tried in the fight with Spitz, and finally used against human beings in Chapter 7, where Buck kills men and feels pride.

In the text

He was beaten (he knew that); but he was not broken.

Chapter 1

The bracket is the sentence’s whole method: knowledge is separated from feeling and filed away, which is how Buck will meet everything from here on. The verbs are passive: things are done to him in this chapter, and the first active thing he does with the lesson is remember it.

They were savages, all of them, who knew no law but the law of club and fang.

Chapter 2

The sentence puts dogs and men in a single category, which is the book’s recurring move and its most questionable one. It also gives the chapter its title before any incident has proved the point; the proof arrives thirty lines later, when Curly goes down.

Work in harness

Everything in the North is measured in loads, miles and rations, and the novel puts the figures in front of the reader again and again: forty miles a day for fourteen days, a pound and a half of dried salmon, a thousand pounds of flour frozen to the snow. Buck’s education is a working life, and the book’s admiration for competence is as strong as its interest in instinct.

Through the book Buck first finds that he does not despise the harness; Dave would sooner die than leave it; and in Chapter 6 the same strength is put to a wager, work made into entertainment.

In the text

For the pride of trace and trail was his, and, sick unto death, he could not bear that another dog should do his work.

Chapter 4

‘Sick unto death’ is biblical language spent on a sled dog, and the effect is to take his preference seriously without making it pathetic. The scene also states the book’s most unexpected proposition: that the harness can be an identity, so that removing it is the injury.

“Pooh! pooh!” said John Thornton; “Buck can start a thousand pounds.”

John Thornton, Chapter 6

The boast is made in the grammar of the saloon, and the narrative immediately records that Thornton did not know whether it was true and had no money to back it. What follows is the one episode in the novel where Buck’s strength is converted directly into cash, and it is the humans who arrange the sale.

Owners and dogs

Buck passes through the hands of a judge, a gardener’s helper, a saloon keeper, a dog-breaker, two couriers, a mail-train driver, three incompetents and finally a man who does not think of him as property at all. Each transfer has a price attached, and the novel’s last move is to remove him from the market entirely.

Through the book Theft in California, three hundred dollars in Seattle, a team sold for a song at Skagway to two men from the States, and then Thornton, whose claim is never written down and who is killed by other men before it can be tested.

In the text

Buck did not read the newspapers, or he would have known that trouble was brewing, not alone for himself, but for every tidewater dog, strong of muscle and with warm, long hair, from Puget Sound to San Diego.

Chapter 1

The joke about newspapers makes Buck ignorant without making him stupid, and the sentence then does the reading for him: the danger is a market, described by geography and by length of coat. Before the story starts, the reader has been told that what happens to Buck happens to a whole class of animals, which is why London keeps count of other dogs.

“Undreamed of!” cried Mercedes, throwing up her hands in dainty dismay. “However in the world could I manage without a tent?”

Mercedes, Chapter 5

Her objection is grammatically about management and actually about self-image, and London lets the word ‘dainty’ do the damage. The line belongs to the drawing room. Everything the three newcomers say is imported, and the North charges them freight on it.

Adaptation

London’s naturalism is practical: the dog who learns to steal survives, the dog who keeps his Southland manners dies on the beach, and morality is a luxury the climate cannot support. The book presents this as observation, though it is a theory.

Through the book Curly’s death sets the terms, the first theft marks Buck as fit, and by Chapter 7 he is described as a creature in full flower, the same vocabulary now used of a killer.

In the text

It marked, further, the decay or going to pieces of his moral nature, a vain thing and a handicap in the ruthless struggle for existence.

Chapter 2

Two appraisals sit in one sentence: ‘decay’ and ‘going to pieces’ condemn the change, while ‘a vain thing and a handicap’ approves of it. The double vocabulary is the naturalist position, and the novel never resolves it; it simply stops using moral words once Buck has stopped needing them.

He was a killer, a thing that preyed, living on the things that lived, unaided, alone, by virtue of his own strength and prowess, surviving triumphantly in a hostile environment where only the strong survived.

Chapter 7

The clauses pile up without a main verb after the first, so the sentence performs the swelling pride it describes. ‘A thing that preyed’ also removes the personal pronoun for a moment: the book’s most individual dog is being described, briefly, as a category of animal.

Ancestral memory

Beside the trail material runs a stranger book, in which Buck dreams of a hairy man beside a fire and hears something in the forest that has no object. London describes this in the language of inheritance, so going wild reads as a recall and not an escape.

Through the book First a half-memory by the campfire, then the wolf brother in Chapter 7, then a period of alternation in which the call and Thornton hold him equally, and finally the pack.

In the text

The dominant primordial beast was strong in Buck, and under the fierce conditions of trail life it grew and grew. Yet it was a secret growth.

Chapter 3

The grammar makes the beast the subject and Buck the place it lives, the book’s way of describing character as inherited. ‘Secret’ matters for the plot: Buck spends the chapter avoiding fights because he means to win the only one that counts.

And closely akin to the visions of the hairy man was the call still sounding in the depths of the forest.

Chapter 7

The call is introduced as a relative of the dreams, which keeps it inside Buck’s nervous system and out of the supernatural. It has no content: London never says what it wants, only that Buck goes looking for it as if it were something he could touch.

Love for Thornton

The bond with Thornton is the only relationship in the novel that is not a transaction, and it is written as a physical habit (the hand taken in the jaws, the head rested on a knee, the watch kept from a distance). It is also the last thing standing between Buck and the forest, which is why London has to kill it.

Through the book Rescue in Chapter 5, convalescence and games in Chapter 6, the rapids and the wager, and then a pool in a mining camp from which no trace leads away.

In the text

Love, genuine passionate love, was his for the first time.

Chapter 6

The sentence comes after a page of games and roughhousing, so the claim rests on evidence. It also disposes of the Santa Clara Valley: the judge’s household gave Buck dignity, partnership and stewardship, and the book counts none of that as love.

John Thornton was dead. The last tie was broken. Man and the claims of man no longer bound him.

Chapter 7

Three flat statements, the shortest sentences in the chapter, and all of them about other people’s claims on him. The novel has been careful never to let him choose the wild over Thornton: the Yeehats cut the tie, so the ending is a release.
