The Call of the Wild · Jack London

Themes

The law of club and fang

The book’s laws are demonstrated, never agreed: a club teaches obedience, a pack teaches that a dog who goes down does not get up, and a lead dog holds his rank only while he can hold it. London never calls this justice, but he does call it law, and the distinction is the novel’s hardest idea.

Stated in the beating of Chapter 1, named in the title of Chapter 2, tried in the fight with Spitz, and finally used against human beings in Chapter 7, where Buck kills men and feels pride.

He was beaten (he knew that); but he was not broken.

Chapter 1

That club was a revelation. It was his introduction to the reign of primitive law, and he met the introduction halfway.

Chapter 1

Work in harness

Everything in the North is measured in loads, miles and rations, and the novel puts the figures in front of the reader again and again: forty miles a day for fourteen days, a pound and a half of dried salmon, a thousand pounds of flour frozen to the snow. Buck’s education is a working life, and the book’s admiration for competence is as strong as its interest in instinct.

Buck first finds that he does not despise the harness; Dave would sooner die than leave it; and in Chapter 6 the same strength is put to a wager, work made into entertainment.

For the pride of trace and trail was his, and, sick unto death, he could not bear that another dog should do his work.

Owners and dogs

Buck passes through the hands of a judge, a gardener’s helper, a saloon keeper, a dog-breaker, two couriers, a mail-train driver, three incompetents and finally a man who does not think of him as property at all. Each transfer has a price attached, and the novel’s last move is to remove him from the market entirely.

Theft in California, three hundred dollars in Seattle, a team sold for a song at Skagway to two men from the States, and then Thornton, whose claim is never written down and who is killed by other men before it can be tested.

Buck did not read the newspapers, or he would have known that trouble was brewing, not alone for himself, but for every tidewater dog, strong of muscle and with warm, long hair, from Puget Sound to San Diego.

Adaptation

London’s naturalism is practical: the dog who learns to steal survives, the dog who keeps his Southland manners dies on the beach, and morality is a luxury the climate cannot support. The book presents this as observation, though it is a theory.

Curly’s death sets the terms, the first theft marks Buck as fit, and by Chapter 7 he is described as a creature in full flower, the same vocabulary now used of a killer.

He was beaten (he knew that); but he was not broken.

Ancestral memory

Beside the trail material runs a stranger book, in which Buck dreams of a hairy man beside a fire and hears something in the forest that has no object. London describes this in the language of inheritance, so going wild reads as a recall and not an escape.

First a half-memory by the campfire, then the wolf brother in Chapter 7, then a period of alternation in which the call and Thornton hold him equally, and finally the pack.

The dominant primordial beast was strong in Buck, and under the fierce conditions of trail life it grew and grew. Yet it was a secret growth.

There is an ecstasy that marks the summit of life, and beyond which life cannot rise.

Love for Thornton

The bond with Thornton is the only relationship in the novel that is not a transaction, and it is written as a physical habit (the hand taken in the jaws, the head rested on a knee, the watch kept from a distance). It is also the last thing standing between Buck and the forest, which is why London has to kill it.

Rescue in Chapter 5, convalescence and games in Chapter 6, the rapids and the wager, and then a pool in a mining camp from which no trace leads away.

For the pride of trace and trail was his, and, sick unto death, he could not bear that another dog should do his work.
