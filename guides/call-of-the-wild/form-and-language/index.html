The Call of the Wild · Jack London

Form and language

The animal point of view

Narration tied to the perceptions of a creature that cannot use language, so that thought has to be shown as sensation, memory and behaviour.

Buck understands the sale of a team as money passing between men, and the narrator regularly marks the limit (“Not that Buck reasoned it out”) to keep him an animal while granting him an inner life.

Buck did not read the newspapers, or he would have known that trouble was brewing, not alone for himself, but for every tidewater dog, strong of muscle and with warm, long hair, from Puget Sound to San Diego.

Chapter 1

Landscape

Using terrain, weather and scale to act on the characters.

Sixty below, thin fall ice, the Chilcoot Divide and a trail that has to be broken with webbed shoes are not scenery: they set the ration, the pace and the odds on any given dog living out the month.

There was no hope for him. Buck was inexorable. Mercy was a thing reserved for gentler climes.

Chapter 3

Elevated diction

Deliberately heightened, Latinate vocabulary applied to creatures and events that would ordinarily be described plainly.

‘The dominant primordial beast’, ‘the articulate travail of existence’, ‘the inexorable elimination of the superfluous’: London writes a dog fight in the language of evolutionary science and epic together.

That club was a revelation. It was his introduction to the reign of primitive law, and he met the introduction halfway.

Chapter 1

Refrain

A phrase repeated at intervals so that its meaning changes with each context.

‘The law of club and fang’ arrives as a description of savages, becomes an excuse for theft, then a rule Buck enforces, and finally the standard he breaks by killing men.

They were savages, all of them, who knew no law but the law of club and fang.

Chapter 2

Dialect

Regional speech written phonetically.

François is given a thick phonetic French-Canadian English (“Dat Buck two devils”), which sets the drivers’ speech apart from a narrator who has the whole vocabulary of Victorian science at his disposal.

“Undreamed of!” cried Mercedes, throwing up her hands in dainty dismay. “However in the world could I manage without a tent?”

Mercedes, Chapter 5

Symbolic objects

Things in the narrative that carry a meaning the characters half-register.

The club, the traces, the thousand-pound sled and the moosehide sack of gold are all ordinary equipment, and each one measures exactly how much of Buck belongs to somebody.

That club was a revelation. It was his introduction to the reign of primitive law, and he met the introduction halfway.
