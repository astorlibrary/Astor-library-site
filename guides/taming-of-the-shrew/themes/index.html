Marriage and money

Nobody in Padua marries without a negotiation. Baptista will not release Bianca until Katherina is sold, Gremio bids his tapestries against Tranio’s imaginary ships, Petruchio states his price in his first scene, and the last act is a wager laid on wives. The romance is real but it is always conducted over a contract.

Through the play Baptista’s condition in 1.1, the dowry settled in 2.1, the auction for Bianca in the same scene, and twenty thousand crowns added as a second dowry in 5.2.

In the text

To see fair Padua, nursery of arts

Lucentio, 1.1.2

The play he has walked into is about prices, and before the scene is over he has abandoned the university for a disguise and a marriage. Padua’s reputation for learning is set up in the second line of the main plot, and nothing that follows has any use for it.

What, will you not suffer me? Nay, now I see She is your treasure, she must have a husband; I must dance bare-foot on her wedding day And for your love to her lead apes in hell.

Katherina, 2.1.31

The violence of the scene is real and so is the grievance underneath it: she is describing, accurately, a household in which one daughter is the treasure and the other the obstacle. Dancing barefoot at a younger sister’s wedding and leading apes in hell were both proverbial fates of the unmarried, so she is naming the future arranged for her.

Taming

The method is deprivation dressed as care: no food, no sleep, no clothes, every refusal delivered as concern for her comfort. Petruchio borrows the vocabulary of hawking to describe it, which is the play at its most disquieting: the language assumes a creature that can be reduced to obedience by hunger and then flown.

Through the play Announced as an intention in 2.1, explained to the audience in 4.1, tested on the road in 4.5 and displayed as a finished result in 5.2.

In the text

For I am he am born to tame you Kate, And bring you from a wild Kate to a Kate Conformable as other household Kates.

Petruchio, 2.1.275

The pun runs through wild cat, Kate and cates (delicacies laid on a table): three meanings of a name she has already told him she does not use. Renaming is the first move of the taming, and it is done here in front of her while she is being told what she will become.

My falcon now is sharp and passing empty; And till she stoop she must not be full-gorged, For then she never looks upon her lure.

Petruchio, 4.1.169

Every word is technical: a sharp hawk is hungry, stooping is the dive to the lure, full-gorged is fed. The audience is handed a working method, and the calm vocabulary is the disturbing thing: this is a man describing standard practice.

Disguise and substitution

A servant becomes his master, a master becomes a tutor, a suitor becomes a music teacher, a passing pedant becomes a father, and a tinker becomes a lord. Identity in this play is a matter of clothes and repetition, the same assumption the taming plot depends on.

Through the play Lucentio and Tranio change places in 1.1; the false Vincentio is recruited in 4.2 and exposed in 5.1; Sly’s transformation in the Induction sets the pattern for all of them.

In the text

Ye are a baggage: the Slys are no rogues; look in the chronicles; we came in with Richard Conqueror.

Sly, Induction 1.2

He defends his family’s standing by appealing to a history book he has never read and getting the Conqueror’s name wrong. So the play opens with a man insisting on an identity he cannot support, which is the trick the lord is about to play on him from the other direction.

To me she’s married, not unto my clothes:

Petruchio, 3.2.106

It is a good argument, and it is made by a man who has spent the morning proving the opposite: the clothes are the entire point of the performance, as the Induction has already demonstrated on a tinker. He will use the same principle in reverse in 4.3, when a cap and a gown become grounds for ruining a tailor.

The obedience speech

Forty-four lines on a wife’s duty to her lord, delivered in front of men who have just bet money on whether she would come when called. Nothing in the text settles the tone, which is why the speech has been played as surrender, as irony, as a private arrangement between two people and as a public humiliation.

Through the play Katherina refuses to be silenced in 4.3, agrees to call the sun the moon in 4.5, and in 5.2 tells the other two wives that ‘our lances are but straws’.

In the text

Why, sir, I trust I may have leave to speak; And speak I will; I am no child, no babe: Your betters have endured me say my mind, And if you cannot, best you stop your ears.

Katherina, 4.3.76

She claims the right to speak, never mind the cap, and her argument is about who has authority over her tongue. Her next lines say that if her heart conceals its anger it will break. That is a physiological claim, and the strongest case in the play for reading the last speech as something other than a change of mind.

Marry, peace it bodes, and love, and quiet life, An awful rule, and right supremacy, And, to be short, what not that’s sweet and happy.

Petruchio, 5.2.114

Peace, love and quiet life are listed in the same breath as awful rule and right supremacy, as though they were the same thing. ‘Awful’ here means awe-inspiring, which makes the sentence a definition of the household he thinks he has built.

Naming and contradiction

Petruchio’s chief weapon is insisting on his own version of the words: she is Kate, the food is burnt, the cap is a bauble, the sun is the moon. Katherina’s resistance is also verbal, and the marriage is settled by who gets to decide what things are called, more than by force.

Through the play The wit duel of 2.1 is fought on equal terms; by 4.5 one party is naming and the other assenting; the last speech is the naming done in her own voice.

In the text

Asses are made to bear, and so are you.

Katherina, 2.1.199

She takes his innuendo and returns it as an insult in the same number of syllables, which is what the whole scene consists of. His reply, ‘Women are made to bear, and so are you’, shifts bearing weight to bearing children, and the exchange shows the two of them evenly matched at the only game either of them is playing.

Then, God be bless’d, it is the blessed sun: But sun it is not, when you say it is not; And the moon changes even as your mind.

Katherina, 4.5.19

She gives him the answer he wants and then explains the rule she has worked out: truth is whatever he last said. The third line describes his behaviour and says nothing of obedience, and productions that play the scene as a shared joke and productions that play it as defeat are both working from the same words.

Watching and performance

Almost everything in the play is performed for an onstage audience: Sly watches the comedy, Gremio reports the wedding, Hortensio spies on Bianca, and the obedience speech is delivered to a room of spectators who have money on it. The Induction makes watching a subject before the main plot has begun.

Through the play The frame is set up in the Induction and abandoned after 1.1; the wager scene reinstates the same arrangement, with the husbands as the lord and Katherina as Sly.

In the text

Come, madam wife, sit by my side and let the world slip: we shall ne’er be younger.

Sly, Induction 2.139

He has accepted the lordship, the wife who is a page in a dress, and the play about to be performed for him, all within a scene. These are almost his final words in the text: the character who has just learned to let the world slip is himself allowed to slip out of the comedy without explanation.

This is a way to kill a wife with kindness; And thus I’ll curb her mad and headstrong humour. He that knows better how to tame a shrew, Now let him speak: ’tis charity to show.

Petruchio, 4.1.187

The phrase now used to mean overwhelming someone with affection is invented here to describe starvation and enforced sleeplessness carried out in the name of care. The closing invitation to the audience is the sharpest thing in the play: he asks a theatre full of husbands whether anyone has a better method, and waits.
