The Taming of the Shrew · William Shakespeare

Characters

Katherina

Baptista’s elder daughter; first appears 1.1

She is called a devil, a fiend and a shrew before she has done anything on stage except resent being auctioned second. Her wit is faster than anyone else’s in Padua and she is never once allowed to use it without being punished for it, which is the strongest argument the play makes on her side.

Petruchio

A gentleman of Verona, come to Padua to marry; first appears 1.2

He announces his financial motive in his first scene and never withdraws it, which makes him harder to sentimentalise than productions often allow. His method is entirely theatrical (arriving in absurd clothes, condemning good food as burnt, insisting the sun is the moon), and he explains it to the audience in the language of hawking.

Bianca

Baptista’s younger daughter; first appears 1.1

She is silent and obedient for as long as it is useful, and the moment she has tutors alone in a room she tells them she will learn as she pleases. By the last scene she has married in secret, refuses to come when sent for, and calls her husband a fool for betting on her: the play’s slyest joke at the taming plot’s expense.

Baptista Minola

A wealthy gentleman of Padua, father of both sisters; first appears 1.1

He runs his household as a market and says so: Bianca goes to the highest bidder, Katherina to anyone who will take her. He asks his elder daughter for her consent exactly once, receives an answer that is plainly not one, and proceeds anyway.

Lucentio

A student from Pisa, in love with Bianca; first appears 1.1

He arrives in Padua announcing that he has come for philosophy and falls in love before the end of his first speech. His solution is to become his own servant and teach Latin, which means the romantic lead spends most of the play pretending to be someone poorer than he is.

Tranio

Lucentio’s servant, impersonating his master; first appears 1.1

Given his master’s clothes, he negotiates with Baptista, outbids a rich old man, hires an impostor father and keeps three deceptions going at the same time. The play’s most competent social performer is a servant, and it lets him get away with all of it.

Hortensio

A suitor to Bianca, disguised as the music master Litio; first appears 1.1

He disguises himself, is beaten over the head with his own lute, gives up on Bianca when he sees her flirting with a Latin tutor, and marries a widow out of pique. He then loses the wager in the last scene, which is the play’s verdict on choosing a wife in a temper.

Gremio

An elderly, rich suitor to Bianca; first appears 1.1

He is the pantaloon of Italian comedy, bidding his plate and his hangings and his ships against a young man’s invented inheritance. Beaten in the auction, he is left to report on the wedding, and his account of Petruchio in church is the only description of it the play gives.

Grumio

Petruchio’s servant; first appears 1.2

He is beaten in the first scene for misunderstanding an instruction and spends the rest of the play as the taming’s foot soldier: withholding food, describing the journey, playing along with a master he plainly finds frightening. His account of the ride to the country house is the closest the play comes to showing what the marriage looks like from outside.

Christopher Sly

A tinker, and the audience for the Padua play; first appears Induction 1

He is talked out of his own identity in under a hundred lines by people who find it funny, which is the experiment the rest of the play repeats on a wife. Shakespeare’s text drops him after the first act and never brings him back, and every production has to decide what to do about that.
