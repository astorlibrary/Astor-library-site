Friendship and betrayal

The play opens with two friends parting and ends with them reconciled, and in between one betrays the other to his mistress’s father. Proteus decides in soliloquy that he is dearer to himself than a friend, and Valentine, betrayed, calls the private wound the deepest.

Through the play The last scene pushes the ideal to its limit. Valentine forgives on the spot and offers Proteus all that was his in Silvia, a gesture older stories of friendship admired and many modern audiences cannot accept.

In the text

He after honour hunts, I after love.

Proteus, 1.1.63

The line splits into matching halves, one for each friend. Proteus sets honour and love against each other, and the rest of the play shows him giving up his honour for a love he then changes.

In love Who respects friend?

Proteus, 5.4.53

The short question breaks the verse into pieces and states Proteus’s position with no disguise at all. Silvia’s reply completes the line: all men but Proteus.

Constancy and broken vows

Proteus swears constancy to Julia with a ring and breaks the vow as soon as he sees Silvia, while Julia and Silvia keep their vows under pressure. The word forsworn runs through his soliloquies, and his name, borrowed from a sea god who could change shape at will, labels him from the start.

Through the play Near the end Proteus states the point himself: were man but constant, he were perfect.

In the text

O, how this spring of love resembleth The uncertain glory of an April day,

Proteus, 1.3.84

Spring weather, bright one moment and clouded the next, stands for his luck in love. The image fits his character better than he knows, because his own feelings turn out to be as changeable as April.

What, gone without a word? Ay, so true love should do. It cannot speak, For truth hath better deeds than words to grace it.

Proteus, 2.2.17

Proteus praises deeds over words moments after swearing a vow. His deeds in Milan will betray every word of it, and the audience finds out within two scenes.

Love and its conventions

Valentine mocks love and then falls into every habit he mocked: sighing, fasting, and calling his lady divine. Speed lists the symptoms, Lance parodies the lover’s praise with a list of a milkmaid’s virtues and vices, and Proteus teaches Thurio to write wailful sonnets.

Through the play Courtly love appears as a set of learned behaviours that anyone, sincere or false, can perform, which is why Proteus performs them so well.

In the text

Home-keeping youth have ever homely wits.

Valentine, 1.1.2

Valentine puns on home and homely, which meant plain or dull. He speaks up for travel and experience, and within a few scenes he is as lovesick as the friend he mocks.

to walk alone, like one that had the pestilence; to sigh, like a schoolboy that had lost his ABC

Speed, 2.1.18

In Speed’s run of similes the fashionable signs of love become symptoms of plague and childishness. Valentine mocked Proteus for these same habits in the first scene.

Disguise and gender

Julia travels to Milan dressed as the page Sebastian. In disguise she hears Proteus court Silvia, is hired as his servant, and is sent to give Silvia her own ring.

Through the play The disguise gives her knowledge no one else has, and in her last speech the costume becomes a charge against Proteus: it is a lesser blot for women to change their shapes than for men to change their minds.

In the text

He plays false, father.

Julia, 4.2.52

False means both out of tune and unfaithful. The Host hears a complaint about the playing; the audience hears Julia’s verdict on Proteus.

It is the lesser blot, modesty finds, Women to change their shapes than men their minds.

Julia, 5.4.107

Julia defends her disguise by comparing it with his inconstancy. Shape means outward form, and the couplet weighs a woman’s costume against a man’s changed heart, to his disadvantage.

Masters and servants

Speed and Lance see through their masters faster than the masters see through each other. Lance takes whippings for his dog, and Julia, as a page, has to serve the man who has abandoned her.

Through the play Lance’s question, how many masters would do this for his servant, sets a servant’s loyalty to a dog against a gentleman’s loyalty to his friend.

In the text

yet did not this cruel-hearted cur shed one tear. He is a stone, a very pebblestone, and has no more pity in him than a dog.

Lance, 2.3.7

The joke is in the last words: Lance complains that his dog has no more pity than a dog. The scene comes straight after Proteus and Julia’s parting and gives a comic version of it, with more tears.

I am but a fool, look you, and yet I have the wit to think my master is a kind of a knave

Lance, 3.1.263

Lance has just seen Proteus offer comfort to the friend he betrayed. The servant who calls himself a fool judges his master correctly, and tells only the audience.

Court, exile and the forest

Valentine is banished from Milan and made captain of outlaws, some of them gentlemen exiled for crimes of their own. Nearly all the main characters end up in the forest, and the play’s crisis is settled there, far from the court.

Through the play Valentine comes to prefer the shadowy desert to peopled towns, and the Duke’s pardon at the end brings the outlaws back with him.

In the text

To die is to be banished from myself, And Silvia is myself; banished from her Is self from self—a deadly banishment.

Valentine, 3.1.172

Valentine reasons that since Silvia is himself, exile from her is exile from himself, and so is death. Romeo argues the same way when he is banished from Verona.

How use doth breed a habit in a man!

Valentine, 5.4.1

Habit means custom and also clothing. Valentine has grown used to the forest and now prefers it to crowded towns, a thought the banished Duke of As You Like It develops in the Forest of Arden.

Silvia’s silence

After Proteus tries to force her and Valentine offers him his claim in her, Silvia does not speak again. Her future is settled by men: her rescuer, her father, and the rival who gives her up.

Through the play The text leaves her reaction open, so every production has to decide what her silence means.

In the text

Thou hast no faith left now, unless thou’dst two, And that’s far worse than none; better have none Than plural faith, which is too much by one.

Silvia, 5.4.50

Silvia’s arithmetic is exact: a man with two faiths has none. This is her last long speech, and within a few lines Proteus tries to force her.

I’ll woo you like a soldier, at arms’ end, And love you ’gainst the nature of love—force you.

Proteus, 5.4.57

Proteus admits that what he intends is against the nature of love, and the line ends in a threat of rape. Valentine, watching unseen, steps forward only now, at the darkest moment in the comedy.
