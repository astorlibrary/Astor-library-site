The Two Gentlemen of Verona · William Shakespeare

Themes

Friendship and betrayal

The play opens with two friends parting and ends with them reconciled, and in between one betrays the other to his mistress’s father. Proteus decides in soliloquy that he is dearer to himself than a friend, and Valentine, betrayed, calls the private wound the deepest.

The last scene pushes the ideal to its limit. Valentine forgives on the spot and offers Proteus all that was his in Silvia, a gesture older stories of friendship admired and many modern audiences cannot accept.

Home-keeping youth have ever homely wits.

Valentine, 1.1.2

He after honour hunts, I after love.

Proteus, 1.1.63

Constancy and broken vows

Proteus swears constancy to Julia with a ring and breaks the vow as soon as he sees Silvia, while Julia and Silvia keep their vows under pressure. The word forsworn runs through his soliloquies, and his name, borrowed from a sea god who could change shape at will, labels him from the start.

Near the end Proteus states the point himself: were man but constant, he were perfect.

O, how this spring of love resembleth The uncertain glory of an April day,

Proteus, 1.3.84

What, gone without a word? Ay, so true love should do. It cannot speak, For truth hath better deeds than words to grace it.

Proteus, 2.2.17

Love and its conventions

Valentine mocks love and then falls into every habit he mocked: sighing, fasting, and calling his lady divine. Speed lists the symptoms, Lance parodies the lover’s praise with a list of a milkmaid’s virtues and vices, and Proteus teaches Thurio to write wailful sonnets.

Courtly love appears as a set of learned behaviours that anyone, sincere or false, can perform, which is why Proteus performs them so well.

Home-keeping youth have ever homely wits.

Valentine, 1.1.2

He after honour hunts, I after love.

Proteus, 1.1.63

Disguise and gender

Julia travels to Milan dressed as the page Sebastian. In disguise she hears Proteus court Silvia, is hired as his servant, and is sent to give Silvia her own ring.

The disguise gives her knowledge no one else has, and in her last speech the costume becomes a charge against Proteus: it is a lesser blot for women to change their shapes than for men to change their minds.

He plays false, father.

Julia, 4.2.52

If ’twere a substance you would sure deceive it And make it but a shadow, as I am.

Julia, 4.2.114

Masters and servants

Speed and Lance see through their masters faster than the masters see through each other. Lance takes whippings for his dog, and Julia, as a page, has to serve the man who has abandoned her.

Lance’s question, how many masters would do this for his servant, sets a servant’s loyalty to a dog against a gentleman’s loyalty to his friend.

yet did not this cruel-hearted cur shed one tear. He is a stone, a very pebblestone, and has no more pity in him than a dog.

Lance, 2.3.7

I am but a fool, look you, and yet I have the wit to think my master is a kind of a knave

Lance, 3.1.263

Court, exile and the forest

Valentine is banished from Milan and made captain of outlaws, some of them gentlemen exiled for crimes of their own. Nearly all the main characters end up in the forest, and the play’s crisis is settled there, far from the court.

Valentine comes to prefer the shadowy desert to peopled towns, and the Duke’s pardon at the end brings the outlaws back with him.

To die is to be banished from myself, And Silvia is myself; banished from her Is self from self—a deadly banishment.

Valentine, 3.1.172

How use doth breed a habit in a man!

Valentine, 5.4.1

Silvia's silence

After Proteus tries to force her and Valentine offers him his claim in her, Silvia does not speak again. Her future is settled by men: her rescuer, her father, and the rival who gives her up.

The text leaves her reaction open, so every production has to decide what her silence means. It has become the most discussed question about the play.

Thou hast no faith left now, unless thou’dst two, And that’s far worse than none; better have none Than plural faith, which is too much by one.

Silvia, 5.4.50

I’ll woo you like a soldier, at arms’ end, And love you ’gainst the nature of love—force you.

Proteus, 5.4.57
