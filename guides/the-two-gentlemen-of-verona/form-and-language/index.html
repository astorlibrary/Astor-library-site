The Two Gentlemen of Verona · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Soliloquy

A speech in which a character alone on stage thinks aloud.

Proteus talks himself into betrayal in soliloquies in 2.4 and 2.6, and in a third, in 4.2, reckons up what he has done. Julia’s soliloquies in 1.2 and 4.4 are where she says what pride or disguise stops her saying aloud.

He after honour hunts, I after love.

Proteus, 1.1.63

Letters

Written messages carried on stage that drive or complicate the action.

Almost every letter in the play goes astray. Julia tears up Proteus’s letter and pieces it together, Silvia makes Valentine write a letter to himself, the Duke finds a letter under Valentine’s cloak, and Julia hands Silvia the wrong paper.

O hateful hands, to tear such loving words!

Julia, 1.2.106

Disguise

A character concealing their identity, so that the audience knows more than the people on stage do.

Julia as the page Sebastian hears Proteus’s courtship of Silvia, carries her own ring to her rival, and describes her own grief as a part she once played in Julia’s gown.

Madam, ’twas Ariadne, passioning For Theseus’ perjury and unjust flight,

Julia, 4.4.158

Parody subplot

Comic scenes, often among servants, that repeat the main plot’s concerns at a lower level.

Lance’s tearful farewell follows straight after Proteus and Julia’s parting, his milkmaid list mocks the lovers’ praise of their ladies, and his loyalty to Crab shows up Proteus’s disloyalty to Valentine.

to walk alone, like one that had the pestilence; to sigh, like a schoolboy that had lost his ABC

Speed, 2.1.18

Dramatic irony

The audience knowing more than a character does.

Valentine praises Proteus to the Duke just before Proteus betrays him, Julia praises Proteus’s truth after he has abandoned her, and in disguise she comments on his courtship in asides while he stands beside her.

O jest unseen, inscrutable, invisible, As a nose on a man’s face, or a weathercock on a steeple!

Speed, 2.1.119

Wordplay

Puns and double meanings.

Sheep and ship, tide and tied, stand-under and understand, false as out of tune and false as faithless, and habit as both custom and clothing.

Home-keeping youth have ever homely wits.

Valentine, 1.1.2

Antithesis

Opposed ideas set against each other in balanced phrases.

The gentlemen’s verse is built from balanced halves: he after honour hunts, I after love. Julia’s lines on serving Proteus set true against false, and her last couplet sets women’s shapes against men’s minds.

He after honour hunts, I after love.

Proteus, 1.1.63

Rhymed couplets

Pairs of rhyming lines, often used to close a speech or scene.

The play’s sharpest turns close on a couplet: Proteus’s decision to pursue Silvia, Valentine’s cry that a friend should be the worst of foes, his offer to Proteus, and Julia’s reproach.

The private wound is deepest. O time most accurst, ’Mongst all foes that a friend should be the worst!

Valentine, 5.4.71

Symbolic objects

An object the narrative comes back to until it stands for the argument itself.

The rings Proteus and Julia exchange in 2.2 come back in 4.4 and reveal her in 5.4. Silvia’s portrait is what Proteus asks for when he cannot have her, and what Julia studies to see what he prefers.

Animal imagery

Comparisons between people and animals.

Speed and Proteus argue over who is the sheep and who the shepherd, Proteus calls his love spaniel-like, and Julia calls herself a fox set to guard lambs, all in a play with a real dog on stage.

Yet, spaniel-like, the more she spurns my love, The more it grows and fawneth on her still.

Proteus, 4.2.14

Epithets

Descriptive words or phrases attached to a name.

Characters sum each other up in strings of adjectives. Silvia calls Proteus subtle, perjured, false and disloyal, and the Duke calls his daughter peevish, sullen, froward, proud, disobedient and stubborn.

Thou subtle, perjured, false, disloyal man,

Silvia, 4.2.83

Broken verse

Verse lines split, cut short or broken off, so that the metre shows strain.

In the forest in 5.4 the lines between Silvia and Proteus break into short pieces, and a half-line question gives away his whole position.

In love Who respects friend?

Proteus, 5.4.53

Song

A sung passage standing outside the dialogue, free to say what the speaking characters cannot.

Who is Silvia? is sung under Silvia’s window for Thurio while Proteus courts her for himself and Julia listens in disguise.

Who is Silvia? What is she, That all our swains commend her?

Song, 4.2.34
