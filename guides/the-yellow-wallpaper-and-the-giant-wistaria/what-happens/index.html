The Yellow Wall-Paper and The Giant Wistaria · Charlotte Perkins Gilman

What happens

The house A rented mansion, and a diagnosis

The narrator arrives at a house she likes and distrusts, with a husband who has decided what is wrong with her and what she may do about it. The rules are set out early: no work, no writing, phosphates, air, and the top-floor room, when she wanted the pretty one downstairs. Everything the story will need is established before anything happens.

First entries
Delight in the house, a joke about ghosts, and the first admission that something is wrong that nobody will name.
The diagnosis
Her husband and her brother, both physicians of high standing, agree there is nothing much the matter. The story’s central problem is that she half agrees with them.
The room
She wanted the downstairs room with roses at the window; she is given the old nursery at the top of the house, with rings in the wall and a gate at the stairs.

The pattern Studying the wallpaper

Deprived of company and of writing, she gives the paper the attention she would have given a book. The description sharpens as her boredom deepens: colour, then smell, then a sub-pattern visible only in certain light. Reading the wall is the only intellectual work she is allowed to do, and she does it well.

Colour and pattern
The yellow is described with a precision nobody in the house will listen to. Her eye is exact; her authority is nil.
Small talk
Affection and dismissal arrive in the same sentences, so the marriage is hard to argue with.
Night
The sub-pattern resolves. What she sees at night is a cage, and something inside it.

The figure The woman behind the paper

The figure becomes a woman, then women, then a woman who creeps outside in daylight. The journal entries shorten and speed up, secrecy grows (she hides the writing, then hides her interest in the paper), and Jennie and John are recast as rivals who want the paper for themselves.

The shaking pattern
The narrator stops describing a design and starts describing an act. The front pattern moves because someone is trying to get through it.
Daylight
She sees the woman outside, on the road and in the arbours, and says she always locks the door when she creeps by daylight herself.
Suspicion
John and Jennie are found looking at the paper. The narrator resolves that nobody else shall find it out.

The last day Tearing down the paper

On the last day of the tenancy she locks herself in, throws the key into the path and strips the room. The final entries are almost without hesitation: the voice that has apologised for itself throughout announces that it has got out. John faints on the threshold, and she creeps over him.

The stripping
She peels what she can reach and rages at what she cannot, having first made sure the door is locked and the rope is ready.
The announcement
Her last words to John have divided readers for a century (an escape, a breakdown, or both), and they contain the name Jane, which appears nowhere else in the story.
The threshold
The doctor faints in the doorway, the patient keeps moving, and the story ends on the floor.

The Giant Wistaria The companion story, seven months earlier

A colonial family disposes of a daughter’s disgrace and sails for England; a century later a party of summer tenants rent the abandoned house, joke about ghosts, and find a body beneath the wistaria root and a small skeleton in the well. The two stories are paired for a reason: the same silencing, told once from inside the room and once from the future, after everyone has stopped listening.

The prologue
A father, a mother and an unnamed daughter, whose child is not to be spoken of, sail for England and leave the house shut up.
The tenants
The new party competes to invent the house’s history, which is the story’s joke: they narrate over the woman whose remains are under them.
The discovery
The evidence arrives without commentary. The story stops as soon as it is found, and leaves the tenants’ cheerful theories standing.
