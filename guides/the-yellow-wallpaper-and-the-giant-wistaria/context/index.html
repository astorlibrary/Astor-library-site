The Yellow Wall-Paper and The Giant Wistaria · Charlotte Perkins Gilman

Context

Dates

1860
Charlotte Perkins born in Hartford Her father left the family early; she grew up moving between relatives, with an education she largely arranged herself.
1884
Charlotte Perkins marries Charles Walter Stetson She had hesitated for two years, telling him she doubted she was fit for marriage.
1885
Gilman’s daughter Katharine born; depression follows The illness the story draws on began within weeks of the birth.
1887
Gilman undergoes S. Weir Mitchell’s rest cure Treated in Philadelphia by S. Weir Mitchell and sent home told to live as domestic a life as possible and never to touch pen, brush or pencil again.
1888
Gilman leaves her marriage and moves to Pasadena Separation and a move to Pasadena, where she began to write and lecture; the divorce followed in 1894.
1890
The Yellow Wall-Paper written Composed in June, in two days, and turned down by the Atlantic Monthly’s editor, who said he could not forgive himself for making others as miserable as it had made him.
1891
The Giant Wistaria printed Published in The New England Magazine in June, seven months before its companion piece.
1892
The Yellow Wall-Paper printed In The New England Magazine for January, with illustrations; some readers wrote in to ask whether such a case could be real.
1899
The first book edition Issued by Small, Maynard & Co. in Boston, the text behind most later American reprints.
1913
Gilman’s essay ‘Why I Wrote The Yellow Wallpaper?’ In her own magazine The Forerunner, Gilman said she had sent the story to Mitchell and heard that he had altered his treatment, an account no other source confirms.
1935
Gilman dies in Pasadena Facing inoperable cancer, she ended her own life, having written that a last duty is to choose one’s own way out.
1973
The Feminist Press reprint Elaine Hedges’s edition returned the story to print and to teaching, where it has stayed.

Places

Hartford, Connecticut
Gilman’s birthplace in 1860.
Providence, Rhode Island
Where she married, had her daughter, and first became ill.
Philadelphia, Pennsylvania
S. Weir Mitchell practised here; the rest cure she was sent for in 1887.
Pasadena, California
Where she wrote the story in June 1890, two years after leaving her marriage.
Boston, Massachusetts
The New England Magazine, which printed both stories, and Small, Maynard & Co., which first published the Wall-Paper as a book.

Critics

Elaine R. Hedges

Afterword to The Yellow Wallpaper (Feminist Press) (1973)

Hedges’s afterword to the Feminist Press reprint brought the story back into print and into teaching. She read it as a story about the sexual politics of marriage and medicine, in which a woman is driven mad by the treatment meant to cure her.

Sandra M. Gilbert and Susan Gubar

The Madwoman in the Attic (1979)

Gilbert and Gubar took the story as a paradigm of the nineteenth-century woman writer’s condition: confined within the patterns of male texts and houses, the narrator sees a woman behind the paper and frees her in an act of madness that is also an escape.
