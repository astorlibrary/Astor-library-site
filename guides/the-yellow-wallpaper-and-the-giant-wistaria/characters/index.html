The Yellow Wall-Paper and The Giant Wistaria · Charlotte Perkins Gilman

Characters

The narrator

A young wife and new mother, under treatment in her husband’s care; first appears First entry

Observant, funny in the early entries, and systematically disbelieved. She is a good reader (her account of the paper is the most precise description in the story), and every skill she has is turned on the only object she is allowed. She is never named, unless Jane in the last line is her.

John

Her husband, a physician of high standing; first appears First entry

Kind in his own account and immovable in practice. He laughs, calls her a blessed little goose, carries her upstairs, schedules her hours and will not hear of a change of room. Nothing he does is cruel by the standards he holds, which is the story’s argument: the harm is in the standards.

Jennie

John’s sister, keeping house; first appears Early entries

A perfect housekeeper who hopes for no better profession, and the narrator’s warder by default. She is not unkind; she simply belongs to the arrangement. When she is found studying the paper, the narrator treats her as a competitor, which shows what the isolation has done.

The woman in the paper

The figure behind the front pattern; first appears Early entries

First a shape, then a woman stooping and creeping, then many women, then one who gets out into the garden by daylight. Whether she is a hallucination, a self-portrait or the story’s one honest witness is the question every reading has to answer.

The baby

The narrator’s child, kept downstairs with Mary; first appears First entry

Mentioned rarely and always with relief that someone else has charge of him. The relief is the most painful thing in the story: she cannot be with her child, cannot say so plainly, and is grateful for a separation she never chose.

Weir Mitchell

The physician named in the story, and the one who treated Gilman; first appears Early entries

He appears only as a threat: if she does not improve, John will send her to him in the autumn. Naming a living, famous doctor inside a piece of fiction was the story’s sharpest move, and the reason its first readers took it as an accusation more than a ghost story.
