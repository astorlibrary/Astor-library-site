The Yellow Wall-Paper and The Giant Wistaria · Charlotte Perkins Gilman

Form and language

The secret journal

A first-person narrative written in entries, in the present tense, with gaps the reader must fill.

The form does the work an omniscient narrator would otherwise do. Entries stop when she is watched and resume when she is alone, so the silences mark the surveillance; and because writing is forbidden, the existence of the text is itself an act of disobedience.

It is very seldom that mere ordinary people like John and myself secure ancestral halls for the summer.

First entry

Unreliable narrator

A first-person account the reader has reason to doubt, with no corroborating voice in the text.

The doubt is unusually double-edged: she is wrong about the paper and right about the marriage. Readers who dismiss her testimony end up agreeing with John, which is the trap the story sets.

Symbolic objects

Ordinary objects that accumulate meaning by repetition, without explanation.

The nailed-down bed, the barred windows, the gate at the stairs and the rings in the wall are introduced as the fittings of a nursery and are never glossed. The story lets the reader notice that they are also the fittings of a cell.

I don’t like our room a bit. I wanted one downstairs that opened on the piazza and had roses all over the window, and such pretty old-fashioned chintz hangings! but John would not hear of it.

Early entry

Gothic conventions

Old house, isolated setting, a locked room and a figure that may or may not be there.

Every gothic expectation is raised and then declined: the house is not haunted, the ghost is wallpaper, and the horror is a medical arrangement everybody in the story considers reasonable. The Giant Wistaria supplies the conventional version for comparison.

It is very seldom that mere ordinary people like John and myself secure ancestral halls for the summer.

First entry

Doors and windows

Boundaries that mark who may pass and who may not.

She is carried up the stairs, kept behind a gate, and looks out of barred windows at a garden she is not in. The last scene puts the doctor across the doorway and makes her get past him on the floor.

Now why should that man have fainted? But he did, and right across my path by the wall, so that I had to creep over him every time!

Final entry

Irony

Saying one thing in terms that let the reader hear another.

“He is very careful and loving, and hardly lets me stir without special direction”: the sentence means both things, and the story is made of sentences like it.

You see he does not believe I am sick!

First entry

Omission

Gaps in the telling that the reader has to bridge.

Nothing is said about the birth, little about the baby, and the worst days are represented by their absence from the journal. The most important events in the story are the ones no entry covers.

I always lock the door when I creep by daylight.

Late entry
