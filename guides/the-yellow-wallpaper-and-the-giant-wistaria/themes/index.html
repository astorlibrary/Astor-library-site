The Yellow Wall-Paper and The Giant Wistaria · Charlotte Perkins Gilman

Themes

The rest cure

Rest is prescribed and enforced: a top-floor nursery with barred windows, a bed that will not move, a gate at the stairs and a schedule for every hour. The cure and the imprisonment are the same arrangement, described by the patient in the tone of someone being looked after.

She asks for the downstairs room and is refused; she asks to go home and is refused; by the last day she has locked the door herself and thrown the key into the path, which is the first time confinement is her own decision.

He is very careful and loving, and hardly lets me stir without special direction.

Early entry

I don’t like our room a bit. I wanted one downstairs that opened on the piazza and had roses all over the window, and such pretty old-fashioned chintz hangings! but John would not hear of it.

Early entry

Medicine

Her husband is her doctor, her brother is a doctor, and the specialist is held in reserve as a threat. Disagreement is reclassified as a symptom, so there is no position from which she can be believed.

The story opens with her half-accepting the diagnosis and ends with the doctor unconscious in the doorway, the only way the narrative can get him to stop talking.

John is a physician, and perhaps—(I would not say it to a living soul, of course, but this is dead paper and a great relief to my mind)—perhaps that is one reason I do not get well faster.

First entry

You see he does not believe I am sick!

First entry

Work

She believes congenial work would do her good and is not allowed any. The story is unusually specific about what the ban costs: company, air, occupation and the right to write down what is happening to her.

Writing goes underground in the first entries, becomes exhausting by the middle, and is replaced by the only intellectual task the room offers: reading the paper.

So I take phosphates or phosphites—whichever it is—and tonics, and journeys, and air, and exercise, and am absolutely forbidden to “work” until I am well again.

First entry

Personally, I believe that congenial work, with excitement and change, would do me good.

First entry

Speech and silence

Every complaint has to be softened, and the softening is audible. She calls herself unreasonably angry, blames her nervousness, praises John’s care and then records the facts that contradict it, leaving the reader to do the arithmetic.

The apologetic manner thins as the entries shorten, and disappears entirely in the last two pages, where the sentences are short, declarative and unhedged.

You see he does not believe I am sick!

First entry

If a physician of high standing, and one’s own husband, assures friends and relatives that there is really nothing the matter with one but temporary nervous depression—a slight hysterical tendency—what is one to do?

First entry

The unreliable journal

Everything comes through one voice, written in secret, in gaps between visits. The form supplies both the intimacy and the doubt: there is no outside account of the room, the paper or the woman in it.

Early entries are dated by events and hedged with qualifications; later ones lose their frame, until the final pages are written in the present tense with the door locked.

It is very seldom that mere ordinary people like John and myself secure ancestral halls for the summer.

Secrecy

The journal is hidden, the interest in the paper is hidden, and by the end the discovery is hoarded like property. Secrecy starts as self-defence and becomes the shape of her thinking.

She hides the writing from John, then hides the paper from Jennie, then locks the door and refuses to admit anyone, protecting a secret that has become the only thing that is hers.

John is a physician, and perhaps—(I would not say it to a living soul, of course, but this is dead paper and a great relief to my mind)—perhaps that is one reason I do not get well faster.

First entry

But John says if I feel so I shall neglect proper self-control; so I take pains to control myself—before him, at least, and that makes me very tired.

Middle entry

Escape

The figure in the paper begins as an object of study and ends as a first person. The last entries move from watching a woman creep to reporting her own creeping without marking the join.

The name Jane in the final speech has no other use in the story; whether she has escaped her keepers, her role or herself is the question the ending deliberately leaves open.

There are things in that paper that nobody knows but me, or ever will.

Middle entry

The front pattern does move—and no wonder! The woman behind shakes it!
