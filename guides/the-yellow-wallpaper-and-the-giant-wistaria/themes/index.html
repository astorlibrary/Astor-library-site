The rest cure

Rest is prescribed and enforced: a top-floor nursery with barred windows, a bed that will not move, a gate at the stairs and a schedule for every hour. The cure and the imprisonment are the same arrangement, described by the patient in the tone of someone being looked after.

Through the book She asks for the downstairs room and is refused; she asks to go home and is refused; by the last day she has locked the door herself and thrown the key into the path, which is the first time confinement is her own decision.

In the text

He is very careful and loving, and hardly lets me stir without special direction.

Early entry

Care and control are stated as one thing, because in this house they are. The sentence can be read as gratitude or as a description of custody, and the story never chooses; it keeps producing examples.

at night in any kind of light, in twilight, candlelight, lamplight, and worst of all by moonlight, it becomes bars!

Middle entry

She lists four kinds of light in the order they fall on the wall, and then the word the room has been asking for. The bars are real (the windows have them), so the paper is not inventing the cage, only repeating it.

Medicine

Her husband is her doctor, her brother is a doctor, and the specialist is held in reserve as a threat. Disagreement is reclassified as a symptom, so there is no position from which she can be believed.

Through the book The story opens with her half-accepting the diagnosis and ends with the doctor unconscious in the doorway, the only way the narrative can get him to stop talking.

In the text

John is a physician, and perhaps—(I would not say it to a living soul, of course, but this is dead paper and a great relief to my mind)—perhaps that is one reason I do not get well faster.

First entry

The most important sentence in the story is delivered in a parenthesis, in a whisper, and immediately hedged with ‘perhaps’. She knows the cause, cannot say it aloud, and says it to paper, which is why the paper has to be dead before it is safe.

Now why should that man have fainted? But he did, and right across my path by the wall, so that I had to creep over him every time!

Final entry

The doctor is unconscious on the floor and the patient is still moving. Calling him ‘that man’ completes the reversal, and the mild puzzlement of the question makes the ending land: she has got past him, and does not much wonder why.

Work

She believes congenial work would do her good and is not allowed any. The story is unusually specific about what the ban costs: company, air, occupation and the right to write down what is happening to her.

Through the book Writing goes underground in the first entries, becomes exhausting by the middle, and is replaced by the only intellectual task the room offers: reading the paper.

In the text

So I take phosphates or phosphites—whichever it is—and tonics, and journeys, and air, and exercise, and am absolutely forbidden to “work” until I am well again.

First entry

The list runs on as though the items were equivalent, then arrives at the one that matters, with ‘work’ in quotation marks: his word, handed back. Her uncertainty about the medicine is comic; her certainty about the ban is not.

The color is repellant, almost revolting; a smouldering, unclean yellow, strangely faded by the slow-turning sunlight.

Early entry

This is expert writing, and the story knows it: rhythm, three adjectives that pull in different directions, and a last clause in which light itself becomes a stain. The woman who is not fit to hold a pen produces the best prose in the house.

Speech and silence

Every complaint has to be softened, and the softening is audible. She calls herself unreasonably angry, blames her nervousness, praises John’s care and then records the facts that contradict it, leaving the reader to do the arithmetic.

Through the book The apologetic manner thins as the entries shorten, and disappears entirely in the last two pages, where the sentences are short, declarative and unhedged.

In the text

John laughs at me, of course, but one expects that in marriage.

First entry

Eleven words that describe a marriage and excuse it in the same breath. ‘Of course’ and ‘one expects’ are the story’s method: the narrator supplies the defence before the reader can object, and the defence is the evidence.

But John says if I feel so I shall neglect proper self-control; so I take pains to control myself—before him, at least, and that makes me very tired.

Middle entry

The performance of wellness is itself exhausting, and she is the only person who can see what it takes out of her. ‘Before him, at least’ announces that there are now two versions of her: the one in the room and the one in the journal.

The unreliable journal

Everything comes through one voice, written in secret, in gaps between visits. The form supplies both the intimacy and the doubt: there is no outside account of the room, the paper or the woman in it.

Through the book Early entries are dated by events and hedged with qualifications; later ones lose their frame, until the final pages are written in the present tense with the door locked.

In the text

It is very seldom that mere ordinary people like John and myself secure ancestral halls for the summer.

First entry

It is a social joke, told by someone pleased with her luck. It establishes the voice the story will use throughout (light, self-deprecating, interested in appearances), and the gothic set-up is delivered as a piece of good fortune.

There is a recurrent spot where the pattern lolls like a broken neck and two bulbous eyes stare at you upside-down.

Middle entry

The first body in the paper is a corpse, seen upside down and looking back. The observation is offered in the same matter-of-fact tone as the colour, which is how the story moves from decoration to horror without changing register.

Secrecy

The journal is hidden, the interest in the paper is hidden, and by the end the discovery is hoarded like property. Secrecy starts as self-defence and becomes the shape of her thinking.

Through the book She hides the writing from John, then hides the paper from Jennie, then locks the door and refuses to admit anyone, protecting a secret that has become the only thing that is hers.

In the text

There are things in that paper that nobody knows but me, or ever will.

Middle entry

Knowledge is the one thing the household has not taken, so she hoards it. The sentence is triumphant and also isolating, and from here on the secrecy in the story is hers.

I always lock the door when I creep by daylight.

Late entry

The two figures merge here, in a subordinate clause, without announcement. The narrator has begun doing what she has been describing, and the journal records it as a sensible precaution.

Escape

The figure in the paper begins as an object of study and ends as a first person. The last entries move from watching a woman creep to reporting her own creeping without marking the join.

Through the book The name Jane in the final speech has no other use in the story; whether she has escaped her keepers, her role or herself is the question the ending deliberately leaves open.

In the text

The front pattern does move—and no wonder! The woman behind shakes it!

Late entry

A design becomes an event: something is behind it and is trying to get out. ‘No wonder’ is the tone of a problem solved, which is why the sentence is frightening: she is not raving, she is concluding.

“I’ve got out at last,” said I, “in spite of you and Jane! And I’ve pulled off most of the paper, so you can’t put me back!”

Final entry

The only speech she makes in the story, and the first sentence in it without a hedge. ‘Jane’ appears nowhere else. It may be her own name, a misprint for Jennie, or the self she has just left behind. The victory and the disaster are the same act.
