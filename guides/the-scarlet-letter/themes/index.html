The Scarlet Letter · Nathaniel Hawthorne

Themes

Public punishment

The real sentence is a lifetime of being looked at; the prison term is already over when the book opens. Hawthorne is precise about what that does: every errand Hester runs becomes a performance, strangers become an audience, and in the end the badge becomes a qualification, which is not the same as a pardon.

From the scaffold hour in Chapter 2, through the years of errands in Chapter 13, to the voluntary resumption of the letter at the end, when there is no magistrate left to require it.

Secrecy and the body

What is hidden in this book does not stay abstract: it gets into the chest, the complexion, the hand that keeps rising to the heart. Dimmesdale’s concealment is treated as a medical condition with a physician in attendance, and the physician is the injured husband, which makes the diagnosis a form of torture.

Announced in Chapter 11’s account of a life so false it hollows out reality, staged at midnight in Chapter 12, and finally exhibited on the scaffold in Chapter 23, though the witnesses in Chapter 24 cannot agree what they saw.

“I will not speak!” answered Hester, turning pale as death, but responding to this voice, which she too surely recognized. “And my child must seek a heavenly Father; she shall never know an earthly one!”

It irks me, nevertheless, that the partner of her iniquity should not, at least, stand on the scaffold by her side. But he will be known!⁠—he will be known!⁠—he will be known!

Roger Chillingworth, Chapter 3

The letter’s meaning

The magistrates intend the A as a fixed sentence; Hester’s embroidery has already altered it before she leaves the prison. Over seven years the same embroidered letter is read as adultery, as ability, as an angel in the sky, and as a judgement on a minister’s chest. Meaning here is contested, and the contest has winners.

Set up by the gold thread in Chapter 2, restated as a matter of town opinion in Chapter 13, and left unresolved in Chapter 24, which offers several accounts and does not choose between them.

Revenge

Chillingworth does not strike; he settles in. The novel’s harshest judgement falls on a man who makes another person’s inner life his life’s work, far more than on the adultery, and it is named as a violation of the sanctity of a human heart. When his subject dies he has nothing left and is gone within the year.

Begins as a scholar’s curiosity in Chapter 4, hardens into a profession by Chapter 10, and is named the blacker sin by Dimmesdale himself in Chapter 17.

Town, forest and brook

The settlement is where the letter is enforced and the forest is where it can be taken off, but Hawthorne will not let the forest be simply free: it is also where Mistress Hibbins signs her book and where Hester admits her thinking has run wild. The brook between mother and child is the book’s most exact piece of geography.

The rosebush at the prison door in Chapter 1 sets the terms; Chapters 16 to 19 test them, and Pearl’s refusal to cross the water withdraws the offer.

Ancestors and offices

‘The Customhouse’ puts two histories in front of the romance: a Salem judge in the family line, and the narrator’s own appointment to a federal post. Both are ways of belonging to an institution that judges people, and the seventeenth-century story is written by a man uneasily aware that he has inherited the bench.

Stated in the introductory sketch, carried into the fiction by magistrates who are never villains, and left with Pearl, who inherits Chillingworth’s money and leaves the country.
