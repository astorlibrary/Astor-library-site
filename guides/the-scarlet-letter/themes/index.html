Public punishment

The real sentence is a lifetime of being looked at; the prison term is already over when the book opens. Hawthorne is precise about what that does: every errand Hester runs becomes a performance, strangers become an audience, and in the end the badge becomes a qualification, which is not the same as a pardon.

Through the book From the scaffold hour in Chapter 2, through the years of errands in Chapter 13, to the voluntary resumption of the letter at the end, when there is no magistrate left to require it.

In the text

On the breast of her gown, in fine red cloth, surrounded with an elaborate embroidery and fantastic flourishes of gold-thread, appeared the letter A.

Chapter 2

The sentence puts four clauses of craftsmanship in front of the letter itself, so the reader meets the needlework before the charge. Hester has been given a sentence and has answered it with her trade: the gold thread exceeds what the magistrates ordered, which is why the goodwives in the crowd are so angry about it.

many people refused to interpret the scarlet A by its original signification. They said that it meant Able; so strong was Hester Prynne, with a woman’s strength.

Chapter 13

The town has not forgiven her; it has relabelled her, which is cheaper. The reading has a passive shape, ‘they said that it meant’, and the letter itself has not moved an inch: the same three stitches now certify a virtue, so the badge has proved capable of holding whatever the community needs it to hold.

Secrecy and the body

What is hidden in this book does not stay abstract: it gets into the chest, the complexion, the hand that keeps rising to the heart. Dimmesdale’s concealment is treated as a medical condition with a physician in attendance, and the physician is the injured husband, which makes the diagnosis a form of torture.

Through the book Announced in Chapter 11’s account of a life so false it hollows out reality, staged at midnight in Chapter 12, and finally exhibited on the scaffold in Chapter 23, though the witnesses in Chapter 24 cannot agree what they saw.

In the text

To the untrue man, the whole universe is false⁠—it is impalpable⁠—it shrinks to nothing within his grasp.

Chapter 11

The falsehood is described as spreading outward from the liar until it reaches the walls, which is a much stronger claim than that lying is wrong. It also explains the shape of Dimmesdale’s sermons: the only thing he can still feel is the anguish, so the anguish is the only thing he can preach, and it is why his congregation loves him.

“Is not this better,” murmured he, “than what we dreamed of in the forest?”

Arthur Dimmesdale, Chapter 23

He is asking her to agree that public death is preferable to private escape, and she does not answer. The question is the last of his half-confessions: even at the end he needs the other person to pronounce the verdict, and Hawthorne withholds her reply so that the reader has to make it instead.

The letter’s meaning

The magistrates intend the A as a fixed sentence; Hester’s embroidery has already altered it before she leaves the prison. Over seven years the same embroidered letter is read as adultery, as ability, as an angel in the sky, and as a judgement on a minister’s chest. Meaning here is contested, and the contest has winners.

Through the book Set up by the gold thread in Chapter 2, restated as a matter of town opinion in Chapter 13, and left unresolved in Chapter 24, which offers several accounts and does not choose between them.

In the text

she felt or fancied, then, that the scarlet letter had endowed her with a new sense

Chapter 5

‘Felt or fancied’ is the book’s characteristic hedge, and it comes before the claim. The sense she acquires is a detector for concealed sin in respectable people, which is either a supernatural gift or the ordinary consequence of being the only person in town with nothing left to hide.

“On a field, sable, the letter A, gules.”

Chapter 24

The novel ends in heraldic French, a language of inherited honour, applied to a mark of shame, and the two words for the colours, black and red, are the only description of the grave we get. A punishment has become a coat of arms, which is either the final rehabilitation of the letter or proof that it will outlast everyone who tried to fix its meaning.

Revenge

Chillingworth does not strike; he settles in. The novel’s harshest judgement falls on a man who makes another person’s inner life his life’s work, far more than on the adultery, and it is named as a violation of the sanctity of a human heart. When his subject dies he has nothing left and is gone within the year.

Through the book Begins as a scholar’s curiosity in Chapter 4, hardens into a profession by Chapter 10, and is named the blacker sin by Dimmesdale himself in Chapter 17.

In the text

It irks me, nevertheless, that the partner of her iniquity should not, at least, stand on the scaffold by her side. But he will be known!⁠—he will be known!⁠—he will be known!

Roger Chillingworth, Chapter 3

Three identical clauses, and each one is a promise: he is describing work he intends to do. The irony is that he is standing in a crowd deciding not to be known himself, so the sentence that launches his revenge is also the moment he takes a false name.

There is one worse than even the polluted priest! That old man’s revenge has been blacker than my sin. He has violated, in cold blood, the sanctity of a human heart. Thou and I, Hester, never did so!

Arthur Dimmesdale, Chapter 17

This is the moral scale the novel actually uses, and it is delivered by the man with the most to gain from it. ‘In cold blood’ is the operative phrase: the adultery is not excused, it is reclassified as a warm-blooded fault, while the sin the book cannot forgive is the deliberate, patient occupation of somebody else’s inner life.

Town, forest and brook

The settlement is where the letter is enforced and the forest is where it can be taken off, but Hawthorne will not let the forest be simply free: it is also where Mistress Hibbins signs her book and where Hester admits her thinking has run wild. The brook between mother and child is the book’s most exact piece of geography.

Through the book The rosebush at the prison door in Chapter 1 sets the terms; Chapters 16 to 19 test them, and Pearl’s refusal to cross the water withdraws the offer.

In the text

which evidently found something congenial in the soil that had so early borne the black flower of civilized society, a prison

Chapter 1

The prison is called a flower, as though punishment grew out of settlement itself and was not a response to crime; the chapter has already noted that every new colony lays out a graveyard and a jail before anything else. The rosebush beside the door arrives one sentence later, so the book’s first image is a pair of flowers, one of them a building.

The mystic token alighted on the hither verge of the stream. With a hand’s breadth farther flight it would have fallen into the water

Chapter 18

The narrator measures the miss in inches, which is the cruellest sentence in the chapter: the letter lands on this side of the brook, still retrievable, and so the whole flood of sunshine that follows is provisional. Pearl will make her pick it up within the hour, and the geography has already said she will have to.

Ancestors and offices

‘The Customhouse’ puts two histories in front of the romance: a Salem judge in the family line, and the narrator’s own appointment to a federal post. Both are ways of belonging to an institution that judges people, and the seventeenth-century story is written by a man uneasily aware that he has inherited the bench.

Through the book Stated in the introductory sketch, carried into the fiction by magistrates who are never villains, and left with Pearl, who inherits Chillingworth’s money and leaves the country.

In the text

Let men tremble to win the hand of woman, unless they win along with it the utmost passion of her heart!

Chapter 15

The warning is addressed to men in general and delivered in the imperative, which is as close as this narrator comes to taking a side. It also moves the novel’s original sin from the forest to the wedding: the marriage was contracted without the wife’s heart, and everything after it follows.

it seemed to me, then, that I experienced a sensation not altogether physical, yet almost so, of burning heat; and as if the letter were not of red cloth, but red-hot iron. I shuddered, and involuntarily let it fall upon the floor.

The Customhouse

The hedging (‘not altogether physical, yet almost so’) is doing serious work, and it establishes the rule for every marvel in the romance that follows. A man who will not commit himself to a sensation he has just had is the right narrator for a book whose final chapter cannot decide whether there was anything on Dimmesdale’s chest at all.
