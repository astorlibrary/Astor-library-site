The Scarlet Letter · Nathaniel Hawthorne

Characters

Hester Prynne

A needleworker sentenced to wear the letter A; first appears Chapter 2

She is the only person in the book punished in public. Left to support a child on embroidery, she gradually makes herself indispensable to the town that sentenced her, and the change in her (from passion to speculation, from beauty to a kind of marble calm) is described by the narrator as a loss more than a triumph.

Arthur Dimmesdale

Minister of the Boston congregation; first appears Chapter 3

A young clergyman of great ability whose sermons improve in exact proportion as his health fails, because the congregation hears the suffering and mistakes it for holiness. He confesses constantly in terms so general that every confession makes him more revered, and the novel is merciless about how comfortable that arrangement is.

Roger Chillingworth

Physician, and Hester’s husband under another name; first appears Chapter 3

A scholar who sent a much younger wife ahead to a colony and arrived two years late to find her on a scaffold. He is right about everything (he identifies the man, reads the symptoms and proves his case), and the book treats the accuracy as the crime, because he uses a doctor’s access to get inside a heart and stay there.

Pearl

Hester’s daughter; first appears Chapter 2

Adults keep calling her a symbol (the scarlet letter given life, a demon child, evidence of sin), and she keeps behaving like a bright, wild, furiously observant little girl. Her questions are the most exact in the book because nobody has taught her what may not be asked.

The Surveyor of Customs

Narrator of ‘The Customhouse’; first appears The Customhouse

A version of Hawthorne who spent three years counting cargo, lost the job to a change of administration, and claims to have found the story in an attic. He is why the book calls itself a romance: he hands the reader a forged provenance and says so in the same breath.

Governor Bellingham

Magistrate of the colony; first appears Chapter 7

He owns the grandest house in Boston, complete with a suit of armour that distorts Hester’s letter into a monstrous size, and considers taking Pearl away from her mother for the child’s spiritual good. He is the book’s ordinary face of authority: not cruel, merely certain.

Mistress Hibbins

The Governor’s sister, reputed a witch; first appears Chapter 8

She invites Hester to the forest to meet the Black Man, recognises Dimmesdale’s condition on sight, and is later executed for witchcraft. Hawthorne uses her to leave one possibility open: the forest bargain the townspeople believe in is one the narrator will neither confirm nor rule out.

The people of Boston

The judging crowd; first appears Chapter 1

They open the book demanding a branding iron and close it deciding that the letter means Able and that the minister’s confession was a parable. As a character they have one consistent trait: whatever they believe, they believe it unanimously and without memory of having believed anything else.
