Noting, overhearing and evidence

Hardly anyone in this play learns anything important by being told it directly. Information arrives by eavesdropping, by report, by a scene arranged for a particular watcher, and the play’s title puns on the word for it. Both love plots and the slander plot use exactly the same device, and that is the comedy’s argument.

Through the play Two staged overhearings in Act II and Act III produce a marriage; a third staged sight in Act III nearly produces a death.

In the text

Some Cupid kills with arrows, some with traps.

Hero, 3.1.107

Hero names the play’s method. A trap needs planning, bait and a victim who walks in, and every match in Messina is made that way.

Comparisons are odorous: palabras, neighbour Verges.

Dogberry, 3.5.14

Leonato is in a hurry and sends Dogberry away. The disaster at the wedding could have been prevented here.

O that he were here to write me down an ass! But, masters, remember that I am an ass; though it be not written down, yet forget not that I am an ass.

Dogberry, 4.2.67

Dogberry wants the record complete even when it damns him, in a town that has just ruined a girl on unwritten testimony.

Reputation and public honour

Hero’s worth is established by other people’s praise and destroyed by other people’s testimony; she is never asked to give an account of herself until it is too late to matter. Messina is a small enough world for a name to be the whole of a person’s estate.

Through the play The accusation is deliberately staged at the altar, and the repair is staged there too: the second wedding is as public as the first because the damage was.

In the text

Can the world buy such a jewel?

Claudio, 1.1.145

Claudio values Hero as a possession priced by general agreement, so a general agreement that she is worthless can undo it.

O, what men dare do! what men may do! what men daily do, not knowing what they do!

Claudio, 4.1.15

Claudio means it as an attack on Hero, and it describes exactly what he is about to do to her in front of her father.

Give not this rotten orange to your friend; She’s but the sign and semblance of her honour.

Claudio, 4.1.28

Claudio hands Hero back like damaged goods. “Sign and semblance” is the play’s subject, stated by its worst reader of signs.

Wit

Beatrice and Benedick keep up their wit so as not to be caught out, and the friends who trick them understand this exactly: both are given permission to love by being told they are already loved. Wit in this play is protective clothing, and the plot works by removing it.

Through the play Their prose skirmishing in 1.1 becomes rhymed resolve in 3.1, awkward confession in 4.1, and a scrap of bad poetry produced as evidence in 5.4.

In the text

What, my dear Lady Disdain! are you yet living?

Benedick, 1.1.97

Neither uses the other’s name. The title he gives her, and the use she makes of it, keeps each at a distance from the other.

I had rather hear my dog bark at a crow than a man swear he loves me.

Beatrice, 1.1.106

Beatrice says it in front of her uncle and his guests, which makes it a position she will have to be seen to give up.

No, the world must be peopled. When I said I would die a bachelor, I did not think I should live till I were married.

Benedick, 2.3.210

Benedick reverses a public vow in two sentences and gives a reason of cosmic size for a private change of heart.

Men and women

Beatrice’s fury in the church is that manhood has melted into courtesy: the men have the swords and the credit, and they use both to destroy a girl. ‘Kill Claudio’ puts the question of whether a man’s loyalty to another man can be outweighed by his loyalty to the truth.

Through the play Benedick does not answer straight away: he says no first, then agrees, and the challenge he eventually delivers is spoken calmly and in prose.

In the text

Kill Claudio.

Beatrice, 4.1.288

The lover’s offer of any service is taken literally. Benedick must choose between loyalty to Claudio and loyalty to the truth.

O God, that I were a man! I would eat his heart in the market-place.

Beatrice, 4.1.304

Hero was shamed in public, so the revenge Beatrice imagines is public too, and in Messina only a man could carry it out.

But manhood is melted into courtesies, valour into compliment, and men are only turned into tongue, and trim ones too

Beatrice, 4.1.315

A complaint about talk from the play’s best talker: wit is what the men have instead of action.

Benign and malicious deception

The play does not draw a clean line between a kind lie and a cruel one. Don Pedro’s trick is a gift and Don John’s is an attack, but both rely on the target’s existing self-image, and both work because being watched makes people believe what they are shown.

Through the play The Friar’s plan in 4.1 is a third kind: a false report used to cancel the effect of another false report.

In the text

it must not be denied but I am a plain-dealing villain. I am trusted with a muzzle and enfranchised with a clog;

Don John, 1.3.24

Don John claims the one virtue left to him and pictures his pardon as a muzzle. The play gives him no motive beyond his position.

Sigh no more, ladies, sigh no more, Men were deceivers ever, One foot in sea and one on shore, To one thing constant never:

Balthasar, 2.3.56

The song warns that men are deceivers, and is sung as cover for three men about to tell a kind lie. Claudio will soon prove it right.

Contempt, farewell! and maiden pride, adieu! No glory lives behind the back of such. And, Benedick, love on; I will requite thee, Taming my wild heart to thy loving hand:

Beatrice, 3.1.110

Beatrice believes what she has overheard and gives up her pride in rhymed verse, the only time she speaks it.

Repair

The comedy ends with the right people married and the villain caught, but Shakespeare does not pretend the wedding scene never happened. Claudio is given a penance, with no argument, and Hero’s first words to him afterwards correct him about who she is.

Through the play The tomb scene, the second veiled bride and Benedick’s call for a dance are three separate attempts to close the play, and the last one has to talk over Don John’s arrest.

In the text

For there was never yet philosopher That could endure the toothache patiently,

Leonato, 5.1.35

Leonato answers consolation with toothache: those who counsel patience have not felt the pain. One scene earlier he wished his daughter dead.

Done to death by slanderous tongues Was the Hero that here lies:

Claudio, 5.3.3

Claudio’s penance is a poem hung on an empty tomb, and the epitaph does not name the man who did most of the slandering.

for man is a giddy thing, and this is my conclusion.

Benedick, 5.4.107

Benedick does not defend his reversal; he makes it a fact about the species and calls that a conclusion.
