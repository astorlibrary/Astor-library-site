Much Ado About Nothing · William Shakespeare

Themes

Noting, overhearing and evidence

Hardly anyone in this play learns anything important by being told it directly. Information arrives by eavesdropping, by report, by a scene arranged for a particular watcher, and the play’s title puns on the word for it. Both love plots and the slander plot use exactly the same device, and that is the comedy’s argument.

Two staged overhearings in Act II and Act III produce a marriage; a third staged sight in Act III nearly produces a death.

There is a kind of merry war betwixt Signior Benedick and her: they never meet but there's a skirmish of wit between them.

Leonato, 1.1.49

Some Cupid kills with arrows, some with traps.

Hero, 3.1.107

Reputation and public honour

Hero’s worth is established by other people’s praise and destroyed by other people’s testimony; she is never asked to give an account of herself until it is too late to matter. Messina is a small enough world for a name to be the whole of a person’s estate.

The accusation is deliberately staged at the altar, and the repair is staged there too: the second wedding is as public as the first because the damage was.

Can the world buy such a jewel?

Claudio, 1.1.145

Comparisons are odorous: palabras, neighbour Verges.

Dogberry, 3.5.14

Wit

Beatrice and Benedick keep up their wit so as not to be caught out, and the friends who trick them understand this exactly: both are given permission to love by being told they are already loved. Wit in this play is protective clothing, and the plot works by removing it.

Their prose skirmishing in 1.1 becomes rhymed resolve in 3.1, awkward confession in 4.1, and a scrap of bad poetry produced as evidence in 5.4.

There is a kind of merry war betwixt Signior Benedick and her: they never meet but there's a skirmish of wit between them.

Leonato, 1.1.49

What, my dear Lady Disdain! are you yet living?

Benedick, 1.1.97

Men and women

Beatrice’s fury in the church is that manhood has melted into courtesy: the men have the swords and the credit, and they use both to destroy a girl. ‘Kill Claudio’ puts the question of whether a man’s loyalty to another man can be outweighed by his loyalty to the truth.

Benedick does not answer straight away: he says no first, then agrees, and the challenge he eventually delivers is spoken calmly and in prose.

I had rather hear my dog bark at a crow than a man swear he loves me.

Beatrice, 1.1.106

Sigh no more, ladies, sigh no more, Men were deceivers ever, One foot in sea and one on shore, To one thing constant never:

Balthasar, 2.3.56

Benign and malicious deception

The play does not draw a clean line between a kind lie and a cruel one. Don Pedro’s trick is a gift and Don John’s is an attack, but both rely on the target’s existing self-image, and both work because being watched makes people believe what they are shown.

The Friar’s plan in 4.1 is a third kind: a false report used to cancel the effect of another false report.

it must not be denied but I am a plain-dealing villain. I am trusted with a muzzle and enfranchised with a clog;

Don John, 1.3.24

Sigh no more, ladies, sigh no more, Men were deceivers ever, One foot in sea and one on shore, To one thing constant never:

Balthasar, 2.3.56

Repair

The comedy ends with the right people married and the villain caught, but Shakespeare does not pretend the wedding scene never happened. Claudio is given a penance, with no argument, and Hero’s first words to him afterwards correct him about who she is.

The tomb scene, the second veiled bride and Benedick’s call for a dance are three separate attempts to close the play, and the last one has to talk over Don John’s arrest.

Kill Claudio.

Beatrice, 4.1.288

For there was never yet philosopher That could endure the toothache patiently,

Leonato, 5.1.35
