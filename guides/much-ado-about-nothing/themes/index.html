Noting, overhearing and evidence

Hardly anyone in this play learns anything important by being told it directly. Information arrives by eavesdropping, by report, by a scene arranged for a particular watcher, and the play’s title puns on the word for it. Both love plots and the slander plot use exactly the same device, and that is the comedy’s argument.

Through the play Two staged overhearings in Act II and Act III produce a marriage; a third staged sight in Act III nearly produces a death.

In the text

There is a kind of merry war betwixt Signior Benedick and her: they never meet but there’s a skirmish of wit between them.

Leonato, 1.1.49

The phrase frames the relationship as a spectacle the household enjoys, which is why the two of them cannot drop it: an audience is watching. It is the uncle who supplies the label, before the two of them are on stage together: their love is a matter of public commentary before it is a matter of feeling.

Some Cupid kills with arrows, some with traps.

Hero, 3.1.107

Hero, who is usually spoken about and seldom speaks, gets the couplet that names the play’s method. A trap is not an arrow: it requires planning, bait and a victim who walks in, and every match in Messina is made that way, including the one that goes wrong.

Reputation and public honour

Hero’s worth is established by other people’s praise and destroyed by other people’s testimony; she is never asked to give an account of herself until it is too late to matter. Messina is a small enough world for a name to be the whole of a person’s estate.

Through the play The accusation is deliberately staged at the altar, and the repair is staged there too: the second wedding is as public as the first because the damage was.

In the text

Can the world buy such a jewel?

Claudio, 1.1.145

Benedick has just asked whether he means to buy her, and Claudio’s answer accepts the vocabulary without noticing it. He values Hero as a possession whose worth is set by general agreement, which is why a general agreement that she is worthless can destroy her in one scene.

Give not this rotten orange to your friend; She’s but the sign and semblance of her honour.

Claudio, 4.1.28

The image is of fruit that looks sound and is rotten inside, and it makes a person damaged goods being returned to a supplier. ‘Sign and semblance’ is the play’s own subject stated by its worst reader of signs: Claudio has believed a semblance, and he is accusing her of being one.

Wit

Beatrice and Benedick keep up their wit so as not to be caught out, and the friends who trick them understand this exactly: both are given permission to love by being told they are already loved. Wit in this play is protective clothing, and the plot works by removing it.

Through the play Their prose skirmishing in 1.1 becomes rhymed resolve in 3.1, awkward confession in 4.1, and a scrap of bad poetry produced as evidence in 5.4.

In the text

What, my dear Lady Disdain! are you yet living?

Benedick, 1.1.97

He greets her with an allegorical title, as though she were a figure in a morality play, and she takes the personification off him in her reply and feeds it. Neither of them uses the other’s name in this whole exchange, which is the shape of the defence they have both agreed to.

Contempt, farewell! and maiden pride, adieu! No glory lives behind the back of such. And, Benedick, love on; I will requite thee, Taming my wild heart to thy loving hand:

Beatrice, 3.1.110

She surrenders in rhymed verse, the only time she speaks it, and the change of metre tells us what a soliloquy would tell us elsewhere. The falconry image, a wild bird taken to the hand, is one she chooses herself, and it concedes rather more than the trick asked for.

Men and women

Beatrice’s fury in the church is that manhood has melted into courtesy: the men have the swords and the credit, and they use both to destroy a girl. ‘Kill Claudio’ puts the question of whether a man’s loyalty to another man can be outweighed by his loyalty to the truth.

Through the play Benedick does not answer straight away: he says no first, then agrees, and the challenge he eventually delivers is spoken calmly and in prose.

In the text

Sigh no more, ladies, sigh no more, Men were deceivers ever, One foot in sea and one on shore, To one thing constant never:

Balthasar, 2.3.56

The song tells women to stop grieving over men who are unreliable by nature, and it is performed as cover for three men about to tell an elaborate lie. Its advice is also the play’s plot in advance: Claudio will be exactly the deceiver described, and the ladies will be told to let him go.

O, what men dare do! what men may do! what men daily do, not knowing what they do!

Claudio, 4.1.15

The exclamation is about men in general, and it is the nearest the scene comes to self-knowledge: what men dare do is exactly what Claudio is about to do to a girl in front of her father. He means it as an attack on Hero, and the last clause, ‘not knowing what they do’, describes himself.

Benign and malicious deception

The play does not draw a clean line between a kind lie and a cruel one. Don Pedro’s trick is a gift and Don John’s is an attack, but both rely on the target’s existing self-image, and both work because being watched makes people believe what they are shown.

Through the play The Friar’s plan in 4.1 is a third kind: a false report used to cancel the effect of another false report.

In the text

it must not be denied but I am a plain-dealing villain. I am trusted with a muzzle and enfranchised with a clog;

Don John, 1.3.24

He claims the one virtue left to him, plain dealing, and describes his pardon as a muzzle and a chained block. The imagery is of a dangerous animal on a lead, and it is his own; Shakespeare gives him no motive beyond the position his birth has left him in.

No, the world must be peopled. When I said I would die a bachelor, I did not think I should live till I were married.

Benedick, 2.3.210

He reverses a public vow in two sentences and gives a reason of cosmic scale for a decision of purely private appetite. The joke in the second sentence is a real piece of self-knowledge dressed as a quibble: he did not expect to live long enough to be caught out, and he has been.

Repair

The comedy ends with the right people married and the villain caught, but Shakespeare does not pretend the wedding scene never happened. Claudio is given a penance, with no argument, and Hero’s first words to him afterwards correct him about who she is.

Through the play The tomb scene, the second veiled bride and Benedick’s call for a dance are three separate attempts to close the play, and the last one has to talk over Don John’s arrest.

In the text

Kill Claudio.

Beatrice, 4.1.288

These two words come straight after a page of elaborate prose, and they are the shortest speech either of them makes in the play. Here the comedy’s machinery, a lover offering extravagant service, is taken literally, and the audience usually laughs, which adds to the discomfort of the moment.

For there was never yet philosopher That could endure the toothache patiently,

Leonato, 5.1.35

He answers moral consolation with dentistry: the men who counsel patience have not been in the chair. It is the best serious writing in the play, and it is given to the man who, one scene earlier, wished his daughter dead sooner than shamed.
