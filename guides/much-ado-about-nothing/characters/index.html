Much Ado About Nothing · William Shakespeare

Characters

Beatrice

Leonato’s niece; first appears 1.1

She has the fastest tongue in Shakespearean comedy and uses it, by her own admission, as armour: the play hints at an earlier hurt from Benedick that she will not describe. Her anger after the wedding is the point at which the comedy nearly stops being one.

Benedick

A lord of Padua, of Don Pedro’s company; first appears 1.1

His anti-marriage act is loud enough to be a performance, and the gulling scene works by letting him surrender without losing face. He takes Beatrice’s account of the wedding seriously, and he changes sides for her at real cost.

Hero

Leonato’s daughter; first appears 1.1

She speaks little in the first half and is discussed constantly, and that makes her vulnerable: her reputation exists entirely in other people’s mouths. In 3.1 she runs the trick that catches Beatrice, so the play does show her capable of managing a scene when she is given one.

Claudio

A young lord of Florence; first appears 1.1

He falls in love by report, asks another man to do his courting, and believes an accusation on the evidence of a window at night. His cruelty at the wedding is a rehearsed public performance, planned the night before, and the play makes him accept a wife sight unseen before it forgives him.

Don Pedro

Prince of Arragon; first appears 1.1

He arranges everything: Claudio’s marriage, the gulling of two friends, and, without meaning to, the conditions in which a lie about Hero can be believed. He ends the play with no partner and one line of consolation offered to him, which several productions have made the quietest moment in the last scene.

Don John

Don Pedro’s illegitimate brother; first appears 1.1

He announces that he is a villain in the play’s third scene and offers no reason beyond his position, which is the interesting thing about him: a defeated bastard brother in a household where legitimacy is everything. He needs to invent almost nothing, because the men around him are ready to believe it.

Leonato

Governor of Messina, Hero’s father; first appears 1.1

Hospitable, pleased with his household, and the first person in the church to believe the accusation against his own daughter. His grief in 5.1 produces the best serious verse in the play, and it is directed at the men who wronged her only after a friar has told him she is innocent.

Dogberry

Constable of the watch; first appears 3.3

He reverses almost every word he uses and is right about almost every fact. The play’s crime is solved by men the gentry cannot be bothered to listen to, and the running joke about being written down an ass carries a real grievance about who is believed.

Margaret

One of Hero’s gentlewomen; first appears 2.1

She stands at the window in her mistress’s clothes and is the physical cause of the catastrophe, and the play excuses her in a line and a half without asking what she thought she was doing. Her bawdy jokes on the wedding morning are the last cheerful thing said before the church scene.
