Much Ado About Nothing · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Prose as the main medium

Unmetred speech carrying the substance of a play as well as its low comedy.

Most of the play is prose, and the lovers’ most important exchanges are in it; verse arrives for ceremony, grief and the moment Beatrice surrenders in rhyme at the end of 3.1.

There is a kind of merry war betwixt Signior Benedick and her: they never meet but there's a skirmish of wit between them.

Leonato, 1.1.49

Eavesdropping

A scene performed by characters who know they are being overheard, for a listener who thinks he is not seen.

Shakespeare builds the same scene twice, once for each lover, and gives the audience the pleasure of watching a performance and its dupe at the same time.

No, the world must be peopled. When I said I would die a bachelor, I did not think I should live till I were married.

Benedick, 2.3.210

Malapropism

Using a word that sounds like the intended one and means something else, usually the opposite.

Dogberry’s comparisons are odorous and his suspects are full of piety; the joke works because the errors keep accidentally telling the truth about Messina.

Comparisons are odorous: palabras, neighbour Verges.

Dogberry, 3.5.14

The pun on nothing and noting

A double meaning carried by two words that sounded almost identical in early modern English.

The title names both the fuss over a non-event and the play’s real mechanism, observation; the same pun runs through Balthasar’s scene about musical notes in 2.3.

I do love nothing in the world so well as you: is not that strange?

Benedick, 4.1.267

Offstage action

Withholding the decisive action from the audience and showing only its report.

The window scene that ruins Hero is never staged; the audience hears it planned and hears it described, which puts them alongside the watch and not alongside Claudio.

Done to death by slanderous tongues Was the Hero that here lies:

Claudio, 5.3.3

Song as commentary

A lyric that states the play’s theme from outside the action.

Balthasar’s ‘Sigh no more, ladies’ advises women to expect men to be deceivers, and it is sung to distract Benedick immediately before he is deceived.

Sigh no more, ladies, sigh no more, Men were deceivers ever, One foot in sea and one on shore, To one thing constant never:

Balthasar, 2.3.56
