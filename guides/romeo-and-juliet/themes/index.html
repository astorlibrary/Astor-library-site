Romeo and Juliet · William Shakespeare

Themes

Haste and timing

The play runs on a clock. Brooke’s poem takes months; Shakespeare takes four days, and then fills them with deadlines (Thursday, the dawn, forty-two hours, three hours of waiting for a Nurse). Almost every disaster is a matter of minutes more than malice.

The Friar’s warnings against speed in 2.3 and 2.6 are ignored by everyone including himself, and by Act V the plot has been reduced to a race between a letter and a horse.

My only love sprung from my only hate! Too early seen unknown, and known too late!

Juliet, 1.5.135

It is too rash, too unadvised, too sudden; Too like the lightning, which doth cease to be Ere one can say 'It lightens.'

Juliet, 2.2.118

The feud and civic order

Nobody in the play can say what the quarrel is about. It is inherited, never argued, and it is carried out mostly by servants and young men with nothing else to do. The Prince’s authority works only in the scene in which he is standing.

Three street brawls mark the play’s three movements, and the last of them kills a man related to neither house.

From forth the fatal loins of these two foes A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life;

Chorus, 1.0.5

What, drawn, and talk of peace! I hate the word, As I hate hell, all Montagues, and thee

Tybalt, 1.1.59

Love and desire

Verona offers several competing accounts of what love is: Mercutio’s obscenity, the Nurse’s memory of bruised knees, Paris’s negotiation with a father, Romeo’s Petrarchan sighing over Rosaline. The lovers have to invent something that fits none of them, and they do it inside fourteen lines.

Romeo’s borrowed oxymorons in Act I give way to shared speech in 1.5 and plain future-tense planning in 2.2.

O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright! It seems she hangs upon the cheek of night As a rich jewel in an Ethiop’s ear; Beauty too rich for use, for earth too dear!

Romeo, 1.5.42

If I profane with my unworthiest hand This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this, My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss.

Romeo, 1.5.91

Parents, children and obedience

The adults in this play are not monsters; they are simply certain they know best and unable to be told otherwise. Capulet’s swing from indulgence to threat in the space of two acts shows what paternal affection is worth in Verona once it is contradicted.

Capulet asks for Juliet’s consent in 1.2, ignores it in 3.4, and threatens to disown her in 3.5; her mother refuses to intervene, and the Nurse, shouted down, advises her to marry Paris.

My child is yet a stranger in the world; She hath not seen the change of fourteen years, Let two more summers wither in their pride, Ere we may think her ripe to be a bride.

Capulet, 1.2.8

And you be not, hang, beg, starve, die in the streets, For, by my soul, I'll ne'er acknowledge thee,

Capulet, 3.5.192

Fate, stars and accident

The Prologue calls the lovers star-cross’d before they appear, and the characters keep blaming the heavens. What the play actually stages is a chain of contingencies: a servant who cannot read, a plague quarantine, a friar who reaches the tomb moments too late.

Romeo moves from a vague misgiving about the stars in 1.4 to defying them in 5.1, and in the tomb the Friar says that a greater power has thwarted the plan.

From forth the fatal loins of these two foes A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life;

Chorus, 1.0.5

Virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied; And vice sometimes by action dignified.

Friar Laurence, 2.3.21

Love and death

Juliet says her grave will be her wedding bed within minutes of meeting Romeo, and the play sustains the equation: a bride laid out for a funeral, a tomb described as a lantern, a husband who imagines Death keeping his wife for a lover.

What begins as courtly hyperbole in 1.5 is literalised in 4.5 when the wedding music is turned to a dirge, and again in 5.3.
