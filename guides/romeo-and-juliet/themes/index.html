Haste and timing

The play runs on a clock. Brooke’s poem takes months; Shakespeare takes four days, and then fills them with deadlines (Thursday, the dawn, forty-two hours, three hours of waiting for a Nurse). Almost every disaster is a matter of minutes more than malice.

Through the play The Friar’s warnings against speed in 2.3 and 2.6 are ignored by everyone including himself, and by Act V the plot has been reduced to a race between a letter and a horse.

In the text

It is too rash, too unadvised, too sudden; Too like the lightning, which doth cease to be Ere one can say ‘It lightens.’

Juliet, 2.2.118

Juliet names the danger more clearly than anyone, in a simile chosen for its speed, and proposes marriage thirty lines later.

Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast.

Friar Laurence, 2.3.94

Sound advice from the Friar, who has just agreed to marry two people who met the night before.

These violent delights have violent ends And in their triumph die, like fire and powder, Which as they kiss consume

Friar Laurence, 2.6.9

The Friar compares quick pleasures to gunpowder meeting flame, and then performs the wedding he is warning against.

The feud and civic order

Nobody in the play can say what the quarrel is about. It is inherited, never argued, and it is carried out mostly by servants and young men with nothing else to do. The Prince’s authority works only in the scene in which he is standing.

Through the play Three street brawls mark the play’s three movements, and the last of them kills a man related to neither house.

In the text

What, drawn, and talk of peace! I hate the word, As I hate hell, all Montagues, and thee

Tybalt, 1.1.59

Tybalt hates the word “peace” in the same breath as hell and all Montagues. The feud is something he is, not something he can explain.

What’s in a name? that which we call a rose By any other name would smell as sweet;

Juliet, 2.2.43

Juliet argues that a name is only a word. In Verona it is not: a name is enough to get Mercutio killed.

A plague o’ both your houses! I am sped.

Mercutio, 3.1.86

Mercutio belongs to neither house and dies for their quarrel. He curses both, and both go on to lose their children.

Love and desire

Verona offers several competing accounts of what love is: Mercutio’s obscenity, the Nurse’s memory of bruised knees, Paris’s negotiation with a father, Romeo’s Petrarchan sighing over Rosaline. The lovers have to invent something that fits none of them, and they do it inside fourteen lines.

Through the play Romeo’s borrowed oxymorons in Act I give way to shared speech in 1.5 and plain future-tense planning in 2.2.

In the text

If I profane with my unworthiest hand This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this, My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss.

Romeo, 1.5.91

Romeo offers four lines and Juliet answers in the same rhyme scheme, so their first conversation becomes a single sonnet.

It is the east, and Juliet is the sun.

Romeo, 2.2.3

Plain words from a man who has spent the first act in paradoxes about Rosaline. The new love sounds nothing like the old one.

My bounty is as boundless as the sea, My love as deep; the more I give to thee, The more I have, for both are infinite.

Juliet, 2.2.133

In a city of dowries and debts, Juliet describes a love that grows as it is given away.

Parents, children and obedience

The adults in this play are not monsters; they are simply certain they know best and unable to be told otherwise. Capulet’s swing from indulgence to threat in the space of two acts shows what paternal affection is worth in Verona once it is contradicted.

Through the play Capulet asks for Juliet’s consent in 1.2, ignores it in 3.4, and threatens to disown her in 3.5; her mother refuses to intervene, and the Nurse, shouted down, advises her to marry Paris.

In the text

My child is yet a stranger in the world; She hath not seen the change of fourteen years, Let two more summers wither in their pride, Ere we may think her ripe to be a bride.

Capulet, 1.2.8

Capulet at his most reasonable, asking Paris to wait two years. The threats later come from the same man once he has been contradicted.

And you be not, hang, beg, starve, die in the streets, For, by my soul, I’ll ne’er acknowledge thee,

Capulet, 3.5.192

Four verbs, each worse than the last. Neither Juliet’s mother nor the Nurse speaks up for her.

Go, counsellor; Thou and my bosom henceforth shall be twain.

Juliet, 3.5.237

Juliet ends fourteen years of trust in the Nurse in a line and a half, and is left with no adult in the house on her side.

Fate, stars and accident

The Prologue calls the lovers star-cross’d before they appear, and the characters keep blaming the heavens. What the play actually stages is a chain of contingencies: a servant who cannot read, a plague quarantine, a friar who reaches the tomb moments too late.

Through the play Romeo moves from a vague misgiving about the stars in 1.4 to defying them in 5.1, and in the tomb the Friar says that a greater power has thwarted the plan.

In the text

From forth the fatal loins of these two foes A pair of star-cross’d lovers take their life;

Chorus, Prologue.5

The Prologue gives away the ending before the lovers appear. “Take their life” means both to be born and to die by their own hands.

O, I am fortune’s fool!

Romeo, 3.1.131

Romeo has just killed Tybalt and blames fortune. The audience has watched him choose.

Is it even so? then I defy you, stars!

Romeo, 5.1.24

Romeo answers the Prologue’s “star-cross’d” by defying the stars, and acts at once on news that is false.

Love and death

Juliet says her grave will be her wedding bed within minutes of meeting Romeo, and the play sustains the equation: a bride laid out for a funeral, a tomb described as a lantern, a husband who imagines Death keeping his wife for a lover.

Through the play What begins as courtly hyperbole in 1.5 is literalised in 4.5 when the wedding music is turned to a dirge, and again in 5.3.

In the text

O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright! It seems she hangs upon the cheek of night As a rich jewel in an Ethiop’s ear; Beauty too rich for use, for earth too dear!

Romeo, 1.5.42

From the first sight of her, Romeo sees Juliet as light against darkness. The image runs on to the tomb, which he calls a lantern.

I have a faint cold fear thrills through my veins, That almost freezes up the heat of life

Juliet, 4.3.15

Before she drinks, Juliet imagines waking in the vault among her ancestors’ bones, and drinks anyway.

Yea, noise? then I’ll be brief. O happy dagger! This is thy sheath; there rust, and let me die.

Juliet, 5.3.172

Thirteen words and a stab. Calling her body the dagger’s sheath makes her death a consummation of the marriage.
