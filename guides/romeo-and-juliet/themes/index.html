Haste and timing

The play runs on a clock. Brooke’s poem takes months; Shakespeare takes four days, and then fills them with deadlines (Thursday, the dawn, forty-two hours, three hours of waiting for a Nurse). Almost every disaster is a matter of minutes more than malice.

Through the play The Friar’s warnings against speed in 2.3 and 2.6 are ignored by everyone including himself, and by Act V the plot has been reduced to a race between a letter and a horse.

In the text

Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast.

Friar Laurence, 2.3.94

It is the exit line of a scene in which the Friar has just agreed to marry two teenagers who met the previous night. The advice is sound, the speaker ignores it, and the consequence comes twice over: a messenger who does not arrive and a friar who arrives half a minute late.

Is it even so? then I defy you, stars!

Romeo, 5.1.24

The Prologue called him star-cross’d; here he answers the Prologue directly, and the defiance takes the form of doing exactly what the stars were said to have arranged. He is acting on news that is fifteen minutes out of date, which is the only thing wrong with the whole plan.

The feud and civic order

Nobody in the play can say what the quarrel is about. It is inherited, never argued, and it is carried out mostly by servants and young men with nothing else to do. The Prince’s authority works only in the scene in which he is standing.

Through the play Three street brawls mark the play’s three movements, and the last of them kills a man related to neither house.

In the text

What, drawn, and talk of peace! I hate the word, As I hate hell, all Montagues, and thee

Tybalt, 1.1.59

He hates a word before he hates a person: the grammar puts ‘peace’, ‘hell’, ‘all Montagues’ and ‘thee’ into the same list, as though they were the same kind of thing. It is the clearest statement in the play that the feud is an identity somebody would have to give up, more than a grievance.

What’s in a name? that which we call a rose By any other name would smell as sweet;

Juliet, 2.2.43

The argument is philosophically sound and turns out to be false in Verona, where names cost lives. Juliet is testing whether language can be separated from the world, and the rest of the play is the experiment failing: a name gets Mercutio killed and a letter that never arrives kills the rest.

Love and desire

Verona offers several competing accounts of what love is: Mercutio’s obscenity, the Nurse’s memory of bruised knees, Paris’s negotiation with a father, Romeo’s Petrarchan sighing over Rosaline. The lovers have to invent something that fits none of them, and they do it inside fourteen lines.

Through the play Romeo’s borrowed oxymorons in Act I give way to shared speech in 1.5 and plain future-tense planning in 2.2.

In the text

O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright! It seems she hangs upon the cheek of night As a rich jewel in an Ethiop’s ear; Beauty too rich for use, for earth too dear!

Romeo, 1.5.42

He does not know her name and is already making her a light source in a room full of torches. The imagery of brightness set in darkness runs from here to the tomb, where Romeo will call a burial vault a lantern; the play’s love scenes are all lit against black.

It is the east, and Juliet is the sun.

Romeo, 2.2.3

Nine monosyllables and one name, which is unusually plain for a man who has spent Act I in elaborate paradoxes. The metaphor also reverses the scene’s conditions: it is the middle of the night, and he is describing a sunrise.

Parents, children and obedience

The adults in this play are not monsters; they are simply certain they know best and unable to be told otherwise. Capulet’s swing from indulgence to threat in the space of two acts shows what paternal affection is worth in Verona once it is contradicted.

Through the play Capulet asks for Juliet’s consent in 1.2, ignores it in 3.4, and threatens to disown her in 3.5; her mother refuses to intervene, and the Nurse, shouted down, advises her to marry Paris.

In the text

My child is yet a stranger in the world; She hath not seen the change of fourteen years, Let two more summers wither in their pride, Ere we may think her ripe to be a bride.

Capulet, 1.2.8

This is the same man who in Act III gives his daughter four days to marry or be turned out of doors, and he has not changed his opinion of her in between; he has only been contradicted. Shakespeare plants his reasonableness early so that the later scene registers as more than a bad temper.

And you be not, hang, beg, starve, die in the streets, For, by my soul, I’ll ne’er acknowledge thee,

Capulet, 3.5.192

Four verbs with nothing between them, each one a worse outcome than the last, from a man who two acts earlier wanted her consent before anything was agreed. Neither her mother nor her nurse contradicts him, and the scene ends with Juliet alone on stage for the first time since the orchard.

Fate, stars and accident

The Prologue calls the lovers star-cross’d before they appear, and the characters keep blaming the heavens. What the play actually stages is a chain of contingencies: a servant who cannot read, a plague quarantine, a friar who reaches the tomb moments too late.

Through the play Romeo moves from a vague misgiving about the stars in 1.4 to defying them in 5.1, and in the tomb the Friar says that a greater power has thwarted the plan.

In the text

From forth the fatal loins of these two foes A pair of star-cross’d lovers take their life;

Chorus, Prologue.5

‘Take their life’ means both to be born and to kill themselves, so the sentence contains the whole plot in three words. Shakespeare gives away the ending on purpose: the audience knows what happens and is asked how long it takes, a question the rest of the play answers in days and hours.

Virtue itself turns vice, being misapplied; And vice sometimes by action dignified.

Friar Laurence, 2.3.21

He is talking about plants, and he has just said that the same flower cheers when smelt and kills when tasted. The couplet is also his own defence in advance: everything he does from here is a good thing misapplied, and he will use one of those flowers to fake a death.

Love and death

Juliet says her grave will be her wedding bed within minutes of meeting Romeo, and the play sustains the equation: a bride laid out for a funeral, a tomb described as a lantern, a husband who imagines Death keeping his wife for a lover.

Through the play What begins as courtly hyperbole in 1.5 is literalised in 4.5 when the wedding music is turned to a dirge, and again in 5.3.

In the text

I have a faint cold fear thrills through my veins, That almost freezes up the heat of life

Juliet, 4.3.15

She describes the drug’s effect before she has taken it: she has already imagined waking among her ancestors’ bones and is going through with it anyway. It is the longest she is left alone on stage, and she spends it testing the plan for flaws: what if the mixture fails, what if the Friar means to poison her, what if she wakes before Romeo comes.

O true apothecary! Thy drugs are quick. Thus with a kiss I die.

Romeo, 5.3.121

He praises the tradesman for the quality of the goods, which is a startling thing to do in the middle of a suicide, and the compliment is about speed. The last words make the poison a kiss, so the drug and the marriage meet in one action.
