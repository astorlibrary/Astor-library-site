Romeo and Juliet · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Sonnet form in dialogue

Fourteen lines of rhymed iambic pentameter, here divided between two speakers.

The Chorus speaks a sonnet, and then Romeo and Juliet build one together at the feast, each taking a quatrain, then sharing the third quatrain and the couplet: the form itself performs the meeting.

From forth the fatal loins of these two foes A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life;

Chorus, 1.0.5

Oxymoron

Two contradictory terms yoked into a single phrase.

Romeo’s loving hate and cold fire in Act I are a poet’s exercise; by 3.2 Juliet’s beautiful tyrant and damned saint are doing real work, because she has just been told her husband killed her cousin.

My only love sprung from my only hate! Too early seen unknown, and known too late!

Juliet, 1.5.135

Light and dark imagery

A repeated contrast of brightness and night used to organise a play’s images.

The declarations happen at night and the street killings in the heat of the day: torches, a jewel in an ear, the sun at the window, and a tomb that Romeo says is full of light because Juliet is in it.

O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright! It seems she hangs upon the cheek of night As a rich jewel in an Ethiop’s ear; Beauty too rich for use, for earth too dear!

Romeo, 1.5.42

Prose and bawdy

Unmetered speech, often obscene, set against the verse of the main plot.

Sampson, Gregory, Mercutio and the Nurse keep a running physical commentary on sex that the lovers’ verse never acknowledges; the two registers meet only when Juliet dismisses the Nurse.

Go, counsellor; Thou and my bosom henceforth shall be twain.

Juliet, 3.5.237

Dramatic irony

The audience knows something the characters on stage do not.

The Prologue removes suspense deliberately, so that the Nurse’s teasing, Capulet’s wedding plans and the whole of Act IV are watched by an audience already told how they end.

From forth the fatal loins of these two foes A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life;

Chorus, 1.0.5

Stichomythia

Rapid exchange of single lines between two speakers.

The pilgrim exchange at the feast and the last calls of the balcony scene (‘Romeo!’ ‘My dear?’) run line for line, so the lovers sound like two halves of one speaker.

If I profane with my unworthiest hand This holy shrine, the gentle sin is this, My lips, two blushing pilgrims, ready stand To smooth that rough touch with a tender kiss.

Romeo, 1.5.91
