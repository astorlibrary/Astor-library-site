Adventures of Huckleberry Finn · Mark Twain

Themes

Conscience

Huck’s conscience is a machine his society built, and it works perfectly: it tells him that helping Jim is theft and a sin, and it makes him feel filthy for doing it. The novel shows a boy acting against everything he believes and calling the result wickedness; he never decides, in so many words, that slavery is wrong.

First felt as an itch in Chapter 16, argued out and abandoned at the end of the same chapter, and brought to a decision in Chapter 31 when he tears up the letter and takes the damnation with it.

She said it was wicked to say what I said; said she wouldn’t say it for the whole world; she was going to live so as to go to the good place. Well, I couldn’t see no advantage in going where she was going, so I made up my mind I wouldn’t try for it.

Chapter 1

“Well, I b’lieve you, Huck. I⁠—I run off.”

Jim, Chapter 8

The river and the shore

The raft holds talk, food, work and the only sustained kindness in the book, and it is moving in the wrong direction the whole time. Twain will not allow the water to be a symbol of liberty: it is a road, a workplace and a commercial highway that carries two fugitives deeper into the country they are escaping.

Established in the night passages of Chapters 12 and 19, undercut when they pass Cairo in the fog, and closed off entirely when the raft is boarded in Chapter 19 by two men who take it over.

“Yes; en I’s rich now, come to look at it. I owns mysef, en I’s wuth eight hund’d dollars. I wisht I had de money, I wouldn’ want no mo’.”

Jim, Chapter 8

En all you wuz thinkin’ ’bout wuz how you could make a fool uv ole Jim wid a lie. Dat truck dah is trash; en trash is what people is dat puts dirt on de head er dey fren’s en makes ’em ashamed.

Jim, Chapter 15

People as property

The novel’s most chilling sentences are arithmetic. Jim is worth eight hundred dollars, and says so about himself; he is sold for forty by a man who wanted a drink; a reward of two hundred is advertised for his return. The system is shown through prices, which is why the book does not need a speech against it.

Named in Chapter 8 when Jim explains why he ran, priced in Chapter 31, and finally made absurd in Chapter 43 when Tom pays him forty dollars for having been a prisoner.

“Well, I b’lieve you, Huck. I⁠—I run off.”

Jim, Chapter 8

“Yes; en I’s rich now, come to look at it. I owns mysef, en I’s wuth eight hund’d dollars. I wisht I had de money, I wouldn’ want no mo’.”

Jim, Chapter 8

Performance and fraud

Almost every character is playing somebody else: Huck is a girl, an orphan, a servant and then Tom Sawyer; the duke and the king are royalty; Tom conducts an escape according to books. The river towns are taken in because they want the show, and the novel is interested in who is left paying when the audience goes home.

Comic from Chapter 19, turns ugly at the Wilks funeral, and becomes cruelty in the last ten chapters, when the performance is staged around a man who cannot leave.

“Well, that’s what I’m a-saying; all kings is mostly rapscallions, as fur as I can make out.”

Huckleberry Finn, Chapter 23

“Good-bye. I’m going to do everything just as you’ve told me; and if I don’t ever see you again, I shan’t ever forget you and I’ll think of you a many and a many a time, and I’ll pray for you, too!”⁠—and she was gone.

Mary Jane Wilks, Chapter 28

Respectability and violence

The Grangerfords are courteous, hospitable, church-going and murderous; the town that wants to lynch Sherburn is the same town that laughed at Boggs; the ladies who set Huck to his spelling own the man on the raft. Twain arranges the episodes so that the violence is always underwritten by good manners.

From the family that takes their guns to church in Chapter 18, through Sherburn’s speech in Chapter 22, to the tarred and feathered frauds in Chapter 33 that make even Huck sick.

She said it was wicked to say what I said; said she wouldn’t say it for the whole world; she was going to live so as to go to the good place. Well, I couldn’t see no advantage in going where she was going, so I made up my mind I wouldn’t try for it.

Chapter 1

a feud is this way: A man has a quarrel with another man, and kills him; then that other man’s brother kills him; then the other brothers, on both sides, goes for one another; then the cousins chip in⁠—and by and by everybody’s killed off, and there ain’t no more feud. But it’s kind of slow, and takes a long time.

Buck Grangerford, Chapter 18

Reading and romance

Tom’s romances give the gang its oath, the caravan of Spaniards that turns out to be a Sunday-school picnic, and finally a three-week escape plan for a man who could walk out of an unlocked door. Set beside them are Emmeline Grangerford’s mortuary verses and the Shakespeare the king butchers for money: reading is presented as a way of not seeing what is in front of you.

Introduced as a joke in Chapter 3, echoed in the Grangerford parlour, and turned into the structural problem of the last section.

Persons attempting to find a motive in this narrative will be prosecuted; persons attempting to find a moral in it will be banished; persons attempting to find a plot in it will be shot.

Notice

The shadings have not been done in a haphazard fashion, or by guesswork; but painstakingly, and with the trustworthy guidance and support of personal familiarity with these several forms of speech.
