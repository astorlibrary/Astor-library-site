Conscience

Huck’s conscience is a machine his society built, and it works perfectly: it tells him that helping Jim is theft and a sin, and it makes him feel filthy for doing it. The novel shows a boy acting against everything he believes and calling the result wickedness; he never decides, in so many words, that slavery is wrong.

Through the book First felt as an itch in Chapter 16, argued out and abandoned at the end of the same chapter, and brought to a decision in Chapter 31 when he tears up the letter and takes the damnation with it.

In the text

Jim said it made him all over trembly and feverish to be so close to freedom. Well, I can tell you it made me all over trembly and feverish, too, to hear him, because I begun to get it through my head that he was most free⁠—and who was to blame for it? Why, me.

Chapter 16

The same four words describe two opposite feelings, and the passage depends on the repetition: Huck can feel exactly what Jim feels, and has been taught to file it as guilt. ‘Who was to blame’ treats another man’s freedom as damage, the vocabulary the whole book is trying to get out of and never entirely does.

You can’t pray a lie⁠—I found that out.

Chapter 31

Five words and a discovery, reported in the past tense as though it were a fact about the world. The sentence is the turning point of the book: Huck has established that he cannot deceive God, which leaves him nothing to hide behind when he sits down twenty lines later with the letter in his hand.

The river and the shore

The raft holds talk, food, work and the only sustained kindness in the book, and it is moving in the wrong direction the whole time. Twain will not allow the water to be a symbol of liberty: it is a road, a workplace and a commercial highway that carries two fugitives deeper into the country they are escaping.

Through the book Established in the night passages of Chapters 12 and 19, undercut when they pass Cairo in the fog, and closed off entirely when the raft is boarded in Chapter 19 by two men who take it over.

In the text

Not a sound anywheres⁠—perfectly still⁠—just like the whole world was asleep, only sometimes the bullfrogs a-cluttering, maybe.

Chapter 19

The sentence is built entirely out of negatives and qualifications, which is how Huck describes anything he loves: he will not commit to the silence without adding the frogs. This is the last unspoiled morning in the book (within four pages two men will climb onto the raft), and Twain gives it the most careful prose Huck ever produces.

But I reckon I got to light out for the Territory ahead of the rest, because Aunt Sally she’s going to adopt me and sivilize me, and I can’t stand it. I been there before.

Chapter the Last

The book ends where it began, with a boy running from a woman who wants to improve him; nothing in four hundred pages has changed his position. ‘I been there before’ is the flattest possible last line, and the Territory he proposes to light out for is Indian land the United States is in the process of taking.

People as property

The novel’s most chilling sentences are arithmetic. Jim is worth eight hundred dollars, and says so about himself; he is sold for forty by a man who wanted a drink; a reward of two hundred is advertised for his return. The system is shown through prices, which is why the book does not need a speech against it.

Through the book Named in Chapter 8 when Jim explains why he ran, priced in Chapter 31, and finally made absurd in Chapter 43 when Tom pays him forty dollars for having been a prisoner.

In the text

“Well, I b’lieve you, Huck. I⁠—I run off.”

Jim, Chapter 8

The hesitation is printed into the line, where the pronoun is said twice with a dash between. He is handing a white child the power to have him hunted, and the novel measures the size of that in two syllables and a piece of punctuation.

It was a close place. I took it up, and held it in my hand. I was a-trembling, because I’d got to decide, forever, betwixt two things, and I knowed it.

Chapter 31

Four short sentences, no subordination, and one adverb doing all the work: ‘forever’. Huck understands the stakes exactly, having just spent a page remembering Jim standing his watch and calling him honey, and the physical detail of the trembling hand is the only description in a passage otherwise made entirely of thought.

Performance and fraud

Almost every character is playing somebody else: Huck is a girl, an orphan, a servant and then Tom Sawyer; the duke and the king are royalty; Tom conducts an escape according to books. The river towns are taken in because they want the show, and the novel is interested in who is left paying when the audience goes home.

Through the book Comic from Chapter 19, turns ugly at the Wilks funeral, and becomes cruelty in the last ten chapters, when the performance is staged around a man who cannot leave.

In the text

“Well, that’s what I’m a-saying; all kings is mostly rapscallions, as fur as I can make out.”

Huckleberry Finn, Chapter 23

Huck defends the frauds by generalising them, and then runs through Henry the Eighth and half the English succession to prove it. It is his one piece of book learning, and it is all wrong. Under the comedy, the duke and the king are a small provincial version of the way power behaves everywhere in this novel.

Well, it made me sick to see it; and I was sorry for them poor pitiful rascals, it seemed like I couldn’t ever feel any hardness against them any more in the world. It was a dreadful thing to see. Human beings can be awful cruel to one another.

Chapter 33

He pities two men who sold his friend for forty dollars, which is the clearest evidence in the book that his sympathy operates independently of desert. The final sentence is the only general statement Huck makes in the whole novel, and it is about the crowd more than the frauds.

Respectability and violence

The Grangerfords are courteous, hospitable, church-going and murderous; the town that wants to lynch Sherburn is the same town that laughed at Boggs; the ladies who set Huck to his spelling own the man on the raft. Twain arranges the episodes so that the violence is always underwritten by good manners.

Through the book From the family that takes their guns to church in Chapter 18, through Sherburn’s speech in Chapter 22, to the tarred and feathered frauds in Chapter 33 that make even Huck sick.

In the text

a feud is this way: A man has a quarrel with another man, and kills him; then that other man’s brother kills him; then the other brothers, on both sides, goes for one another; then the cousins chip in⁠—and by and by everybody’s killed off, and there ain’t no more feud. But it’s kind of slow, and takes a long time.

Buck Grangerford, Chapter 18

The definition is given as a process with a predictable result, like a recipe, and the last sentence complains about how long it takes. Buck cannot say what the quarrel was about, beyond a lawsuit that somebody lost, and he will be dead before the chapter is over. His offhand tone shows how completely the feud has been inherited.

I was born and raised in the South, and I’ve lived in the North; so I know the average all around. The average man’s a coward.

Colonel Sherburn, Chapter 22

The speech works because it is true and because the man delivering it has just committed murder in front of a child. Twain gives the book’s sharpest analysis of crowd cowardice to a killer and then gives nobody the reply, which leaves the reader holding an argument they cannot comfortably accept or refute.

Reading and romance

Tom’s romances give the gang its oath, the caravan of Spaniards that turns out to be a Sunday-school picnic, and finally a three-week escape plan for a man who could walk out of an unlocked door. Set beside them are Emmeline Grangerford’s mortuary verses and the Shakespeare the king butchers for money: reading is presented as a way of not seeing what is in front of you.

Through the book Introduced as a joke in Chapter 3, echoed in the Grangerford parlour, and turned into the structural problem of the last section.

In the text

Persons attempting to find a motive in this narrative will be prosecuted; persons attempting to find a moral in it will be banished; persons attempting to find a plot in it will be shot.

Notice

Three legal punishments escalate from prosecution to execution across one sentence, which is the same escalation the book’s river towns run on. The joke is also a set of instructions: it names the motive, the moral and the plot, the three things the novel will be judged on, and the notice knows it.

The shadings have not been done in a haphazard fashion, or by guesswork; but painstakingly, and with the trustworthy guidance and support of personal familiarity with these several forms of speech.

Explanatory

Having just forbidden the reader to look for anything, Twain makes one claim in earnest, and it is about accuracy of ear. The effect is to put speech under warranty before the first chapter: how a character talks is going to be offered as evidence, so the reader is warned not to treat any voice in the book as neutral.
