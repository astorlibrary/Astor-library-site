Adventures of Huckleberry Finn · Mark Twain

Characters

Huckleberry Finn

Narrator; a thirteen-year-old runaway; first appears Chapter 1

He lies constantly, competently and mostly to protect other people, and he tells the reader the truth about everything he does not understand. His moral education happens entirely in the wrong vocabulary: every decent thing he does, he records as a sin, which is why the book can show a conscience working without ever letting him take credit for it.

Jim

An enslaved man, husband and father, running for his family; first appears Chapter 2

He runs because he has heard he is to be sold to New Orleans and separated from his wife and children, and everything he does afterwards is directed at getting them back. He is the most competent adult in the book (he reads the river, the weather and the people), and he is the one character permitted a scene of unguarded grief, about a daughter he once struck for disobedience she could not hear.

Tom Sawyer

Huck’s friend, and the author of the last ten chapters’ plans; first appears Chapter 2

He has read the romances and intends to live by them, which is charming at the start of the book and unbearable at the end. He knows throughout the escape that Jim has already been freed, and keeps that fact to himself because the adventure requires a prisoner. The novel lets him be shot in the leg and does not otherwise punish him.

Pap Finn

Huck’s father; first appears Chapter 5

A drunk who appears when he hears his son has money, sues for custody, and is granted it by a new judge who believes in the sanctity of the family. His rant about a well-dressed free Black college professor is the clearest statement in the book of what the racial system is actually for, and it is delivered by the least competent man in Missouri.

Miss Watson

The Widow Douglas’s sister, and Jim’s owner; first appears Chapter 1

She teaches Huck to spell and to pray, promises him a heaven consisting of a harp, and is about to sell a man away from his family for eight hundred dollars. The novel never treats these as separate facts. Her deathbed manumission arrives too late to be generosity and too conveniently to be anything else.

The duke and the king

Two confidence men who take over the raft; first appears Chapter 19

One claims to be a displaced duke, the other the lost dauphin of France, and the river towns keep paying. They are funny for ten chapters and then they sell a man for forty dollars, which is the book’s coldest joke: the frauds finally deal in the one commodity the respectable characters deal in too.

Buck Grangerford

A boy of Huck’s age in a feuding family; first appears Chapter 17

He is delighted to have a friend his own age and cannot explain the war he is fighting: a lawsuit, thirty years ago, that somebody lost. He is killed within two chapters of meeting Huck, and the manner of his death is the one thing Huck refuses to write down in detail.

Colonel Sherburn

A storekeeper who shoots a drunk in the street; first appears Chapter 21

He tells Boggs he will put up with him until one o’clock, kills him in front of the man’s daughter, and then stands on his own porch and tells the lynch mob exactly why it will not come up the steps. The speech is contemptuous, accurate and entirely unpunished, and the novel gives nobody a voice to answer it.

Mary Jane Wilks

The eldest of three orphaned sisters; first appears Chapter 24

She inherits, with her sisters, the estate the king and the duke have come for, and she is the person whose straightforward decency finally makes lying unendurable to Huck. She is also the one character who cries over a family of enslaved people being sold apart, which the novel notes and the town does not.
