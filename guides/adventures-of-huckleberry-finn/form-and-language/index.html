Adventures of Huckleberry Finn · Mark Twain

Form and language

Vernacular first person

Telling a whole novel in the unliterary speech of its narrator, including its grammar and its blind spots.

There is no adult voice anywhere in the book to explain what Huck has got wrong, so the reader has to make the judgement from inside a sentence that is not making it.

You don’t know about me without you have read a book by the name of The Adventures of Tom Sawyer; but that ain’t no matter. That book was made by Mr. Mark Twain, and he told the truth, mainly. There was things which he stretched, but mainly he told the truth.

Chapter 1

Written dialect

Rendering different regional and social speech by respelling it on the page.

The Explanatory insists on seven distinct dialects, done by ear and not by guesswork, and the claim is a warning: the way a character talks will be treated as evidence about them.

The shadings have not been done in a haphazard fashion, or by guesswork; but painstakingly, and with the trustworthy guidance and support of personal familiarity with these several forms of speech.

Explanatory

Dramatic irony

A narrator reporting something the reader understands better than he does.

Huck calls his decision to help Jim wickedness, and the whole moral force of the book depends on the reader disagreeing with the only voice available.

Jim said it made him all over trembly and feverish to be so close to freedom. Well, I can tell you it made me all over trembly and feverish, too, to hear him, because I begun to get it through my head that he was most free⁠—and who was to blame for it? Why, me.

Chapter 16

Episodic structure

A journey story built from self-contained encounters, with no single developing plot.

Feud, camp meeting, murder, mob, Shakespeare, funeral fraud: each landing is a separate examination of a community, and the raft between them is where the book breathes.

a feud is this way: A man has a quarrel with another man, and kills him; then that other man’s brother kills him; then the other brothers, on both sides, goes for one another; then the cousins chip in⁠—and by and by everybody’s killed off, and there ain’t no more feud. But it’s kind of slow, and takes a long time.

Buck Grangerford, Chapter 18

Deadpan

Delivering an outrage or an absurdity in the same flat tone as everything else.

Huck reports a shooting, a lynch mob and his own damnation in the same register he uses for catfish and corn-dodgers, and the flatness lets them land.

Persons attempting to find a motive in this narrative will be prosecuted; persons attempting to find a moral in it will be banished; persons attempting to find a plot in it will be shot.

Notice

The opening notice

Prefatory matter that tells the reader how, or how not, to read what follows.

A Notice threatens prosecution, banishment and shooting for anyone who looks for motive, moral or plot; the joke is a dare, and the book has a moral, a motive and very little plot.

Persons attempting to find a motive in this narrative will be prosecuted; persons attempting to find a moral in it will be banished; persons attempting to find a plot in it will be shot.
