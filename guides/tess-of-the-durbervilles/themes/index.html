Tess of the d’Urbervilles · Thomas Hardy

Themes

Purity and the verdict

The subtitle picks a fight before the first page. Hardy’s method is to show the judgement being made (by Angel, by the parson who will not bury Sorrow, by Tess herself) and then to show the evidence on which it rests, which is always somebody else’s conduct.

From the narrator’s flat statement in Phase the Second that she had broken an accepted social law but no law known to her environment, through the refused burial, to the wedding night at the end of Phase the Fourth.

might some say, where was Tess’s guardian angel? where was the providence of her simple faith? Perhaps, like that other god of whom the ironical Tishbite spoke, he was talking, or he was pursuing, or he was in a journey, or he was sleeping and not to be awaked.

Phase the First, Chapter XI

Why it was that upon this beautiful feminine tissue, sensitive as gossamer, and practically blank as snow as yet, there should have been traced such a coarse pattern as it was doomed to receive; why so often the coarse appropriates the finer thus, the wrong man the woman, the wrong woman the man, many thousand years of analytical philosophy have failed to explain to our sense of order.

Phase the First, Chapter XI

Class and ancestry

The d’Urberville name is the novel’s original accident: it is worthless as property, potent as fantasy, and purchasable by anyone with money. Hardy sets a family that owns the name legitimately and has nothing against a family that bought it and has everything.

Announced on the road in Chapter I, exploited at Trantridge, revived at Kingsbere when the family sleeps under the window of its own vault, and finally irrelevant at the gallows.

Then what might your meaning be in calling me ‘Sir John’ these different times, when I be plain Jack Durbeyfield, the haggler?

John Durbeyfield, Phase the First, Chapter I

’Tis because we be on a blighted star, and not a sound one, isn’t it, Tess?

Abraham Durbeyfield, Phase the First, Chapter IV

Work and the land

Nobody in this novel is ever outside the labour market. Tess moves between a poultry farm, a dairy, a swede field and a threshing rick, and each place has its own skills, wages, weather and physical cost, described with the accuracy of someone who had watched it done.

The fertile Var valley of Phase the Third is set against the starve-acre uplands of Phase the Fifth, and the threshing machine in Phase the Sixth makes the pace of work no longer human.

The world was drawn to a larger pattern here. The enclosures numbered fifty acres instead of ten

Phase the Third, Chapter XVI

Close under the eaves of the stack, and as yet barely visible, was the red tyrant that the women had come to serve⁠—a timber-framed construction, with straps and wheels appertaining⁠—the threshing-machine which, whilst it was going, kept up a despotic demand upon the endurance of their muscles and nerves.

Phase the Sixth, Chapter XLVII

Chance, timing and cause

A letter slides under a carpet, a horse meets a mail-cart, a husband comes home too late. Hardy shows that a woman with no money and no standing cannot survive the ordinary accidents that other people absorb.

Each phase turns on a small event that happens at the wrong moment, and the narrator never calls any of them fate, though the characters do.

’Tis because we be on a blighted star, and not a sound one, isn’t it, Tess?

Phase the First, Chapter XI

Religion and its officers

The novel is full of clergy and empty of comfort. A vicar will not give a Christian burial and then privately says it makes no difference; a rake preaches in a barn and soon gives it up; Angel keeps his father’s morality after throwing out his father’s creed.

The home baptism in Phase the Second is the book’s one true sacrament, and it is performed by an unmarried mother in a cottage bedroom.

might some say, where was Tess’s guardian angel? where was the providence of her simple faith? Perhaps, like that other god of whom the ironical Tishbite spoke, he was talking, or he was pursuing, or he was in a journey, or he was sleeping and not to be awaked.

Phase the First, Chapter XI

But all the while she was making a distinction where there was no difference. Feeling herself in antagonism, she was quite in accord. She had been made to break an accepted social law, but no law known to the environment in which she fancied herself such an anomaly.

Phase the Second, Chapter XIII

The double standard

Hardy constructs the wedding night as a controlled experiment: two confessions, the same offence, told within an hour of each other, with the sexes reversed. Angel is forgiven in a sentence; the novel then watches him fail to return the favour for the next hundred pages.

Set up by the paired confessions at the end of Phase the Fourth, named by the title of the phase that follows, and never retracted: Angel’s eventual forgiveness arrives after it can do any good.

What a fresh and virginal daughter of Nature that milkmaid is!

Angel Clare, Phase the Third, Chapter XVIII

I repeat, the woman I have been loving is not you.

Angel Clare, Phase the Fifth, Chapter XXXV
