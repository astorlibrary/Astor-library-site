Purity and the verdict

The subtitle picks a fight before the first page. Hardy’s method is to show the judgement being made (by Angel, by the parson who will not bury Sorrow, by Tess herself) and then to show the evidence on which it rests, which is always somebody else’s conduct.

Through the book From the narrator’s flat statement in Phase the Second that she had broken an accepted social law but no law known to her environment, through the refused burial, to the wedding night at the end of Phase the Fourth.

In the text

How could I be expected to know? I was a child when I left this house four months ago. Why didn’t you tell me there was danger in menfolk? Why didn’t you warn me? Ladies know what to fend hands against, because they read novels that tell them of these tricks; but I never had the chance o’ learning in that way, and you did not help me!

Tess Durbeyfield, Phase the Second, Chapter XII

Tess names the cause of her ruin as a lack of information: ladies read novels and know, and nobody told her.

But all the while she was making a distinction where there was no difference. Feeling herself in antagonism, she was quite in accord. She had been made to break an accepted social law, but no law known to the environment in which she fancied herself such an anomaly.

Phase the Second, Chapter XIII

Only the accepted social law has been broken, not the law of the world around her, and Tess feels the guilt anyway.

Angel, I am almost glad⁠—yes, glad! This happiness could not have lasted. It was too much. I have had enough; and now I shall not live for you to despise me!

Tess Durbeyfield, Phase the Seventh, Chapter LVIII

Her last argument is about time: the happiness was too much to last. The wedding night still governs her life.

Class and ancestry

The d’Urberville name is the novel’s original accident: it is worthless as property, potent as fantasy, and purchasable by anyone with money. Hardy sets a family that owns the name legitimately and has nothing against a family that bought it and has everything.

Through the book Announced on the road in Chapter I, exploited at Trantridge, revived at Kingsbere when the family sleeps under the window of its own vault, and finally irrelevant at the gallows.

In the text

Then what might your meaning be in calling me ‘Sir John’ these different times, when I be plain Jack Durbeyfield, the haggler?

John Durbeyfield, Phase the First, Chapter I

A man asks why a parson calls him “Sir John”, and the whole plot follows from the answer.

An immeasurable social chasm was to divide our heroine’s personality thereafter from that previous self of hers who stepped from her mother’s door to try her fortune at Trantridge poultry-farm.

Phase the First, Chapter XI

The damage is measured socially, as the distance between two versions of her, from her mother’s door to a poultry farm.

Remember, my lady, I was your master once! I will be your master again. If you are any man’s wife you are mine!

Alec d’Urberville, Phase the Sixth, Chapter XLVII

“My lady” sneers at the ancestry that sent her to Alec in the first place, and the threat is also a forecast.

Work and the land

Nobody in this novel is ever outside the labour market. Tess moves between a poultry farm, a dairy, a swede field and a threshing rick, and each place has its own skills, wages, weather and physical cost, described with the accuracy of someone who had watched it done.

Through the book The fertile Var valley of Phase the Third is set against the starve-acre uplands of Phase the Fifth, and the threshing machine in Phase the Sixth makes the pace of work no longer human.

In the text

The world was drawn to a larger pattern here. The enclosures numbered fifty acres instead of ten

Phase the Third, Chapter XVI

The move to the dairy is registered as a change of measurement: the fields, the herds and the horizon all get bigger.

Close under the eaves of the stack, and as yet barely visible, was the red tyrant that the women had come to serve⁠—a timber-framed construction, with straps and wheels appertaining⁠—the threshing-machine which, whilst it was going, kept up a despotic demand upon the endurance of their muscles and nerves.

Phase the Sixth, Chapter XLVII

The threshing machine gets a colour and a title before a description: a wage relationship made political, with the women as its last link.

Chance, timing and cause

A letter slides under a carpet, a horse meets a mail-cart, a husband comes home too late. Hardy shows that a woman with no money and no standing cannot survive the ordinary accidents that other people absorb.

Through the book Each phase turns on a small event that happens at the wrong moment, and the narrator never calls any of them fate, though the characters do.

In the text

’Tis because we be on a blighted star, and not a sound one, isn’t it, Tess?

Abraham Durbeyfield, Phase the First, Chapter IV

A child takes an astronomy lesson as an account of his family’s luck, and Hardy lets it stand as the best explanation on offer.

under the edge of the carpet she discerned the faint white margin of the envelope containing her letter to him, which he obviously had never seen, owing to her having in her haste thrust it beneath the carpet as well as beneath the door.

Phase the Fourth, Chapter XXXIII

The disaster comes down to half an inch of carpet and Tess’s haste, with no malice or fate involved.

I must cry to you in my trouble⁠—I have no one else! I am so exposed to temptation, Angel. I fear to say who it is, and I do not like to write about it at all. But I cling to you in a way you cannot think! Can you not come to me now, at once, before anything terrible happens?

Tess Durbeyfield, Phase the Sixth, Chapter XLVIII

Tess will not name the danger even in a private letter, the reticence that has cost her twice already.

Religion and its officers

The novel is full of clergy and empty of comfort. A vicar will not give a Christian burial and then privately says it makes no difference; a rake preaches in a barn and soon gives it up; Angel keeps his father’s morality after throwing out his father’s creed.

Through the book The home baptism in Phase the Second is the book’s one true sacrament, and it is performed by an unmarried mother in a cottage bedroom.

In the text

might some say, where was Tess’s guardian angel? where was the providence of her simple faith? Perhaps, like that other god of whom the ironical Tishbite spoke, he was talking, or he was pursuing, or he was in a journey, or he was sleeping and not to be awaked.

Phase the First, Chapter XI

The narrator asks where providence was through an imagined objector, and answers with Elijah mocking the priests of Baal.

Sorrow, I baptize thee in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost.

Tess Durbeyfield, Phase the Second, Chapter XIV

Tess uses the exact words of the rite, so the vicar cannot call it invalid, only refuse the child a Christian burial.

the President of the Immortals, in Aeschylean phrase, had ended his sport with Tess.

Phase the Seventh, Chapter LIX

Hardy credits a god he does not believe in with a sentence passed by an English court.

The double standard

Hardy constructs the wedding night as a controlled experiment: two confessions, the same offence, told within an hour of each other, with the sexes reversed. Angel is forgiven in a sentence; the novel then watches him fail to return the favour for the next hundred pages.

Through the book Set up by the paired confessions at the end of Phase the Fourth, named by the title of the phase that follows, and never retracted: Angel’s eventual forgiveness arrives after it can do any good.

In the text

What a fresh and virginal daughter of Nature that milkmaid is!

Angel Clare, Phase the Third, Chapter XVIII

Angel describes a type, not a woman, and one of his words, “virginal”, will be held against her within the year.

I repeat, the woman I have been loving is not you.

Angel Clare, Phase the Fifth, Chapter XXXV

Forgiven his own confession an hour earlier, Angel does not accuse Tess; he unmakes her. “I repeat” is the cruellest word.

How can we live together while that man lives?⁠—he being your husband in nature, and not I.

Angel Clare, Phase the Fifth, Chapter XXXVI

Angel turns an assault into a marriage, “your husband in nature”, and reasons from it.
