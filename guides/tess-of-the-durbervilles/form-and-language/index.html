Tess of the d’Urbervilles · Thomas Hardy

Form and language

The named phases

Dividing a novel into large titled units that carry an argument of their own.

Hardy calls his seven divisions phases and names them so that the contents page reads as a sentence: The Maiden, Maiden No More, The Rally, The Consequence, The Woman Pays, The Convert, Fulfilment.

Landscape

Using terrain, weather and scale to act on the characters.

The Var valley makes bodies fertile and Flintcomb-Ash makes them cold; the same woman doing the same work is a different creature in each, and Hardy changes his vocabulary accordingly.

The world was drawn to a larger pattern here. The enclosures numbered fifty acres instead of ten

Phase the Third, Chapter XVI

Narratorial intervention

A narrator stepping out of the story to contest the judgement the story is receiving.

The narrator asks where Tess’s guardian angel was, declares that she had broken no law known to her surroundings, and closes with a sentence about the President of the Immortals that is deliberately unfair to the universe.

might some say, where was Tess’s guardian angel? where was the providence of her simple faith? Perhaps, like that other god of whom the ironical Tishbite spoke, he was talking, or he was pursuing, or he was in a journey, or he was sleeping and not to be awaked.

Phase the First, Chapter XI

Machinery

Industrial equipment introduced into a rural setting as an agent in the plot.

The threshing machine at Flintcomb-Ash sets the tempo of a whole chapter: the engineer is described as a stranger from another world, and Tess cannot leave the platform while it runs.

Close under the eaves of the stack, and as yet barely visible, was the red tyrant that the women had come to serve⁠—a timber-framed construction, with straps and wheels appertaining⁠—the threshing-machine which, whilst it was going, kept up a despotic demand upon the endurance of their muscles and nerves.

Phase the Sixth, Chapter XLVII

Dialect and register

Marking class and education through the grammar characters are given.

Tess speaks two languages (the dialect at home and ordinary English at school and abroad), and Hardy says so explicitly, which makes the gap between her and her mother measurable in sentences.

Then what might your meaning be in calling me ‘Sir John’ these different times, when I be plain Jack Durbeyfield, the haggler?

John Durbeyfield, Phase the First, Chapter I

Omens

Planting details whose significance is only available on a second reading.

The cock that crows in the afternoon, the d’Urberville coach, the blood on the ceiling at Sandbourne: the superstitions belong to the characters, and the patterning belongs to Hardy.

’Tis because we be on a blighted star, and not a sound one, isn’t it, Tess?

Abraham Durbeyfield, Phase the First, Chapter IV
