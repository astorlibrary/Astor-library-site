White Fang · Jack London

Themes

Heredity and environment

London states his thesis outright and repeats it at every change of owner: heredity is a life-stuff like clay, and environment is what models it. The cub who would have become a true wolf becomes a wolfish dog, then a fiend in a cage, then a guardian of children, without his nature changing at all. The argument is applied even-handedly to human beings, which is where it becomes uncomfortable.

Announced in Part 3, Chapter 6; extended to Beauty Smith, who is pronounced not responsible, in Part 4; turned into the thumb of circumstance, which is Weedon Scott, in Part 4, Chapter 6; and closed with Jim Hall, the soft clay of a San Francisco slum, in the last chapter.

So to him the entrance of the cave was a wall—a wall of light.

Part 2, Chapter 3

Fear!—that legacy of the Wild which no animal may escape nor exchange for pottage.

Part 2, Chapter 4

Men as gods

White Fang’s theology is strictly empirical. Men are gods because sticks and stones fly at their bidding, because they make fire, and later because they make steamboats and cable cars; the more power, the higher the god. The scheme lets London describe domination from underneath, as something felt to be in the order of things, and it means that every beating in the book is also an act of worship.

First felt as the man-animals’ mastery over dead things in Part 3; ranked, with white men above Indians, at Fort Yukon in Part 4; pushed to the point of terror on the pavement of San Francisco; and domesticated at Sierra Vista, where the gods’ power shows chiefly in what they forbid.

His fangs be white, and White Fang shall be his name. I have spoken. He is my dog.

Grey Beaver, Part 3, Chapter 1

They enforced their live strength with the power of dead things. Dead things did their bidding.

Part 3, Chapter 1

Survival

The Wild of this novel is a food chain with no outside. Part 1 shows it from the point of view of two men being hunted, one of whom is eaten, Part 2 from that of a cub learning to eat, and the narrator is explicit that the cub lives the law without ever thinking it. What follows in the camps and the fighting pit is the same law with men added, which is London’s reason for starting where he does.

The pack runs down the sled team in Part 1; the cub kills ptarmigan chicks and is nearly killed by a weasel in Part 2; the code becomes obey the strong and oppress the weak in the camp; and in California he must unlearn it hen by hen.

Dark spruce forest frowned on either side the frozen waterway.

Part 1, Chapter 1

It is not the way of the Wild to like movement. Life is an offence to it, for life is movement; and the Wild aims always to destroy movement.

Part 1, Chapter 1

The covenant with man

The central bargain of the book is put in contractual terms: food, fire, protection and companionship on one side; work, obedience and the guarding of property on the other, with liberty as the price. London calls it the ancient covenant of the first wolf that came in from the Wild, and he is honest that it is a relief as well as a loss, since it is easier to lean upon another than to stand alone.

Kiche crawls to her name; White Fang comes in to Grey Beaver’s fire of his own choice in Part 3, Chapter 4; the terms are set out in Chapter 5; whisky breaks the contract from the god’s side in Part 4; and under Scott the same service is rendered for love where it had been rendered for awe.

His fangs be white, and White Fang shall be his name. I have spoken. He is my dog.

Grey Beaver, Part 3, Chapter 1

This in itself was compensation, for it is always easier to lean upon another than to stand alone.

Part 3, Chapter 2

Liking and love

For three parts the best feeling available to White Fang is liking, and the book says flatly that his service to Grey Beaver had no love in it because he did not know what love was. Scott’s hand, which comes down and does not hurt, reaches a capacity that had nearly died. The love that results is exclusive, jealous of its dignity, and expressed by a growl with a crooning note in it.

Absent in the Indian camp; awakened against every instinct in Part 4, Chapter 6; proved by the broken window and the pining when Scott is away; and tested in the South, where he must extend a grudging tolerance to everyone the master loves.

“If you don’t mind my expressin’ my feelin’s, Mr. Scott, I’ll make free to say you’re seventeen kinds of a damn fool an’ all of ’em different, an’ then some.”

Matt, Part 4, Chapter 6

Yet again, in this new orientation, it was the thumb of circumstance that pressed and prodded him, softening that which had become hard and remoulding it into fairer form. Weedon Scott was in truth this thumb.

Part 4, Chapter 6

Solitude

White Fang belongs to no pack after the cave. The puppies reject him as a wolf, the sled team hates him as a leader, the fighting pit makes every dog an opponent, and he learns to want only to be let alone. London treats this solitude as manufactured, and the measure of his recovery at the end is that he can lie still while another animal’s young climb over him.

Lip-lip’s persecution in Part 3; the leader’s place at the head of a team that wants to pull him down in Part 4; the cage; then Dick’s rebuffed overtures and Collie’s long hostility in Part 5, ending with the puppies in the stable doorway.

He turned and fled away, not from the hurt of the fire, but from the laughter that sank even deeper, and hurt in the spirit of him.

Part 3, Chapter 1

There was no place for her in his scheme of things, as there was no place for him in hers.

Part 3, Chapter 6

Responsibility

If character is pressed into shape from outside, blame becomes hard to place, and the novel knows it. The narrator excuses Beauty Smith, indicts society for Jim Hall, and shows a kindly judge passing a fifty-year sentence on perjured evidence. Yet Beauty Smith is still punched and Jim Hall is still killed, and the household applauds. The book’s theory and its sense of what is deserved never meet.

Grey Beaver’s rough fairness in the camp is called justice and felt as such; Scott tells Beauty Smith he has forfeited his rights; and the last chapter sets a railroaded man against the dog, with the reader’s sympathy deliberately divided.

In short, Beauty Smith was a monstrosity, and the blame of it lay elsewhere. He was not responsible. The clay of him had been so moulded in the making.

Part 4, Chapter 2

“A man’s got his rights. But you’re not a man. You’re a beast.”

Weedon Scott, Part 4, Chapter 4
