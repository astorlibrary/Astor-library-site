Heredity and environment

London states his thesis outright and repeats it at every change of owner: heredity is a life-stuff like clay, and environment is what models it. The cub who would have become a true wolf becomes a wolfish dog, then a fiend in a cage, then a guardian of children, without his nature changing at all. The argument is applied even-handedly to human beings, which is where it becomes uncomfortable.

Through the book Announced in Part 3, Chapter 6; extended to Beauty Smith, who is pronounced not responsible, in Part 4; turned into the thumb of circumstance, which is Weedon Scott, in Part 4, Chapter 6; and closed with Jim Hall, the soft clay of a San Francisco slum, in the last chapter.

In the text

His heredity was a life-stuff that may be likened to clay. It possessed many possibilities, was capable of being moulded into many different forms.

Part 3, Chapter 6

The novel’s thesis is stated as a simile, in the hedged voice of a lecture (‘may be likened to’). What matters is what it rules out. There is no wolf nature that must come out in the end and no dog nature either; there are possibilities, and then there is pressure. Everything that follows (the fighting pit, the taming, the convict) is offered as an experiment on this proposition.

Yet again, in this new orientation, it was the thumb of circumstance that pressed and prodded him, softening that which had become hard and remoulding it into fairer form. Weedon Scott was in truth this thumb.

Part 4, Chapter 6

London will not allow even the redemption to be an exception to his rule. Kindness is not a moral force entering a mechanical world; it is one more thumb, and Scott is an environment like Lip-lip or the cage. The short second sentence, a man identified as a digit, is almost comic in its literalness, and it is the most rigorous thing in the book.

Men as gods

White Fang’s theology is strictly empirical. Men are gods because sticks and stones fly at their bidding, because they make fire, and later because they make steamboats and cable cars; the more power, the higher the god. The scheme lets London describe domination from underneath, as something felt to be in the order of things, and it means that every beating in the book is also an act of worship.

Through the book First felt as the man-animals’ mastery over dead things in Part 3; ranked, with white men above Indians, at Fort Yukon in Part 4; pushed to the point of terror on the pavement of San Francisco; and domesticated at Sierra Vista, where the gods’ power shows chiefly in what they forbid.

In the text

His fangs be white, and White Fang shall be his name. I have spoken. He is my dog.

Grey Beaver, Part 3, Chapter 1

Naming and owning happen in the same breath, and the cub who was the grey cub for five chapters gets a name only at the moment he becomes property. The ceremonial English London gives Grey Beaver makes the claim sound like a legal finding, which in the camp it is: the title to the dog descends through the mother and the dead brother, and nobody disputes it.

“A man’s got his rights. But you’re not a man. You’re a beast.”

Weedon Scott, Part 4, Chapter 4

The scene is written to be cheered, and it should also be noticed. Scott agrees that a man has rights and then removes Beauty Smith from the category, which is the move the novel’s theory forbids: two chapters earlier the narrator ruled that this man was not responsible. ‘Beast’ is the book’s most unstable word, used with contempt here, of a convict later, and of its hero never.

Survival

The Wild of this novel is a food chain with no outside. Part 1 shows it from the point of view of two men being hunted, one of whom is eaten, Part 2 from that of a cub learning to eat, and the narrator is explicit that the cub lives the law without ever thinking it. What follows in the camps and the fighting pit is the same law with men added, which is London’s reason for starting where he does.

Through the book The pack runs down the sled team in Part 1; the cub kills ptarmigan chicks and is nearly killed by a weasel in Part 2; the code becomes obey the strong and oppress the weak in the camp; and in California he must unlearn it hen by hen.

In the text

Dark spruce forest frowned on either side the frozen waterway.

Part 1, Chapter 1

The forest is given a face before any living thing appears, and the missing preposition (‘either side the frozen waterway’) gives the line a faintly archaic stiffness that suits a landscape about to be credited with wisdom and malice. The waterway is frozen: the first thing the reader learns about this country is that it has stopped something moving.

The aim of life was meat. Life itself was meat. Life lived on life. There were the eaters and the eaten. The law was: EAT OR BE EATEN.

Part 2, Chapter 5

Five sentences, each shorter than a line, with meat and life exchanging places until they are the same word. The capitals are the only ones of their kind in the novel and give the law the look of a notice nailed to a wall. The law of club and fang in the earlier book was taught by men; this one needs nobody to teach it.

The covenant with man

The central bargain of the book is put in contractual terms: food, fire, protection and companionship on one side; work, obedience and the guarding of property on the other, with liberty as the price. London calls it the ancient covenant of the first wolf that came in from the Wild, and he is honest that it is a relief as well as a loss, since it is easier to lean upon another than to stand alone.

Through the book Kiche crawls to her name; White Fang comes in to Grey Beaver’s fire of his own choice in Part 3, Chapter 4; the terms are set out in Chapter 5; whisky breaks the contract from the god’s side in Part 4; and under Scott the same service is rendered for love where it had been rendered for awe.

In the text

Of his own choice, he came in to sit by man’s fire and to be ruled by him.

Part 3, Chapter 4

The sentence that most exactly reverses Buck’s story. The door to the Wild was open, and the phrase of his own choice is placed first so it cannot be missed. Fire and rule arrive together in one clause, comfort and government as a single offer. What he gets, instead of the blow, is half a lump of tallow, which is the book’s first act of kindness and costs Grey Beaver almost nothing.

And the chief thing demanded by these intricacies of civilisation was control, restraint—a poise of self that was as delicate as the fluttering of gossamer wings and at the same time as rigid as steel.

Part 5, Chapter 3

Civilisation is defined by what it forbids, and the definition is not flattering: the tame life asks for more self-suppression than the sled ever did. Gossamer and steel are an odd pair for a wolf, and the strain in the simile is the strain it describes. This is the sentence to set against any reading of Part 5 as a simple reward.

Liking and love

For three parts the best feeling available to White Fang is liking, and the book says flatly that his service to Grey Beaver had no love in it because he did not know what love was. Scott’s hand, which comes down and does not hurt, reaches a capacity that had nearly died. The love that results is exclusive, jealous of its dignity, and expressed by a growl with a crooning note in it.

Through the book Absent in the Indian camp; awakened against every instinct in Part 4, Chapter 6; proved by the broken window and the pining when Scott is away; and tested in the South, where he must extend a grudging tolerance to everyone the master loves.

In the text

“If you don’t mind my expressin’ my feelin’s, Mr. Scott, I’ll make free to say you’re seventeen kinds of a damn fool an’ all of ’em different, an’ then some.”

Matt, Part 4, Chapter 6

The taming is the emotional centre of the book, and London protects it from solemnity by having it watched by a man with a dishpan. Matt’s elaborate courtesy before the insult marks the distance between hired man and mining expert, and his seventeen comes back at the chapter’s end, when Beauty Smith is said to have thought he had hold of seventeen devils.

This passed away as the puppies’ antics and mauling continued, and he lay with half-shut patient eyes, drowsing in the sun.

Part 5, Chapter 5

The book that began with frost, dusk and a land hostile to movement ends with a wolf lying still in sunlight by his own consent. Mauling is the right word, recalling every mauling he has taken, now harmless. Whether this is fulfilment or extinction is left to the reader: Buck ends singing at the head of a pack, and White Fang ends half asleep with an audience.

Solitude

White Fang belongs to no pack after the cave. The puppies reject him as a wolf, the sled team hates him as a leader, the fighting pit makes every dog an opponent, and he learns to want only to be let alone. London treats this solitude as manufactured, and the measure of his recovery at the end is that he can lie still while another animal’s young climb over him.

Through the book Lip-lip’s persecution in Part 3; the leader’s place at the head of a team that wants to pull him down in Part 4; the cage; then Dick’s rebuffed overtures and Collie’s long hostility in Part 5, ending with the puppies in the stable doorway.

In the text

He turned and fled away, not from the hurt of the fire, but from the laughter that sank even deeper, and hurt in the spirit of him.

Part 3, Chapter 1

London gives his animal shame on the first day among men, and admits in the next breath that it is not given us to know how animals know laughter. Ridicule remains White Fang’s sorest point: the crowds at the cage laugh, Beauty Smith laughs. The measure of Scott’s achievement in Part 5 is a single detail, that the dog learns to be laughed at by him and to laugh back.

They were his environment, these men, and they were moulding the clay of him into a more ferocious thing than had been intended by Nature.

Part 4, Chapter 3

Environment, up to now a matter of snow, famine and other dogs, is redefined as spectators. The crowd at the bars is making a fierce animal, more than watching one, and a paragraph later London adds that the ferocity fed upon itself because everyone treated him as ferocious. Intended by Nature is a rare lapse into purpose in a book that otherwise allows Nature no intentions at all.

Responsibility

If character is pressed into shape from outside, blame becomes hard to place, and the novel knows it. The narrator excuses Beauty Smith, indicts society for Jim Hall, and shows a kindly judge passing a fifty-year sentence on perjured evidence. Yet Beauty Smith is still punched and Jim Hall is still killed, and the household applauds. The book’s theory and its sense of what is deserved never meet.

Through the book Grey Beaver’s rough fairness in the camp is called justice and felt as such; Scott tells Beauty Smith he has forfeited his rights; and the last chapter sets a railroaded man against the dog, with the reader’s sympathy deliberately divided.

In the text

In short, Beauty Smith was a monstrosity, and the blame of it lay elsewhere. He was not responsible. The clay of him had been so moulded in the making.

Part 4, Chapter 2

The clay figure is transferred from dog to man without any change of wording, and that is the test of whether London means it. He does, in theory: three flat sentences acquit the villain before he has done anything. But the preceding page has read cowardice off a jaw and cruelty off a forehead with obvious relish, so the chapter both excuses Beauty Smith and expects the reader to be revolted by him.

It was the treatment he had received from the time he was a little pulpy boy in a San Francisco slum—soft clay in the hands of society and ready to be formed into something.

Part 5, Chapter 5

The refrain’s last appearance, and its most political. Every term used of the cub is reused: soft, clay, hands, formed. Pulpy is the unexpected word, making the child unpleasant and also defenceless. London then has his rescued animal tear out the throat of his unrescued man, and lets the household call it a blessing, which is either a failure of nerve or the bleakest irony in the book.
