White Fang · Jack London

Characters

White Fang

A wolf-dog, three parts wolf: sled dog, pit fighter, house dog; first appears Part 2, Chapter 3

He is the only survivor of his litter and the quickest learner in every company he enters, and what he learns depends entirely on who has hold of him. The book is careful to make him neither noble nor vicious by nature: he is morose and murderous under Lip-lip and Beauty Smith and devoted under Scott, and London’s point is that it is the same animal each time.

Kiche, the she-wolf

White Fang’s mother; half wolf, half Indian camp dog; first appears Part 1, Chapter 2

In Part 1 she is the red-hued decoy who lures sled dogs out of camp to be eaten, and in Part 2 a fierce and competent mother; in Part 3 she drops to her belly at the sound of her own name. She is the book’s first proof that a creature can belong to the Wild and to men by turns, and her forgetting of her son is given as a plain fact of wolf-motherhood, without grief.

One Eye

An old grey wolf, White Fang’s father; first appears Part 2, Chapter 1

A scarred veteran who runs on the she-wolf’s blind side and wins her by patience and by treachery, killing a rival while the rival is licking a wound. He is a dutiful provider who is rather afraid of his mate, and he dies offstage, killed by the lynx, leaving only the remains his mate finds at the end of his trail.

Grey Beaver

A Mackenzie Indian, White Fang’s first master; first appears Part 3, Chapter 1

He is just by his own lights, never caresses and never beats without cause, and defends his dog against the whole camp when it has killed another man’s. What he offers is a bargain (food, fire and protection for work and obedience), and the novel respects the bargain while insisting it contains no love. Whisky ruins him at Fort Yukon, and he sells for bottles what he had refused to sell for dollars.

Lip-lip

A camp puppy, older and larger than White Fang; first appears Part 3, Chapter 1

A bully who wins his first fight with the newcomer and makes a career of it, leading the puppy pack against White Fang until the cub has no companion in the camp but his tied-up mother. The narrator holds him responsible for a good deal: without him, we are told, White Fang might have grown up more doglike and more liking of dogs.

Beauty Smith

Cook and drudge at Fort Yukon; owner of the Fighting Wolf; first appears Part 4, Chapter 2

Misshapen, cowardly and cruel, he takes out on a caged animal what stronger men have done to him. London describes him at cruel length and then pronounces him not responsible, since the clay of him was moulded that way in the making. The verdict is consistent with the book’s theory, though the chapter’s evident loathing of him is not.

Weedon Scott

A mining expert from California; the love-master; first appears Part 4, Chapter 4

He arrives in the book as a fist in Beauty Smith’s face and then turns out to be its gentlest figure, undertaking the taming as a debt: a way, he thinks, of redeeming mankind from the wrong it had done the dog. He is also a judge’s son with money and connections, and the ease with which he overrides another man’s property rights is something the novel enjoys and never examines.

Matt

Scott’s dog-musher; first appears Part 4, Chapter 4

The practical man of Part 4, who spots the harness marks on White Fang’s chest, argues for giving him a chance, and feeds him for months without ever being accepted as his master. His slangy commentary keeps the taming chapters from turning solemn, and his puzzlement that the dog should love the man who never feeds him is the book’s own question put in plain speech.

Collie

A sheepdog at Sierra Vista; first appears Part 5, Chapter 2

She alone rejects the household’s verdict on the newcomer: generations of guarding flocks tell her what he is, and she nags, nips and shoulders him for months. She is right, as the chicken-yard shows, and the novel lets her be right and lose. By the last chapter she has a litter by the ancient marauder and is still snarling at him.

Judge Scott

Weedon Scott’s father, master of Sierra Vista; first appears Part 5, Chapter 2

A genial retired judge who bets that a chicken-killer cannot be cured and cheerfully pays up when he is. He is also the man who, in good faith and on perjured evidence, sent Jim Hall to prison for fifty years, so the comfortable household that receives White Fang rests on exactly the kind of mistaken sentence the novel has been arguing against.

Jim Hall

A convict escaped from San Quentin; first appears Part 5, Chapter 5

He enters five chapters from the end and is given, in two pages, the same life as the hero: soft clay handled harshly, a cage, a keeper who was as great a beast as he, and teeth used on a throat. The difference is that nobody comes for him. He is innocent of the crime he was sentenced for, and the dog who was saved kills the man who was not.

Bill and Henry

Two dog-drivers carrying a coffin to Fort McGurry; first appears Part 1, Chapter 1

They own Part 1 and then vanish from the book. Bill frets, counts dogs and cartridges and finally goes after the pack with a rifle; Henry eats his beans, reproves him for being off colour, and survives. Their laconic talk across the fire is London’s way of measuring how near death is without having to say so.
