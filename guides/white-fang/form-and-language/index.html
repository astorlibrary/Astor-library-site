White Fang · Jack London

Form and language

The animal point of view

Narration tied to the perceptions of a creature that cannot use language, so that thought has to be shown as sensation, memory and behaviour.

The cave mouth is a wall of light, men are man-animals and then gods, a cable car is a screaming lynx. The narrator marks the limit again and again (had the cub thought in man-fashion, which he did not), so that the theory belongs to London and only the experience to the wolf.

So to him the entrance of the cave was a wall—a wall of light.

Part 2, Chapter 3

Refrain

A phrase repeated at intervals so that its meaning changes with each context.

The clay comes back at least a dozen times: the clay of his nature, the clay of Beauty Smith, the plasticity of his clay, soft clay in the hands of society. Each return widens the claim, until a figure introduced to explain a puppy is being used to indict a prison system.

The aim of life was meat. Life itself was meat. Life lived on life. There were the eaters and the eaten. The law was: EAT OR BE EATEN.

Part 2, Chapter 5

Elevated diction

Deliberately heightened, Latinate vocabulary applied to creatures and events that would ordinarily be described plainly.

A dog’s submission is written as theology (gods, altars, covenant, allegiance, fealty), and a nest of ptarmigan chicks leads to a paragraph on a chaos of gluttony and slaughter, merciless, planless, endless. The register insists that nothing here is merely an animal story.

It is not the way of the Wild to like movement. Life is an offence to it, for life is movement; and the Wild aims always to destroy movement.

Part 1, Chapter 1

The Wild

Using terrain, weather and scale to act on the characters.

The opening page gives the Northland a will: it laughs, it hates movement, it freezes water to stop it running. California, by contrast, is all fences, porches and chicken-yards, and its pressure on White Fang is a matter of rules where the North’s was a matter of cold.

Dark spruce forest frowned on either side the frozen waterway.

Part 1, Chapter 1

Dialect

Regional speech written phonetically.

Bill, Henry and Matt speak a clipped frontier slang (“I’m jes’ plump tuckered out”), while Grey Beaver is given a formal, almost biblical English and then, to the white man, a trader’s pidgin. The narrator alone has the vocabulary of science, which places every speaker beneath the explanation of his own behaviour.

His fangs be white, and White Fang shall be his name. I have spoken. He is my dog.

Grey Beaver, Part 3, Chapter 1

Foils and doubles

A character constructed to throw another’s choices into relief by making different ones.

Lip-lip is the dog White Fang might have been in a friendlier camp; Cherokee is patience against speed; Collie is instinct that cannot be talked round; and Jim Hall is White Fang without a Weedon Scott, which is why the two are brought together on a staircase in the dark.

“A man’s got his rights. But you’re not a man. You’re a beast.”

Weedon Scott, Part 4, Chapter 4

Delayed entrance

Beginning a narrative with characters and events that the main story will leave behind, so that the protagonist enters a world the reader already fears.

Part 1 is a self-contained tale of two men eaten out of their dog team, and neither man is heard of again. Its purpose is positional: the reader meets White Fang’s mother as a killer of dogs before meeting her as a mother, and knows what the Wild does to meat before a cub is asked to learn it.

They reminded him of children gathered about a spread table and awaiting permission to begin to eat. And he was the food they were to eat!

Part 1, Chapter 3
