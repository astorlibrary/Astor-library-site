The Willows · Algernon Blackwood

Form and language

Landscape as character

Extended, exact description of a place, doing the work a character would usually do.

The first section is almost entirely river and willows (current, sandbanks, the word on the map), and the story’s threat is never separated from that description. Blackwood was a camper before he was a writer, and the detail is the detail of someone who has slept there.

across it may be seen in large straggling letters the word Sümpfe, meaning marshes

Section I

The doubting narrator

A first-person account the reader learns to read against.

He tells the truth and keeps explaining it away. The wind takes the paddle, the wind digs the funnels, and each time the reader is pushed a little further ahead of him. The technique lets the ending land without a monster.

absolutely determined to find an explanation for everything

Section IV

Recurring sound

A recurring sensory detail that marks the progress of the story.

The gong-like humming arrives at intervals, then continuously, then from inside the listener, and finally out of the surface of a corpse. It is the story’s clock.

I can liken it to nothing so much as to the sound of an immense gong, suspended far up in the sky

Section IV

Equipment

Ordinary objects whose condition carries the narrative.

Two paddles, then one; a hull with a hole; a shrinking patch of sand. The supernatural is only ever visible in what it does to kit a canoeist would check anyway.

For one thing—the steering paddle’s gone

Section IV

The unseen

Leaving the central event out of the telling.

Nothing is ever described directly: the shapes may be mist, the sound has no source, and the beings get no adjective beyond the Swede’s theory. The story’s reputation rests on that refusal.

I can liken it to nothing so much as to the sound of an immense gong, suspended far up in the sky

Section IV

Open-air horror

The gothic apparatus of isolation and dread, moved outdoors.

No castle, no ghost, no family curse: a tent on a sandbank. Blackwood keeps the pressure of gothic fiction and throws away its furniture, which is why the story reads as modern.

Retrospective narration

A narrator recounting events he has had years to think about.

The telling is calm, and that is the most frightening thing about it. He has had time to find a better explanation and has not found one.

All my life, he said, I have been strangely, vividly conscious of another region—not far removed from our own world in one sense, yet wholly different in kind
