Hostile nature

The marshes are simply not arranged around human beings. Blackwood spends his first pages making the reader love the landscape and the rest of the story showing that the affection is not returned.

Through the book The willows are beautiful in section one, watchful in section two, and by section four they are described as a mask over something else entirely.

In the text

across it may be seen in large straggling letters the word Sümpfe, meaning marshes

Section I

The place is introduced as a blank with a label. Blackwood starts from the map, and deliberately: the reader is told the marshes are a real, mapped, unremarkable feature before being told anything else about them.

It’s the willows, of course. The willows mask the others, but the others are feeling about for us.

Section IV

‘Mask’ is a word from stagecraft: the willows are scenery hiding what is behind them. ‘Feeling about for us’ is worse than searching: it makes the hunt blind, patient and impersonal, which fits everything else he has just said.

Isolation

No village, no road, no second boat, no way to signal. The story removes every option in order, and the removals are physical: a paddle, a hull, dry ground, sleep.

Through the book The island itself shrinks each night, so the setting is running out at the same rate as the characters’ composure.

In the text

For one thing—the steering paddle’s gone

Section IV

It is said without fuss, and it ends any possibility of leaving. Blackwood takes the escape route away with a piece of missing equipment, where another writer would use a locked door, and the practical consequence (the Danube in flood without a rudder) is spelled out immediately.

turning over and over on the waves like an otter

Section V

The comparison returns to an animal the two men had argued about seeing days earlier, and the evidence leaves with the current. Nothing is resolved, nobody can be told, and the story ends on a simile borrowed from its own unreliable middle.

The danger of understanding

The Swede is in more trouble the more he works out, and the narrator is safer for refusing to think. The story inverts the usual arrangement in which knowledge is a defence.

Through the book By section four the rule is stated outright: to name is to reveal, and thought itself leaves a trace in the other world.

In the text

the way a sound in the fourth dimension is supposed to come

Section IV

Blackwood reaches for the popular science of 1907, and the borrowed phrase does something folklore could not: it makes the thing geometrical. The horror is now a direction nobody can point in.

All my life, he said, I have been strangely, vividly conscious of another region—not far removed from our own world in one sense, yet wholly different in kind

Section IV

The man introduced as having no imagination turns out to have carried this since childhood. Putting the story’s cosmology in the mouth of its most literal character is the reason the reader accepts it.

The unspeakable

Both men see things they do not report to each other, and the plot advances through what they decide not to mention. The story’s central instruction is a prohibition on speech and then on thinking.

Through the book The narrator hides the second paddle from the conversation; the Swede hides his conclusions; and the night is survived by two men keeping their minds deliberately empty.

In the text

If we let our minds betray our fear, we’re lost, lost utterly.

Section IV

Fear is reclassified as a leak. In most horror stories courage is a virtue; here it is a technical requirement, and the repetition of lost is the only place the Swede’s own composure slips.

Their mark!

Section V

Two words, said twice, are the only naming in the story: a possessive pronoun with nothing attached to it. The horror is that a pronoun turns out to be enough.

Rational explanation

Every unexplained thing gets an explanation from the narrator, and the explanations get worse: wind lifted the paddle, wind dug the funnels, wind tore the canoe. Blackwood lets him do it without comment, and the effect is far more frightening than any argument.

Through the book He abandons the method only on the last page, when the marks in the sand and the marks in the dead man’s flesh turn out to be the same.

In the text

absolutely determined to find an explanation for everything

Section IV

He tells us his own method in one phrase, and it is need, more than scepticism. Every explanation he offers is available to the reader as well, and that is why the story frightens instead of seeming silly: you are invited to use the same defence and to watch it fail.

how the skin and flesh were indented with small hollows, beautifully formed, and exactly similar in shape and kind to the sand-funnels that we had found all over the island

Section V

The proof arrives as a match between two sets of measurements, and the word beautifully is the most frightening thing in the sentence. The narrator’s habit of exact observation, which has been his defence all along, is what finally convicts him.

Myth and religion

The Swede considers the spirit of the elements and then the old gods, and rejects both for the same reason: those have relations with people and can be bargained with. What is here cannot.

Through the book The peasant’s sign of the cross in section one is the only religious act in the story, and it is made by a man leaving at speed.

In the text

these beings who are now about us have absolutely nothing to do with mankind, and it is mere chance that their space happens just at this spot to touch our own

Section IV

This sentence separates the story from every haunting before it. Gods and spirits can be appeased because they want something from us; these do not, and the island was never chosen; it is a coincidence. Lovecraft’s fiction rests on the same premise.

A sacrifice, a victim, might save us by distracting them until we could get away

Section IV

He follows it with an image of wolves stopping to eat the sleigh dogs, and then with the observation that there is no other victim available. The reader works out what he is proposing about a page before the narrator does.

Physical evidence

For a story with nothing in it, the physical detail is relentless: two paddles counted before bed, a scraped blade, a measured tear, cones in the sand described as beautifully formed. The horror is built entirely out of things that can be examined.

Through the book The last of it is the body, which carries the proof and is then swept away, leaving the two men with a story nobody has any reason to believe.

In the text

I can liken it to nothing so much as to the sound of an immense gong, suspended far up in the sky

Section IV

The simile is reached for and admitted to be inadequate (‘I can liken it to nothing so much as’). Every sensory detail in the story arrives with that qualification, which is how a writer builds dread out of a noise he never identifies.

There, you see an attempt to prepare a victim for the sacrifice

Section IV

He is reading the damage as intention, and the reader cannot prove him wrong. The correction that follows, that there are two victims, is worse than the claim, because it means he has already counted himself in.
