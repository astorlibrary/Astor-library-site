The Willows · Algernon Blackwood

Themes

Hostile nature

The marshes are simply not arranged around human beings. Blackwood spends his first pages making the reader love the landscape and the rest of the story showing that the affection is not returned.

The willows are beautiful in section one, watchful in section two, and by section four they are described as a mask over something else entirely.

across it may be seen in large straggling letters the word Sümpfe, meaning marshes

Section I

they somehow give the impression that the entire plain is moving and alive

Section I

Isolation

No village, no road, no second boat, no way to signal. The story removes every option in order, and the removals are physical: a paddle, a hull, dry ground, sleep.

The island itself shrinks each night, so the setting is running out at the same rate as the characters’ composure.

A sacrifice, a victim, might save us by distracting them until we could get away

Section IV

For one thing—the steering paddle’s gone

Section IV

The danger of understanding

The Swede is in more trouble the more he works out, and the narrator is safer for refusing to think. The story inverts the usual arrangement in which knowledge is a defence.

By section four the rule is stated outright: to name is to reveal, and thought itself leaves a trace in the other world.

the way a sound in the fourth dimension is supposed to come

Section IV

All my life, he said, I have been strangely, vividly conscious of another region—not far removed from our own world in one sense, yet wholly different in kind

Section IV

The unspeakable

Both men see things they do not report to each other, and the plot advances through what they decide not to mention. The story’s central instruction is a prohibition on speech and then on thinking.

The narrator hides the second paddle from the conversation; the Swede hides his conclusions; and the night is survived by two men keeping their minds deliberately empty.

If we let our minds betray our fear, we’re lost, lost utterly.

Section IV

Do not refer to them by name. To name is to reveal

Section IV

Rational explanation

Every unexplained thing gets an explanation from the narrator, and the explanations get worse: wind lifted the paddle, wind dug the funnels, wind tore the canoe. Blackwood lets him do it without comment, and the effect is far more frightening than any argument.

He abandons the method only on the last page, when the marks in the sand and the marks in the dead man’s flesh turn out to be the same.

absolutely determined to find an explanation for everything

Section IV

how the skin and flesh were indented with small hollows, beautifully formed, and exactly similar in shape and kind to the sand-funnels that we had found all over the island

Section V

Myth and religion

The Swede considers the spirit of the elements and then the old gods, and rejects both for the same reason: those have relations with people and can be bargained with. What is here cannot.

The peasant’s sign of the cross in section one is the only religious act in the story, and it is made by a man leaving at speed.

All my life, he said, I have been strangely, vividly conscious of another region—not far removed from our own world in one sense, yet wholly different in kind

Section IV

these beings who are now about us have absolutely nothing to do with mankind, and it is mere chance that their space happens just at this spot to touch our own

Section IV

Physical evidence

For a story with nothing in it, the physical detail is relentless: two paddles counted before bed, a scraped blade, a measured tear, cones in the sand described as beautifully formed. The horror is built entirely out of things that can be examined.

The last of it is the body, which carries the proof and is then swept away, leaving the two men with a story nobody has any reason to believe.

I can liken it to nothing so much as to the sound of an immense gong, suspended far up in the sky
