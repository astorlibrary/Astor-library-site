The Willows · Algernon Blackwood

What happens

Section I The Danube marshes, and the island

Fifty paragraphs of landscape before a single event: the Danube losing its channel, the willows moving like water, the sense of a country that is not meant for people. The two canoeists land on an island for the night, and a peasant in a boat shouts something across the water and crosses himself as he is swept past.

Section I
The marshes as they appear on the map and as they appear from a canoe, described at length and with affection.
Section I
Tent, fire, provisions hung from a willow-stem, two yellow paddles under the upturned canoe: all counted, because it will matter.
Section I
A Hungarian peasant passes at speed, shouts a word neither man catches, and crosses himself.

Section II Shapes in the willows at night

The narrator wakes in the night, goes outside, and sees columns rising from the bushes, or thinks he does. He says nothing in the morning. The Swede, who has been the practical one all along, begins to let slip that he has noticed more than he has mentioned, and the island is measurably smaller than it was.

Section II
Bronze-coloured shapes rising and whirling upward out of the willows, seen while fully awake and explained away by morning.
Section II
The bank crumbles into the flood all night; the campsite has less ground than it had at dusk.

Section III One paddle, and a slit in the canoe

The steering paddle is gone, the second paddle has been scraped thin, and there is a long clean tear through the bottom of the canoe. The Swede calls it an attempt to prepare a victim and corrects himself: two victims. The narrator starts whistling, which is what he does when there is nothing he is willing to say.

Section III
Gone in the night, and without it the Danube in flood is not survivable.
Section III
A slither of wood taken neatly out of the hull, in a place with no rocks.
Section III
Hollows in the sand shaped like inverted cones, and the narrator’s explanation that the wind made them.

Section IV The humming, and the Swede’s explanation

The humming becomes continuous and arrives from no direction, or from inside the listener. The Swede sets out his conclusion: this is not the spirit of the elements and not the old gods, because both of those have dealings with people. What is here has none. Their space touches ours at this spot by accident, and the only defence is to keep them out of your mind.

Section IV
Like an immense gong struck far up in the sky, at regular intervals, then without interval at all.
Section IV
The Swede’s account of a lifelong awareness of somewhere adjacent to this world and wholly unlike it.
Section IV
His rule for the night: do not speak of them, do not think of them, because thought is visible where they are.

Section V A night held out, and a body in the willow roots

The Swede goes out in the dark to offer himself and is dragged back. In the morning the sound has gone, the air is ordinary, and they find a corpse caught in the roots at the water’s edge. Touching it releases the humming from its surface. The current turns the body over, and its skin is pitted with the same funnels they found in the sand.

Section V
The Swede tries to give them what they want and has to be fought for.
Section V
Sunlight, a normal river, and two men who have stopped arguing about what happened.
Section V
The last page: a face turned up to the sky, small hollows in the flesh, and a body carried off before it can be buried.
