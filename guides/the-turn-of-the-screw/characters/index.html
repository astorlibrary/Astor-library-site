The Turn of the Screw · Henry James

Characters

The governess

Twenty years old, a country parson’s youngest daughter, in her first post; first appears Prologue

She writes the account, so every fact in it has already passed through her. She is brave, observant and entirely alone with a responsibility she has promised not to share, and the novella gives the reader no instrument for telling her courage apart from her certainty.

Mrs Grose

The housekeeper at Bly; first appears Chapter I

She cannot read, which means every written thing at Bly passes through the governess, and she knows the household’s history but volunteers almost none of it. Her agreement is usually a repetition of what she has just been told, and the governess treats each repetition as independent evidence.

Miles

Ten years old, sent down from school; first appears Chapter III

Expelled for something the school will not put in writing and he will not put into words, he is courteous, oddly adult and perfectly capable of managing the woman in charge of him. The single thing he finally admits, that he said things to boys he liked, is small enough to be anything at all.

Flora

Eight years old, Miles’s sister; first appears Chapter I

For half the book she is all charm, and for one page she is something else, refusing to look where she is told to look and turning a face on the governess that the narrative calls hard. She is removed to London and never gets to give her account.

Peter Quint

The master’s former valet, dead before the story begins; first appears Chapter III

Described before he is named, in a passage of clothes and hair and a habit of going bareheaded, and identified by a housekeeper who has heard the description but not seen the man. He died on the ice coming back from a public house, and everything else said about him at Bly is inference.

Miss Jessel

The previous governess, dead before the story begins; first appears Chapter VI

She left Bly and died, and the reason is given only in hints that the present governess completes for herself. Seen in black across the lake and at the schoolroom desk, she is the position the narrator currently occupies, which is a large part of why she is so frightening.

The master

The children’s uncle, in Harley Street; first appears Prologue

Handsome, busy and entirely offstage after the prologue, he creates the conditions of the story by declining any part in it. His prohibition removes the one person who could confirm or dismiss anything the governess believes, and she keeps to it long after keeping to it has stopped making sense.

Douglas

The reader of the manuscript, in the frame; first appears Prologue

He knew the governess when he was an undergraduate and she was his sister’s governess, kept her manuscript in a locked drawer for years, and admits she was in love with her employer before he begins. He dies soon afterwards and sends the papers to the narrator, so the text arrives with its witness already gone.
