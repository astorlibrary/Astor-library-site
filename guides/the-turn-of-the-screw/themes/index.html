The Turn of the Screw · Henry James

Themes

Evidence and the single witness

Nobody else in the story ever sees an apparition. The governess’s proof is that Mrs Grose recognises a description, and Mrs Grose is being asked to recognise it after being told what to expect. James keeps the reader in exactly the position of a juror with one witness and no corroboration, and then stops.

Corroboration in Chapter V feels total; by Chapter XX, when Flora looks at an empty stretch of water, the same procedure produces nothing at all.

That she should never trouble him⁠—but never, never: neither appeal nor complain nor write about anything; only meet all questions herself, receive all moneys from his solicitor, take the whole thing over and let him alone.

Douglas, Prologue

He was incredibly beautiful, and Mrs. Grose had put her finger on it: everything but a sort of passion of tenderness for him was swept away by his presence.

Chapter III

Innocence

The children’s beauty is described in religious terms and then read as a disguise, and the reading comes first: the governess decides what she is looking for and then finds the children charming in a way that confirms it. Whatever is wrong at Bly, the adults’ certainty about it is doing measurable harm by the last chapter.

A passion of tenderness in Chapter III, a theory of possession in Chapter XII, and a boy held to his confession until his heart stops in Chapter XXIV.

He was incredibly beautiful, and Mrs. Grose had put her finger on it: everything but a sort of passion of tenderness for him was swept away by his presence.

Chapter III

They’re not mine⁠—they’re not ours. They’re his and they’re hers!

The governess, Chapter XII

Silence

Nothing in this book is ever specified. The school will not state Miles’s offence, Mrs Grose will not describe what Quint did, and the governess repeatedly decides that naming a thing would be worse than leaving it. James makes the reader supply the content, which is why no two readers frighten themselves in quite the same way.

A letter that gives no reason, a housekeeper who says only that Quint was too free, and a confession that amounts to saying things to boys who liked him.

The story won’t tell

Douglas, Prologue

Quint was much too free.

Mrs Grose, Chapter VI

Class and service

Everyone at Bly is somebody’s employee, and the horror is organised by rank: a valet who wore his master’s waistcoats, a governess who is neither family nor servant, a housekeeper who cannot read, and a master who has purchased the right not to be told. Quint’s real offence, as Bly describes it, is a matter of position before it is anything else.

The prohibition in the Prologue, the too-free valet in Chapter VI, and the schoolroom desk that two women occupy in turn.

That she should never trouble him⁠—but never, never: neither appeal nor complain nor write about anything; only meet all questions herself, receive all moneys from his solicitor, take the whole thing over and let him alone.

Mrs Grose, Chapter VI

Possession, influence and rescue

The governess understands herself as standing between the children and the dead, a screen, a shield, the one person who will act. That self-description is heroic and also exactly what a frightening adult would say, and the story ends with the rescue and the fatality being the same gesture.

The screen in Chapter VI, the demons in Chapter XII, and the embrace in Chapter XXIV, which is meant to save and is the thing that kills.

Someone would appear there at the turn of a path and would stand before me and smile and approve.

Chapter III

He was looking for little Miles.

The governess, Chapter VI

The frame narrative

Three narrators and a forty-year delay stand between the reader and Bly. The manuscript is in a locked drawer, its keeper is in love with its author, and by the time it is copied out both of them are dead, so the text arrives with every route back to a fact closed off.

Douglas promises a second turn of the screw in the Prologue and then dies; the novella never returns to the fire, and no outer narrator comments at the end.

The story had held us, round the fire, sufficiently breathless, but except the obvious remark that it was gruesome, as, on Christmas Eve in an old house, a strange tale should essentially be, I remember no comment uttered till somebody happened to say that it was the only case he had met in which such a visitation had fallen on a child.
