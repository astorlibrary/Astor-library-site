Evidence and the single witness

Nobody else in the story ever sees an apparition. The governess’s proof is that Mrs Grose recognises a description, and Mrs Grose is being asked to recognise it after being told what to expect. James keeps the reader in exactly the position of a juror with one witness and no corroboration, and then stops.

Through the book Corroboration in Chapter V feels total; by Chapter XX, when Flora looks at an empty stretch of water, the same procedure produces nothing at all.

In the text

Someone would appear there at the turn of a path and would stand before me and smile and approve.

Chapter III

This is the wish that the first apparition answers, and James puts it on the page a paragraph before Quint appears. The man she wants is the employer she has promised not to write to, so the figure on the tower arrives in a space her own longing has already prepared.

She’s there, she’s there!

The governess, Chapter XX

This is the one moment when the apparition is put to a public test, in daylight, with two other people present. Neither of them sees anything, and the governess responds by insisting more loudly, which tells the reader what kind of proof she is now capable of accepting.

Innocence

The children’s beauty is described in religious terms and then read as a disguise, and the reading comes first: the governess decides what she is looking for and then finds the children charming in a way that confirms it. Whatever is wrong at Bly, the adults’ certainty about it is doing measurable harm by the last chapter.

Through the book A passion of tenderness in Chapter III, a theory of possession in Chapter XII, and a boy held to his confession until his heart stops in Chapter XXIV.

In the text

He was incredibly beautiful, and Mrs. Grose had put her finger on it: everything but a sort of passion of tenderness for him was swept away by his presence.

Chapter III

She decides the charge against him is impossible on the evidence of his face, and says so before she has asked him a single question. Notice ‘swept away’: from the start her judgement of Miles is described as something that happens to her.

Peter Quint⁠—you devil!

Miles, Chapter XXIV

The line can be read two ways with no change of wording: the devil is either Quint at the window or the woman holding him. The governess takes it as her triumph and calls it ‘his supreme surrender of the name’, which is the language of an interrogation.

Silence

Nothing in this book is ever specified. The school will not state Miles’s offence, Mrs Grose will not describe what Quint did, and the governess repeatedly decides that naming a thing would be worse than leaving it. James makes the reader supply the content, which is why no two readers frighten themselves in quite the same way.

Through the book A letter that gives no reason, a housekeeper who says only that Quint was too free, and a confession that amounts to saying things to boys who liked him.

In the text

The story won’t tell

Douglas, Prologue

He is answering a question about the governess’s feelings, and he makes the manuscript the one that will not tell. James establishes in the frame that the document is capable of keeping things back, and readers are warned before the first chapter that omission here is deliberate.

Quint was much too free.

Mrs Grose, Chapter VI

Four words, and they are the entire documented case against Peter Quint. Free is a word about rank as much as conduct, and the governess converts it into something much larger over the following chapters without ever being given more.

Class and service

Everyone at Bly is somebody’s employee, and the horror is organised by rank: a valet who wore his master’s waistcoats, a governess who is neither family nor servant, a housekeeper who cannot read, and a master who has purchased the right not to be told. Quint’s real offence, as Bly describes it, is a matter of position before it is anything else.

Through the book The prohibition in the Prologue, the too-free valet in Chapter VI, and the schoolroom desk that two women occupy in turn.

In the text

That she should never trouble him⁠—but never, never: neither appeal nor complain nor write about anything; only meet all questions herself, receive all moneys from his solicitor, take the whole thing over and let him alone.

Douglas, Prologue

Every verb in the list is one the governess is forbidden to perform, and the accumulation makes the prohibition sound like a courtesy. The plot that follows is simply this sentence being kept: there is no adult she is permitted to consult, and she never breaks it.

You terrible, miserable woman!

The governess, Chapter XV

She has just recorded the extraordinary chill of feeling that she was the intruder, and the outburst is what that feeling turns into. Both women have sat at this desk in charge of these children, which is why the insult lands so close to home.

Possession, influence and rescue

The governess understands herself as standing between the children and the dead, a screen, a shield, the one person who will act. That self-description is heroic and also exactly what a frightening adult would say, and the story ends with the rescue and the fatality being the same gesture.

Through the book The screen in Chapter VI, the demons in Chapter XII, and the embrace in Chapter XXIV, which is meant to save and is the thing that kills.

In the text

He was looking for little Miles.

The governess, Chapter VI

She calls this a portentous clearness, and for the first time she states a motive for an apparition, where before she stated facts about them. From here the sightings stop being events to be explained and become moves in a campaign she believes she has understood.

They’re not mine⁠—they’re not ours. They’re his and they’re hers!

The governess, Chapter XII

The children’s goodness has been reinterpreted as evidence against them, which means no conduct of theirs can now count in their favour. Possession is stated grammatically, in four possessive pronouns, and the sentence takes them away from Mrs Grose as well.

The frame narrative

Three narrators and a forty-year delay stand between the reader and Bly. The manuscript is in a locked drawer, its keeper is in love with its author, and by the time it is copied out both of them are dead, so the text arrives with every route back to a fact closed off.

Through the book Douglas promises a second turn of the screw in the Prologue and then dies; the novella never returns to the fire, and no outer narrator comments at the end.

In the text

The story had held us, round the fire, sufficiently breathless, but except the obvious remark that it was gruesome, as, on Christmas Eve in an old house, a strange tale should essentially be, I remember no comment uttered till somebody happened to say that it was the only case he had met in which such a visitation had fallen on a child.

Prologue

The sentence is sixty-two words long and arrives at its subject, a child, only at the very end, which is how the whole book proceeds. And the first thing we are told about the story we are about to read is that it is not the one being told.

So I saw him as I see the letters I form on this page

Chapter III

She guarantees the apparition by comparing it with her own handwriting, which is the only thing the reader actually has. The comparison is meant as proof and works as a warning: everything we can see is the writing.
