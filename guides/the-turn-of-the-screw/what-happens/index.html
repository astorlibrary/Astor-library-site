The Turn of the Screw · Henry James

What happens

Prologue Christmas Eve, and a manuscript in a locked drawer

Guests at a country house are trading ghost stories when Douglas offers one he has kept to himself for years. He has to send to town for the manuscript, which delays the telling by two nights, and he dies before the outer narrator makes the copy the reader is holding.

Prologue, the wager
A story about a child prompts Douglas to promise one about two.
Prologue, the interview
A young clergyman’s daughter answers an advertisement in Harley Street and accepts a post nobody else will take.
Prologue, the condition
She promises never to appeal to her employer about anything, and never sees him again.

Chapters I-IV Bly, two beautiful children, and a man on the tower

The governess arrives to find Flora enchanting, Mrs Grose welcoming and the house larger than she expected. A letter says Miles has been expelled and gives no reason; she decides to ask nobody. Twice she sees a strange man, once on the tower and once looking in through the dining-room window.

Chapter I
First evening at Bly, and a place that seems to her like a great drifting ship.
Chapter II
The headmaster’s letter, forwarded unopened by the uncle, is read, talked over with Mrs Grose and never answered.
Chapter III
Miles arrives; at the end of her own hour a figure appears at the top of the tower.
Chapter IV
The same man looks in at a window, and the governess goes out to take his place at the glass.

Chapters V-VIII Mrs Grose names Quint and Miss Jessel

From the governess’s description, Mrs Grose identifies Peter Quint, a former valet who is dead, and then Miss Jessel, the previous governess, also dead. What Mrs Grose actually says about the pair is much less than what the governess takes from it, and the difference between the two is the engine of the rest of the book.

Chapter V
The description is given, and Mrs Grose’s face finishes it.
Chapter VI
Quint was too free with the boy; the governess decides she is a screen between the children and the dead.
Chapter VII
A woman in black across the lake, seen while Flora plays with her back to the water.
Chapter VIII
Mrs Grose is pressed on what she knew and admits she said nothing at the time.

Chapters IX-XIV Night watches, and the children’s explanations

The governess keeps watch by night and finds Quint on the staircase, Miss Jessel on the stair below, Flora at the window and Miles on the lawn looking up. Every incident is explained away charmingly by one child or the other, and she begins to read the charm itself as the proof.

Chapter IX
A figure on the staircase in the small hours, met without a candle.
Chapter X
Flora is found at the window; Miles admits he was out on the lawn to be, for once, bad.
Chapter XI
The governess reconstructs the night with Mrs Grose in the daylight.
Chapter XII
She concludes that the children belong to the dead and always have.
Chapter XIII
Weeks of unspoken watching, in which nobody names anything.
Chapter XIV
Miles, walking to church, asks when he is to go back to school.

Chapters XV-XXI A letter, a lake, and Flora sent away

The governess writes to the guardian at last, finds Miss Jessel at her own schoolroom desk, and takes Mrs Grose to the lake to force Flora into an admission. Flora sees nothing, turns on her, and is taken to London the next morning; the letter never left the house.

Chapter XV
Miss Jessel is found in the schoolroom and addressed aloud.
Chapter XVI
The governess agrees to write to the children’s uncle.
Chapter XVII
A night scene at Miles’s bedside, ended by a gust that blows out the candle.
Chapter XVIII
Miles plays the piano while Flora slips out of the house.
Chapter XIX
The search leads to the lake, where the boat has been moved.
Chapter XX
The governess names Miss Jessel across the water; Flora sees nothing and asks to be taken away.
Chapter XXI
Mrs Grose reports Flora’s new vocabulary, and the letter is found to have been stolen.

Chapters XXII-XXIV Alone in the house with Miles

With Flora and Mrs Grose gone, the governess sets out to get a confession over a dish of mutton. Miles admits he said things at school; the white face appears at the window; and in getting the name out of him she is holding a child whose heart has stopped.

Chapter XXII
The house’s servants stare, and dinner is served downstairs like a trial.
Chapter XXIII
Miles asks to be let alone and is not.
Chapter XXIV
The confession, the figure at the glass, and the last three words of the book.
