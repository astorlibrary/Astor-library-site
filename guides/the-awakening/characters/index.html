The Awakening · Kate Chopin

Characters

Edna Pontellier

A Kentucky-born woman of twenty-eight, married to a New Orleans broker; first appears Chapter I

She married partly by accident and partly because her father disapproved, and she has been performing a role ever since without noticing that it was one. The novel gives her a series of refusals instead of a plan, and Chopin is careful that she never finds words equal to what she wants.

Léonce Pontellier

A New Orleans broker, Edna’s husband; first appears Chapter I

He is not cruel, and that is the difficulty: he sends bonbons from New York, pays every bill, and honestly cannot understand why a wife would object to being valued. When Edna becomes unmanageable he consults a doctor, and when she moves out he puts a notice in the paper about redecorating so that nobody’s credit suffers.

Robert Lebrun

The son of the Grand Isle proprietor; first appears Chapter I

He has spent every summer attaching himself harmlessly to a married woman, and is caught out when one of them takes it seriously. His last proposal, that Léonce might be persuaded to set her free, shows that he has understood nothing, and he leaves sooner than face what she has actually said.

Adèle Ratignolle

Edna’s friend at Grand Isle, a Creole mother; first appears Chapter IV

She is the novel’s mother-woman and is not caricatured: she is warm, shrewd, physically frank, and the person who first gets Edna talking about herself. She also delivers the sentence that does most damage, at the moment when Edna is least able to argue with it.

Mademoiselle Reisz

A pianist, disliked by nearly everyone; first appears Chapter IX

She is unmarried, ill-tempered, badly dressed and the best musician in the book, and she is the only character who tells Edna the truth about what an independent life would cost. Her test (feeling the shoulder blades for wings) is affectionate and entirely serious.

Alcée Arobin

A man about town; first appears Chapter XXIII

He is practised, unserious and entirely unembarrassed, and Chopin punishes neither of them for what happens between them. What Edna feels afterwards is a kind of clarity, with no shame and no love in it, and Chopin’s refusal to moralise the scene is one reason reviewers condemned the book in 1899.

Doctor Mandelet

The Pontelliers’ family physician; first appears Chapter XXII

Called in to explain a wife to a husband, he declines to pathologise her and watches instead. He is the one man who guesses what is happening, and his offer to listen comes on the night Edna has already decided there is nothing left to say.

Victor Lebrun

Robert’s younger brother, left in charge at Grand Isle; first appears Chapter VIII

He is careless, boastful and useful to the structure: he supplies gossip about Robert, sings the song Edna cannot get out of her head, and is the last person to speak to her. He is hammering at a shutter out of season when she walks down to the water.
