The Awakening · Kate Chopin

What happens

Chapters I–XIII A summer at the shore

Léonce Pontellier looks up from his newspaper at a sunburnt wife and sees damaged property. Over the weeks that follow Edna sits with Adèle Ratignolle, hears Mademoiselle Reisz play, and finally swims out alone at night and has a moment’s terror that she will not get back. The next morning she and Robert Lebrun cross to Chênière Caminada and she sleeps through most of a day.

Chapters I–V
The parrot, the husband’s newspaper, the Creole frankness Edna cannot match, and Robert attached to her for the season.
Chapters VI–IX
Adèle’s playing keeps them company; Mademoiselle Reisz’s undoes Edna completely in a room full of people.
Chapters X–XIII
Edna swims out alone, refuses to be walked home, and crosses to the Chênière the following morning with Robert.

Chapters XIV–XVIII Robert goes to Mexico, and the season ends

Robert announces at a few hours’ notice that he is leaving for Mexico, and Edna discovers how much has been arranged without reference to her. Back in New Orleans she abandons her reception day, throws her wedding ring on the carpet, and stamps on it.

Chapters XIV–XVI
Robert’s departure; Edna tells Adèle that she would give her life for her children but not herself.
Chapters XVII–XVIII
The abandoned Tuesday, the ring on the carpet, and a visit to the Ratignolles that leaves her flat.

Chapters XIX–XXVIII Painting, money, and two visitors

Edna paints in the atelier, goes to the races, lets Alcée Arobin into the house, and begins to keep accounts of her own. Mademoiselle Reisz reads her Robert’s letters and tells her what it takes to be an artist; her father visits, her husband goes to New York, and the children are sent to their grandmother.

Chapters XIX–XXII
The atelier, the abandoned household routine, and Doctor Mandelet’s cautious diagnosis.
Chapters XXIII–XXVI
Arobin’s attentions, Reisz’s test of her shoulder blades, and the decision to take a small house of her own.
Chapters XXVII–XXVIII
A night with Arobin, and a morning of understanding, with no shame in it.

Chapters XXIX–XXXIV The dinner, and the small house

Edna gives a farewell dinner at her husband’s table, wearing her husband’s birthday diamonds in her hair and looking, as she leans back, like a woman who stands alone. She moves into the little house around the corner, paid for out of her own winnings and her sketches, and Robert walks back into the city and into Mademoiselle Reisz’s rooms.

Chapters XXIX–XXX
Nine guests, too much wine, and a hostess presiding over the end of a household.
Chapters XXXI–XXXIV
A house small enough to be hers, and Robert’s return, awkward and formal, at her own table.

Chapters XXXV–XXXIX Adèle's confinement, and the water again

Robert says at last what he has been imagining, that Léonce might set her free, and Edna tells him she is not anyone’s to give away. She is called out to Adèle’s childbirth, hears the words about the children, walks home, finds Robert gone, lies awake till morning, and goes back to Grand Isle.

Chapters XXXV–XXXVI
Robert’s plan for her freedom and Edna’s refusal of the terms it assumes.
Chapter XXXVII
Adèle’s confinement, witnessed against Edna’s will, and a whispered instruction.
Chapters XXXVIII–XXXIX
A last conversation with Doctor Mandelet, an empty house, and the beach out of season.
