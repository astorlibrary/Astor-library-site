The Awakening · Kate Chopin

Themes

Waking

Chopin’s title names a process, and the process is mostly subtraction: Edna stops receiving visitors, stops going to weddings, stops pretending to be interested. Chopin is careful that waking supplies no programme: the light that dawns in Chapter VI is the kind that, showing the way, forbids it.

The vague anguish of Chapter III, the formulation in Chapter VI, the swim in Chapter X, the plain statement in Chapter XIX that she began to do as she liked, and the conversation with Doctor Mandelet about being a dupe to illusions.

An indescribable oppression, which seemed to generate in some unfamiliar part of her consciousness, filled her whole being with a vague anguish.

Chapter III

A certain light was beginning to dawn dimly within her

Chapter VI

Marriage and property

Léonce’s affection is expressed as inventory: he checks her for damage, sends expensive parcels, and is complimented by the town on his generosity. Edna’s rebellion takes the same vocabulary and turns it around, until she can say flatly that she is not among her husband’s possessions and will give herself where she chooses.

The opening inspection of Chapter I, the wedding ring stamped on in Chapter XVII, the accounts and winnings of the middle chapters, and the refusal offered to Robert in Chapter XXXVI.

looking at his wife as one looks at a valuable piece of personal property which has suffered some damage

Chapter I

Once she stopped, and taking off her wedding ring, flung it upon the carpet. When she saw it lying there, she stamped her heel upon it, striving to crush it.

Chapter XVII

Mothers and children

The novel sets a woman who is entirely absorbed in her children beside one who loves hers and will not be dissolved by them, and declines to call either of them a bad mother. Edna’s formula (she would give her money and her life for her children, but not herself) is tested by the one thing the book will not let her avoid, which is Adèle’s confinement.

The mother-women of Chapter IV, the distinction drawn for Adèle in Chapter XVI, the whispered instruction in Chapter XXXVII, and the thought of Raoul and Etienne in the last pages.

In short, Mrs. Pontellier was not a mother-woman.

Chapter IV

I would give up the unessential; I would give my money, I would give my life for my children; but I wouldn’t give myself.

Edna Pontellier, Chapter XVI

Art

Edna paints, sells sketches and puts the proceeds, with her race winnings and her mother’s money, towards a house of her own, which makes the question of whether she is an artist a financial one as well as a spiritual one. Mademoiselle Reisz supplies the standard (“The soul that dares and defies”), and the novel leaves open whether Edna meets it.

The atelier in Chapter XIX, Reisz’s test of the shoulder blades in Chapter XXVII, the money that buys the pigeon house, and Reisz’s imagined sneer in Chapter XXXIX.

But the very passions themselves were aroused within her soul, swaying it, lashing it, as the waves daily beat upon her splendid body.

Chapter IX

The bird that would soar above the level plain of tradition and prejudice must have strong wings. It is a sad spectacle to see the weaklings bruised, exhausted, fluttering back to earth.

Mademoiselle Reisz, Chapter XXVII

Isolation

The original title was A Solitary Soul, and the book finds again and again that Edna’s independence and her loneliness are the same fact. Every person she might have confided in (a husband, a lover, a friend, a doctor, a pianist) is available on terms that would require her to become somebody else.

The ‘abysses of solitude’ of Chapter VI, the depression after the Ratignolles’ domestic evening, the empty pigeon house after Robert goes, and the entirely deserted beach of the last chapter.

An indescribable oppression, which seemed to generate in some unfamiliar part of her consciousness, filled her whole being with a vague anguish.

Chapter III

In short, Mrs. Pontellier was beginning to realize her position in the universe as a human being, and to recognize her relations as an individual to the world within and about her.

Chapter VI

The sea

The Gulf is where Edna first does something no one has done for her, and the passage describing its voice is printed twice, thirty-three chapters apart, with almost no alteration. Chopin makes the water both the medium of her freedom and the thing she has no strength to come back from.

The first statement of the sea passage in Chapter VI, the night swim in Chapter X, and the same words returning at the opening of Chapter XXXIX.

The voice of the sea is seductive; never ceasing, whispering, clamoring, murmuring, inviting the soul to wander for a spell in abysses of solitude; to lose itself in mazes of inward contemplation.

Chapter VI

But the very passions themselves were aroused within her soul, swaying it, lashing it, as the waves daily beat upon her splendid body.

Chapter IX
