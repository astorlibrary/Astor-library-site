Waking

Chopin’s title names a process, and the process is mostly subtraction: Edna stops receiving visitors, stops going to weddings, stops pretending to be interested. Chopin is careful that waking supplies no programme: the light that dawns in Chapter VI is the kind that, showing the way, forbids it.

Through the book The vague anguish of Chapter III, the formulation in Chapter VI, the swim in Chapter X, the plain statement in Chapter XIX that she began to do as she liked, and the conversation with Doctor Mandelet about being a dupe to illusions.

In the text

An indescribable oppression, which seemed to generate in some unfamiliar part of her consciousness, filled her whole being with a vague anguish.

Chapter III

Every term in the sentence admits ignorance (indescribable, unfamiliar, vague), and the vagueness is deliberate. Chopin does not give Edna a word for her own condition at the moment she first feels it, and that sets the book apart from a novel of grievance.

She began to do as she liked and to feel as she liked.

Chapter XIX

One flat sentence, two verbs, no adverb of approval or alarm, and it describes what would have been a scandal in most novels of the period. The second half is the more radical: she stops performing feelings as well as duties, and the household’s whole machinery of visits and callers falls over as a result.

Marriage and property

Léonce’s affection is expressed as inventory: he checks her for damage, sends expensive parcels, and is complimented by the town on his generosity. Edna’s rebellion takes the same vocabulary and turns it around, until she can say flatly that she is not among her husband’s possessions and will give herself where she chooses.

Through the book The opening inspection of Chapter I, the wedding ring stamped on in Chapter XVII, the accounts and winnings of the middle chapters, and the refusal offered to Robert in Chapter XXXVI.

In the text

looking at his wife as one looks at a valuable piece of personal property which has suffered some damage

Chapter I

The simile is the narrator’s, dropped into an otherwise placid domestic paragraph without comment. Two sentences later Edna holds out her hands for her rings, and Chopin leaves the reader to make the connection.

Once she stopped, and taking off her wedding ring, flung it upon the carpet. When she saw it lying there, she stamped her heel upon it, striving to crush it.

Chapter XVII

The gesture fails: the boot heel does not mark the ring, and she puts it back on within the paragraph. Chopin gives her heroine one melodramatic act and then shows the object surviving it undamaged, which is a fair summary of what the institution will do to her rebellion.

Mothers and children

The novel sets a woman who is entirely absorbed in her children beside one who loves hers and will not be dissolved by them, and declines to call either of them a bad mother. Edna’s formula (she would give her money and her life for her children, but not herself) is tested by the one thing the book will not let her avoid, which is Adèle’s confinement.

Through the book The mother-women of Chapter IV, the distinction drawn for Adèle in Chapter XVI, the whispered instruction in Chapter XXXVII, and the thought of Raoul and Etienne in the last pages.

In the text

In short, Mrs. Pontellier was not a mother-woman.

Chapter IV

‘In short’ presents the sentence as a summary of the preceding paragraph, when in fact it introduces a category Chopin has just named. The hyphen makes the mother-woman a type of person, and Edna is defined by not being one.

Think of the children, Edna. Oh think of the children! Remember them!

Adèle Ratignolle, Chapter XXXVII

Three imperatives from a woman who has just given birth, addressed to the one person in the room who did not want to be there. Chopin gives the novel’s decisive sentence to the sweetest character in it, and makes it an appeal, which is why Edna cannot dismiss it.

Art

Edna paints, sells sketches and puts the proceeds, with her race winnings and her mother’s money, towards a house of her own, which makes the question of whether she is an artist a financial one as well as a spiritual one. Mademoiselle Reisz supplies the standard (“The soul that dares and defies”), and the novel leaves open whether Edna meets it.

Through the book The atelier in Chapter XIX, Reisz’s test of the shoulder blades in Chapter XXVII, the money that buys the pigeon house, and Reisz’s imagined sneer in Chapter XXXIX.

In the text

The bird that would soar above the level plain of tradition and prejudice must have strong wings. It is a sad spectacle to see the weaklings bruised, exhausted, fluttering back to earth.

Mademoiselle Reisz, Chapter XXVII

It reaches the reader at second hand, quoted by a woman repeating a warning she has decided to treat as a joke. Chopin then plants the image where it will be needed: the last chapter opens with a bird with a broken wing coming down to the water, and offers no gloss on it at all.

A bird with a broken wing was beating the air above, reeling, fluttering, circling disabled down, down to the water.

Chapter XXXIX

Mademoiselle Reisz’s warning about weaklings fluttering back to earth arrives as an event in the landscape, twelve chapters later and without a word of commentary. Whether the reader takes it as Edna’s verdict on herself or as something merely happening on the beach is left entirely open, and the sentence is placed between two blocks of prose about the sea.

Isolation

The original title was A Solitary Soul, and the book finds again and again that Edna’s independence and her loneliness are the same fact. Every person she might have confided in (a husband, a lover, a friend, a doctor, a pianist) is available on terms that would require her to become somebody else.

Through the book The ‘abysses of solitude’ of Chapter VI, the depression after the Ratignolles’ domestic evening, the empty pigeon house after Robert goes, and the entirely deserted beach of the last chapter.

In the text

In short, Mrs. Pontellier was beginning to realize her position in the universe as a human being, and to recognize her relations as an individual to the world within and about her.

Chapter VI

The phrasing is that of a philosophical treatise, and it is applied to a woman on a holiday gallery who has done nothing more remarkable than agree to a swim. Chopin repeats the formula ‘In short, Mrs. Pontellier’ from two chapters earlier, so that the negative definition is now answered by a positive one.

And you call yourself an artist! What pretensions, Madame! The artist must possess the courageous soul that dares and defies.

Mademoiselle Reisz, Chapter XXXIX

The one standard Edna is still measuring herself against is delivered in a voice she is supplying herself, from a woman who is not present. It is the last piece of dialogue in the novel, and it is a rebuke: the book ends with its heroine inventing her sternest critic and then going on.

The sea

The Gulf is where Edna first does something no one has done for her, and the passage describing its voice is printed twice, thirty-three chapters apart, with almost no alteration. Chopin makes the water both the medium of her freedom and the thing she has no strength to come back from.

Through the book The first statement of the sea passage in Chapter VI, the night swim in Chapter X, and the same words returning at the opening of Chapter XXXIX.

In the text

The voice of the sea is seductive; never ceasing, whispering, clamoring, murmuring, inviting the soul to wander for a spell in abysses of solitude; to lose itself in mazes of inward contemplation.

Chapter VI

The tense is present and general, describing what the sea always does and not what it did that evening, which lifts the sentence out of the narrative and lets it be used again. The invitation is to solitude, which is not the same as freedom, and Chopin takes the offer literally in the last chapter.

There was the hum of bees, and the musky odor of pinks filled the air.

Chapter XXXIX

The book closes on sounds and smells from Edna’s Kentucky childhood, with no statement about the water at all. Chopin ends with bees and flowers, the world of the senses where Edna’s awakening began, and does not write the sentence a reader is waiting for.
