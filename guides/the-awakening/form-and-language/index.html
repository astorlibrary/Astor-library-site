The Awakening · Kate Chopin

Form and language

Free indirect style

Third-person narration that takes on the vocabulary and bias of the character it follows.

The narration stays inside Edna’s perception without explaining her, so phrases like ‘she began to do as she liked’ arrive with the flatness of her own thinking, and none of the emphasis a moralising narrator would add.

looking at his wife as one looks at a valuable piece of personal property which has suffered some damage

Chapter I

The repeated sentence

A passage reprinted almost word for word at a distance, so that the second appearance carries the first with it.

The description of the sea’s voice appears in Chapter VI, near the beginning of Edna’s awakening, and again at the start of Chapter XXXIX with the tenses and the setting unchanged, so the reader recognises it before Edna reaches the water.

The voice of the sea is seductive; never ceasing, whispering, clamoring, murmuring, inviting the soul to wander for a spell in abysses of solitude; to lose itself in mazes of inward contemplation.

Chapter VI

Symbolic objects

An object the narrative comes back to until it stands for the argument itself.

A parrot in a cage speaking a language nobody understands, a wedding ring that will not crush under a boot heel, a house named after pigeons, a bird with a broken wing over the water: the objects are placed and then left alone.

looking at his wife as one looks at a valuable piece of personal property which has suffered some damage

Chapter I

Music as event

Performance used to make something happen in the plot.

Adèle’s playing makes agreeable pictures; Mademoiselle Reisz’s playing leaves Edna trembling, choking and blinded by tears in a public room, and the difference between the two performances is the difference between the two futures on offer.

But the very passions themselves were aroused within her soul, swaying it, lashing it, as the waves daily beat upon her splendid body.

Chapter IX

Foils

A character constructed to throw another’s choices into relief by making different ones.

Adèle and Mademoiselle Reisz are the two lives on offer, the adored mother and the unloved artist, and Chopin makes each of them attractive and each insufficient, so Edna is turning down a real choice.

In short, Mrs. Pontellier was not a mother-woman.

Chapter IV

Omission

Decisive events placed in the gaps between chapters, off the page.

The night with Arobin and the drowning itself are handled by omission or by a change of scene, and the second gap is one reason the ending is still argued about.

But they need not have thought that they could possess her, body and soul.

Chapter XXXIX
