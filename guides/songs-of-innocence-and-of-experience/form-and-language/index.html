Songs of Innocence and of Experience · William Blake

Form and language

Illuminated printing

Blake’s method of etching words and pictures together on copper, printing them and colouring each copy by hand.

Every song was designed as a picture as well as a poem, with vines, figures and scenes growing round the text. The designs sometimes reinforce the words and sometimes complicate them, and because each copy was coloured separately, no two readers of Blake’s own copies saw quite the same book.

And I wrote my happy songs / Every child may joy to hear.

The Piper, Introduction (Innocence)

Paired poems

Poems that share a title or situation and answer each other.

Holy Thursday, The Chimney Sweeper and Nurse’s Song appear in both books, and Infant Joy, The Divine Image and The Lamb have clear counterparts in Infant Sorrow, The Human Abstract and The Tyger. Reading the pairs together shows each state limiting the other.

I have no name: / I am but two days old.

The two-day-old infant, Infant Joy

Symbols

An object or creature that carries a meaning beyond itself.

Lamb, tiger, rose, worm, sunflower, chains and charters work as symbols that gather meaning across the book without being pinned to one sense. The Sick Rose can be read as love, sexuality, the soul or England, and its brevity keeps all the readings open.

He is called by thy name, / For he calls himself a Lamb.

The Lamb

Questions

Questions that the poem does not answer, or answers only in part.

The Lamb asks a question and answers it; The Tyger asks question after question and answers none. Holy Thursday in Experience and On Another’s Sorrow are built of questions too. The shift from answered to unanswered questions is one of the clearest differences between the two states.

Little Lamb who made thee? / Dost thou know who made thee?

The Lamb

Refrain and repetition

A phrase brought back at intervals, so that its meaning shifts with what has happened since.

Blake borrows the repetitions of hymns and nursery rhymes: Little Lamb, God bless thee!; Merrily, merrily, to welcome in the year; the repeated every of London. The first stanza of The Tyger returns at the end with one word changed, could becoming dare.

Little Lamb who made thee? / Dost thou know who made thee?

The Lamb

Hymn and nursery metres

Short, strongly rhythmic lines and simple rhymes drawn from children’s verse and hymns.

Isaac Watts’s hymns for children and the forms of nursery rhymes echo through the songs, which makes them easy to remember and deceptively simple. In Experience the same metres carry bitterness and horror, and the gap between the tune and the content does much of the work.

So your chimneys I sweep, & in soot I sleep.

Tom Dacre’s friend, The Chimney Sweeper (Innocence)

Irony

Saying one thing in a form that invites the opposite judgement.

Some songs of Innocence end on a moral that many readers find hard to accept: if all do their duty they need not fear harm, cherish pity, lest you drive an angel from your door. The speakers mean them; Blake invites us to see who benefits from them.

And I am black, but O my soul is white; / White as an angel is the English child, / But I am black, as if bereav'd of light.

The little black boy, The Little Black Boy

Voices and speakers

Poems spoken in the voice of a character distinct from the poet.

Most songs are spoken by someone other than Blake: a piper, a bard, a black child, sweeps, nurses, a vagabond, a clod and a pebble, Earth herself. Each voice has its own limits, and the meaning often lies in what the speaker cannot see.

And I wrote my happy songs / Every child may joy to hear.

The Piper, Introduction (Innocence)
