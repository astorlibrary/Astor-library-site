Songs of Innocence and of Experience · William Blake

Themes

Contrary states

Blake’s subtitle calls innocence and experience two contrary states of the human soul, and Blake prints them side by side, often as paired poems. Innocence is trusting and joyful, and also open to exploitation; experience sees through cruelty and hypocrisy, and is also soured by jealousy and fear. Neither book is the whole truth, and many poems only make full sense beside their counterparts.

The Piper and the Bard; The Lamb and The Tyger; the two Chimney Sweepers, Nurse’s Songs, Holy Thursdays and Infant songs; The Divine Image and The Human Abstract.

And I wrote my happy songs / Every child may joy to hear.

The Piper, Introduction (Innocence)

I have no name: / I am but two days old.

The two-day-old infant, Infant Joy

Childhood

Almost every poem involves a child: playing, lost, sold, shaved, fed by charity, taught by a mother, blessed or bound. In Innocence children are protected by nurses, mothers, angels and God; in Experience the same adults become the agents of misery, and the newborn leaps into a dangerous world. Blake presents the child as a figure of vision and as a victim of the society that uses children’s labour.

Infant Joy and Infant Sorrow; Tom Dacre and the sweep in the snow; the charity children; the school boy’s cage; the little vagabond.

And I wrote my happy songs / Every child may joy to hear.

The Piper, Introduction (Innocence)

Little Lamb who made thee? / Dost thou know who made thee?

The Lamb

The Church and the priests

Innocence imagines a God who is present in mercy, pity, peace and love and who weeps with every child. Experience attacks the Church that preaches that God while children freeze: parents pray while their son sweeps, priests in black gowns bind joys and desires, Thou shalt not is written over a chapel door, and the church blackens with the sweeper’s cry. The target is institutional religion; the divine image in humankind is left standing.

The Divine Image; On Another’s Sorrow; God and his Priest and King; The Garden of Love; every black’ning Church; The Little Vagabond; A Little Boy Lost.

He is called by thy name, / For he calls himself a Lamb.

The Lamb

And I am black, but O my soul is white; / White as an angel is the English child, / But I am black, as if bereav'd of light.

The little black boy, The Little Black Boy

Poverty and labour

The songs name the poverty of Blake’s London: climbing boys sold to sweep chimneys, charity children paraded through St Paul’s, soldiers and harlots in the streets, a rich and fruitful land where babes are reduced to misery. The Human Abstract suggests that the virtues of pity and mercy depend on the poverty that calls them out.

So your chimneys I sweep; Holy Thursday; Pity would be no more; the hapless Soldier’s sigh; the youthful Harlot’s curse.

So your chimneys I sweep, & in soot I sleep.

Tom Dacre’s friend, The Chimney Sweeper (Innocence)

So if all do their duty they need not fear harm.

Tom Dacre’s friend, The Chimney Sweeper (Innocence)

Authority and the mind-forg’d manacles

Parents, priests, kings, schoolmasters and a jealous father of men all keep others bound, and Blake’s images of chains, bands, cages, charters and nets recur. In London he hears mind-forg’d manacles in every voice: the most powerful constraints are the ones people make and accept in their own minds.

Earth’s heavy chain; the swaddling bands; the caged bird of The School Boy; charter’d streets; mind-forg’d manacles; the snare of The Human Abstract.

So if all do their duty they need not fear harm.

Tom Dacre’s friend, The Chimney Sweeper (Innocence)

Selfish father of men! / Cruel, jealous, selfish fear!

Earth, Earth’s Answer

Love and repression

Experience shows love hidden, forbidden or poisoned: a worm’s dark secret love destroys a rose, a garden of love is filled with graves, anger kept secret grows into a poison tree, and a girl who loved in the open is called a crime. Blake treats the repression of desire as the source of cruelty, and the free expression of love as holy.

The Clod and the Pebble; Earth’s free Love with bondage bound; The Sick Rose; The Garden of Love; A Poison Tree; A Little Girl Lost.

Selfish father of men! / Cruel, jealous, selfish fear!

Earth, Earth’s Answer

Break this heavy chain / That does freeze my bones around.

Earth, Earth’s Answer

Creation

The Lamb and The Tyger ask the same question from opposite sides: who made thee? In Innocence the answer comes in the next stanza: the gentle Lamb of God. In Experience the question is asked again and again, about a burning creature forged in a furnace, and the poem ends by asking whether the same maker made both. Blake leaves the answer open.

Little Lamb who made thee?; What immortal hand or eye; Did he who made the Lamb make thee?; Dare frame thy fearful symmetry.

Little Lamb who made thee? / Dost thou know who made thee?

The Lamb

He is called by thy name, / For he calls himself a Lamb.

The Lamb

London

Blake’s city is present in the dome of St Paul’s, the Thames, the chimneys, the palace walls and the charter’d streets. London, the most concentrated poem in the book, compresses a whole society, church, army, marriage and the sale of sex, into sixteen lines heard on a night walk.

The high dome of Paul’s; the charter’d Thames; black’ning Church; Palace walls; midnight streets.

In every voice, in every ban, / The mind-forg'd manacles I hear.

London

How the youthful Harlot's curse / Blasts the new born Infant's tear, / And blights with plagues the Marriage hearse.

London
