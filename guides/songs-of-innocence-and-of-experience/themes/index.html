Contrary states

Blake’s subtitle calls innocence and experience two contrary states of the human soul, and Blake prints them side by side, often as paired poems. Innocence is trusting and joyful, and also open to exploitation; experience sees through cruelty and hypocrisy, and is also soured by jealousy and fear. Neither book is the whole truth, and many poems only make full sense beside their counterparts.

Through the book The Piper and the Bard; The Lamb and The Tyger; the two Chimney Sweepers, Nurse’s Songs, Holy Thursdays and Infant songs; The Divine Image and The Human Abstract.

In the text

And I wrote my happy songs / Every child may joy to hear.

The Piper, Introduction (Innocence)

Writing the songs down is the Piper’s last act, and it is ambiguous. The songs are for every child, but to write them he has plucked a reed and stained the clear water, and the stain can be read as the first mark of experience. Blake himself wrote, etched and coloured every page.

Who Present, Past, & Future, sees; / Whose ears have heard / The Holy Word / That walk’d among the ancient trees,

Introduction (Experience)

Experience begins with a prophet, where Innocence began with a piper. The Bard has heard the voice of God walking in Eden, and he calls the fallen world to return. The shift from song to prophecy sets the tone for the second book.

Childhood

Almost every poem involves a child: playing, lost, sold, shaved, fed by charity, taught by a mother, blessed or bound. In Innocence children are protected by nurses, mothers, angels and God; in Experience the same adults become the agents of misery, and the newborn leaps into a dangerous world. Blake presents the child as a figure of vision and as a victim of the society that uses children’s labour.

Through the book Infant Joy and Infant Sorrow; Tom Dacre and the sweep in the snow; the charity children; the school boy’s cage; the little vagabond.

In the text

I have no name: / I am but two days old.

The two-day-old infant, Infant Joy

Before it can speak, the baby names itself Joy. The poem is a dialogue of blessing between mother and child. Infant Sorrow in Experience gives the same moment a different voice: a baby who leaps into a dangerous world and struggles against its bands.

And I am black, but O my soul is white; / White as an angel is the English child, / But I am black, as if bereav’d of light.

The little black boy, The Little Black Boy

Already this child has absorbed a language in which white means goodness and black means loss. Blake gives him real dignity and a real hope, and he also lets the reader hear how the speaker has been taught to see himself. Whether the poem criticises that teaching or shares it is still debated.

The Church and the priests

Innocence imagines a God who is present in mercy, pity, peace and love and who weeps with every child. Experience attacks the Church that preaches that God while children freeze: parents pray while their son sweeps, priests in black gowns bind joys and desires, Thou shalt not is written over a chapel door, and the church blackens with the sweeper’s cry. The target is institutional religion; the divine image in humankind is left standing.

Through the book The Divine Image; On Another’s Sorrow; God and his Priest and King; The Garden of Love; every black’ning Church; The Little Vagabond; A Little Boy Lost.

In the text

And these black bodies and this sun-burnt face / Is but a cloud, and like a shady grove.

The little black boy’s mother, The Little Black Boy

His mother reverses the boy’s shame: the dark body is a shelter from the sun of God’s love, which will be shed when souls can bear the heat. By the end the boy imagines protecting the English child in heaven, so the poem makes the colour of the skin a matter of how much love a soul can bear.

For Mercy has a human heart, / Pity a human face, / And Love, the human form divine, / And Peace, the human dress.

The Divine Image

In four lines Blake sets out his theology: the virtues men pray to are God, and they have a human shape. The next stanzas extend the divine image to heathen, Turk and Jew. A Divine Image, etched later and rarely printed, answers it line for line with Cruelty, Jealousy, Terror and Secrecy.

Poverty and labour

The songs name the poverty of Blake’s London: climbing boys sold to sweep chimneys, charity children paraded through St Paul’s, soldiers and harlots in the streets, a rich and fruitful land where babes are reduced to misery. The Human Abstract suggests that the virtues of pity and mercy depend on the poverty that calls them out.

Through the book So your chimneys I sweep; Holy Thursday; Pity would be no more; the hapless Soldier’s sigh; the youthful Harlot’s curse.

In the text

So your chimneys I sweep, & in soot I sleep.

Tom Dacre’s friend, The Chimney Sweeper (Innocence)

Speaking straight to the reader, the line says your chimneys: a child addresses the householders who pay for his labour, with a nursery-rhyme internal rhyme of sweep and sleep. The cheerful rhythm makes the fact harder to bear.

In a rich and fruitful land, / Babes reduc’d to misery, / Fed with cold and usurous hand?

Holy Thursday (Experience)

Experience answers the charity-children song with questions about the economics behind it. The same children are seen as the products of a wealthy country that starves them, and charity itself is called usurous, lending at interest.

Authority and the mind-forg’d manacles

Parents, priests, kings, schoolmasters and a jealous father of men all keep others bound, and Blake’s images of chains, bands, cages, charters and nets recur. In London he hears mind-forg’d manacles in every voice: the most powerful constraints are the ones people make and accept in their own minds.

Through the book Earth’s heavy chain; the swaddling bands; the caged bird of The School Boy; charter’d streets; mind-forg’d manacles; the snare of The Human Abstract.

In the text

Selfish father of men! / Cruel, jealous, selfish fear!

Earth, Earth’s Answer

Earth answers the Bard by blaming the very god he seems to speak for, a jealous father who keeps love in darkness. Blake gives her the book’s most direct indictment of a religion founded on fear.

In every voice, in every ban, / The mind-forg’d manacles I hear.

London

Repeated, the word every makes the oppression universal, and the ban may be a curse, a proclamation or a banns of marriage. The manacles are forged in the mind: people have made and accepted their own chains. It is Blake’s most quoted image of how power works.

Love and repression

Experience shows love hidden, forbidden or poisoned: a worm’s dark secret love destroys a rose, a garden of love is filled with graves, anger kept secret grows into a poison tree, and a girl who loved in the open is called a crime. Blake treats the repression of desire as the source of cruelty, and the free expression of love as holy.

Through the book The Clod and the Pebble; Earth’s free Love with bondage bound; The Sick Rose; The Garden of Love; A Poison Tree; A Little Girl Lost.

In the text

Nor for itself hath any care, / But for another gives its ease, / And builds a Heaven in Hell’s despair.

The Clod of Clay, The Clod and the Pebble

Self-sacrificing love is beautiful in the Clod’s song, and it belongs to something trodden underfoot. The Pebble’s answer uses the same rhymes for the opposite view, and the poem gives neither the last word, so the reader must hold both kinds of love in mind.

O Rose, thou art sick! / The invisible worm, / That flies in the night, / In the howling storm,

The Sick Rose

Sickness comes before any explanation, and the destroyer is invisible. In the second stanza the worm’s dark secret love destroys the rose’s crimson joy. The poem’s brevity lets it stand for sexual shame, disease, jealousy or corrupted love, and Blake does not choose among them.

Creation

The Lamb and The Tyger ask the same question from opposite sides: who made thee? In Innocence the answer comes in the next stanza: the gentle Lamb of God. In Experience the question is asked again and again, about a burning creature forged in a furnace, and the poem ends by asking whether the same maker made both. Blake leaves the answer open.

Through the book Little Lamb who made thee?; What immortal hand or eye; Did he who made the Lamb make thee?; Dare frame thy fearful symmetry.

In the text

Little Lamb who made thee? / Dost thou know who made thee?

The Lamb

A child asks the lamb a catechism question and then answers it: the maker is the Lamb of God, who became a little child. The question is simple and the answer is certain, which is the mark of Innocence. Its companion in Experience, The Tyger, asks the same question and gets no answer.

And there the lion’s ruddy eyes / Shall flow with tears of gold

Night

Even the predator weeps and guards the lamb, recalling Isaiah’s prophecy of the wolf dwelling with the lamb. Innocence can imagine violence healed as well as denied. The Tyger’s burning eyes in Experience make a sharp contrast with these golden tears.

London

Blake’s city is present in the dome of St Paul’s, the Thames, the chimneys, the palace walls and the charter’d streets. London, the most concentrated poem in the book, compresses a whole society, church, army, marriage and the sale of sex, into sixteen lines heard on a night walk.

Through the book The high dome of Paul’s; the charter’d Thames; black’ning Church; Palace walls; midnight streets.

In the text

How the youthful Harlot’s curse / Blasts the new born Infant’s tear, / And blights with plagues the Marriage hearse.

London

London ends with its fiercest compression. Prostitution and disease pass from the streets into marriage and infancy, and Marriage hearse fuses a wedding with a funeral. London’s moral economy destroys life at its beginning.
