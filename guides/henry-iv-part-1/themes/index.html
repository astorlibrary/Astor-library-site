Henry IV, Part 1 · William Shakespeare

Themes

Honour

Shakespeare sets three definitions against each other and does not arbitrate. For Hotspur honour is a thing you go and fetch from the moon or the sea floor; for Hal it is an account that can be transferred from one man’s head to another; for Falstaff it is a word, air, and no use whatever to the man who died on Wednesday.

Hotspur defines it in 1.3, Hal treats it as bookkeeping in 3.2, Falstaff takes it apart in 5.1, and in 5.4 a corpse is stabbed to claim it.

So honour cross it from the north to south, And let them grapple: O, the blood more stirs To rouse a lion than to start a hare!

Hotspur, 1.3.196

By heaven, methinks it were an easy leap, To pluck bright honour from the pale-faced moon, Or dive into the bottom of the deep, Where fathom-line could never touch the ground, And pluck up drowned honour by the locks;

Hotspur, 1.3.201

Legitimacy

Henry IV holds the throne by the method his enemies are now using against him, and everybody in the play knows it. The rebels’ grievance is practical: they helped him to the crown and feel unpaid, so the rebellion is a kind of debt collection.

The crusade that would expiate Richard’s death is postponed in 1.1, the Percys recite their part in the usurpation in 4.3, and the fighting settles nothing beyond this season.

So shaken as we are, so wan with care, Find we a time for frighted peace to pant, And breathe short-winded accents of new broils To be commenced in strands afar remote.

King Henry IV, 1.1.1

Had I so lavish of my presence been, So common-hackney'd in the eyes of men, So stale and cheap to vulgar company, Opinion, that did help me to the crown, Had still kept loyal to possession

King Henry IV, 3.2.39

Fathers and sons

The play has three fathers: the king who envies another man’s son, the knight who plays the king in a chair with a cushion for a crown, and Hotspur’s father, who stays away from the battle. In the tavern game in 2.4 Hal plays both parts, son and king, before he has to play the scene in earnest in 3.2.

Henry wishes his son exchanged in 1.1, Falstaff plays him in 2.4, and Hal is finally arraigned in earnest in 3.2 and answers with a promise about Percy.

banish plump Jack, and banish all the world.

Falstaff, 2.4.413

I do, I will.

Prince Hal, 2.4.415

Performance and counterfeiting

Almost everyone in the play is playing someone: a prince playing a wastrel, a knight playing a king, lords and knights dressed as Henry IV, a live man playing dead. The word ‘counterfeit’ does the heavy lifting: a false coin in 2.4, and in 5.4 a man in the king’s coat and, in Falstaff’s mouth, a corpse.

Hal announces the performance in 1.2, Falstaff and Hal rehearse a royal interview in 2.4, and the battle in 5.3 and 5.4 is fought against and by imitations.

I know you all, and will awhile uphold The unyoked humour of your idleness: Yet herein will I imitate the sun, Who doth permit the base contagious clouds To smother up his beauty from the world,

Prince Hal, 1.2.159

If all the year were playing holidays, To sport would be as tedious as to work; But when they seldom come, they wish'd for come, And nothing pleaseth but rare accidents.

Prince Hal, 1.2.168

Time, debt and redemption

Falstaff’s first question is what time of day it is, and the play never stops keeping accounts: reckonings in taverns, reckonings of honour, a debt owed to God, a promise to pay back time misspent. ‘Redeem’ is used as a commercial word before it is used as a moral one.

Hal promises in 1.2 to pay a debt he never promised, promises in 3.2 to redeem it on Percy’s head, and in 5.1 Falstaff will not settle with a creditor who has not called.

A country in parts

The play moves between court, tavern, inn yard, Welsh hall and rebel camp, and each has its own speech: royal blank verse, Eastcheap prose, carriers talking about oats and fleas, a song in Welsh nobody on stage translates. The kingdom being fought over is shown as a set of places that do not understand each other.

The rebels literally divide it on a map in 3.1 and argue about a river; by 5.5 the king is splitting his own armies to go north and west together.

So shaken as we are, so wan with care, Find we a time for frighted peace to pant, And breathe short-winded accents of new broils To be commenced in strands afar remote.

King Henry IV, 1.1.1

Why, so can I, or so can any man; But will they come when you do call for them?

Hotspur, 3.1.54
