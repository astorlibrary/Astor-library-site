Honour

Shakespeare sets three definitions against each other and does not arbitrate. For Hotspur honour is a thing you go and fetch from the moon or the sea floor; for Hal it is an account that can be transferred from one man’s head to another; for Falstaff it is a word, air, and no use whatever to the man who died on Wednesday.

Through the play Hotspur defines it in 1.3, Hal treats it as bookkeeping in 3.2, Falstaff takes it apart in 5.1, and in 5.4 a corpse is stabbed to claim it.

In the text

So honour cross it from the north to south, And let them grapple: O, the blood more stirs To rouse a lion than to start a hare!

Hotspur, 1.3.196

Worcester is trying to lay out a plan and Hotspur answers with hunting. The two animals are the whole of his politics: he cannot imagine an opponent worth less than a lion, and he dies partly because he never notices the hares around him, an absent father and an ally who does not arrive.

Percy is but my factor, good my lord, To engross up glorious deeds on my behalf; And I will call him to so strict account, That he shall render every glory up,

Prince Hal, 3.2.148

A factor is an agent who buys goods for someone else, so Hal casts his rival as an employee who has been accumulating stock on his account. Honour here is audited more than fought for, and the vocabulary is the tavern’s (reckonings, accounts, rendering up) applied to a battlefield.

Legitimacy

Henry IV holds the throne by the method his enemies are now using against him, and everybody in the play knows it. The rebels’ grievance is practical: they helped him to the crown and feel unpaid, so the rebellion is a kind of debt collection.

Through the play The crusade that would expiate Richard’s death is postponed in 1.1, the Percys recite their part in the usurpation in 4.3, and the fighting settles nothing beyond this season.

In the text

So shaken as we are, so wan with care, Find we a time for frighted peace to pant, And breathe short-winded accents of new broils To be commenced in strands afar remote.

King Henry IV, 1.1.1

The kingdom is described as a body out of breath, and the sentence itself is short-winded, breaking into clauses that never get to the main proposal. By the end of the scene the crusade has been cancelled, so the play’s first policy is abandoned inside fifty lines.

Had I so lavish of my presence been, So common-hackney’d in the eyes of men, So stale and cheap to vulgar company, Opinion, that did help me to the crown, Had still kept loyal to possession

King Henry IV, 3.2.39

The king’s theory of power is entirely about supply: appear rarely and you are valued, appear often and you are spent. He also lets slip the true basis of his title in a single word. ‘Opinion’ helped him to the crown, and he mentions neither God nor law: it is the most honest sentence he speaks in either part.

Fathers and sons

The play has three fathers: the king who envies another man’s son, the knight who plays the king in a chair with a cushion for a crown, and Hotspur’s father, who stays away from the battle. In the tavern game in 2.4 Hal plays both parts, son and king, before he has to play the scene in earnest in 3.2.

Through the play Henry wishes his son exchanged in 1.1, Falstaff plays him in 2.4, and Hal is finally arraigned in earnest in 3.2 and answers with a promise about Percy.

In the text

banish plump Jack, and banish all the world.

Falstaff, 2.4.413

He has manoeuvred himself into speaking the prince’s lines, so the plea for his own life comes out of Hal’s mouth by proxy. ‘All the world’ is an accurate description of what the tavern scenes contain, carriers, drawers, hostesses, thieves, none of whom survives into the reign that follows.

I do, I will.

Prince Hal, 2.4.415

Four words, two tenses, and the whole of Part 2 in the second one. The present tense keeps it inside the game, the future steps outside it, and the knocking at the door arrives immediately afterwards so that nobody on stage has to decide which he meant.

Performance and counterfeiting

Almost everyone in the play is playing someone: a prince playing a wastrel, a knight playing a king, lords and knights dressed as Henry IV, a live man playing dead. The word ‘counterfeit’ does the heavy lifting: a false coin in 2.4, and in 5.4 a man in the king’s coat and, in Falstaff’s mouth, a corpse.

Through the play Hal announces the performance in 1.2, Falstaff and Hal rehearse a royal interview in 2.4, and the battle in 5.3 and 5.4 is fought against and by imitations.

In the text

I know you all, and will awhile uphold The unyoked humour of your idleness: Yet herein will I imitate the sun, Who doth permit the base contagious clouds To smother up his beauty from the world,

Prince Hal, 1.2.159

The tense does the damage: ‘will uphold’ means he is choosing to keep company he has already judged. Putting this soliloquy in the first tavern scene means an audience watches every later joke knowing it is being kept for a purpose, which is why the play can be very funny and never entirely comfortable.

O monstrous! eleven buckram men grown out of two!

Prince Hal, 2.4.193

Hal supplies the arithmetic the audience has been doing silently, which is why the line always gets its laugh. It also shows the pattern of their friendship: Falstaff invents and Hal audits, and every one of their scenes ends with the accounts being read back to him.

Time, debt and redemption

Falstaff’s first question is what time of day it is, and the play never stops keeping accounts: reckonings in taverns, reckonings of honour, a debt owed to God, a promise to pay back time misspent. ‘Redeem’ is used as a commercial word before it is used as a moral one.

Through the play Hal promises in 1.2 to pay a debt he never promised, promises in 3.2 to redeem it on Percy’s head, and in 5.1 Falstaff will not settle with a creditor who has not called.

In the text

If all the year were playing holidays, To sport would be as tedious as to work; But when they seldom come, they wish’d for come, And nothing pleaseth but rare accidents.

Prince Hal, 1.2.168

Pleasure is explained as a matter of scarcity management, which is the theory of kingship his father will set out in 3.2 applied to a tavern. The balance of ‘playing holidays’ against ‘work’ also admits that Hal finds Eastcheap hard work, which nothing in his behaviour there suggests.

Why, thou owest God a death.

Prince Hal, 5.1.126

The commonplace is delivered as a piece of bookkeeping: a death is a debt, and debts are what this friendship runs on. Falstaff answers that it is not due yet and he would be loath to pay before the day, treating death as a bill he means to dispute, and leading straight into the catechism on honour.

A country in parts

The play moves between court, tavern, inn yard, Welsh hall and rebel camp, and each has its own speech: royal blank verse, Eastcheap prose, carriers talking about oats and fleas, a song in Welsh nobody on stage translates. The kingdom being fought over is shown as a set of places that do not understand each other.

Through the play The rebels literally divide it on a map in 3.1 and argue about a river; by 5.5 the king is splitting his own armies to go north and west together.

In the text

Why, so can I, or so can any man; But will they come when you do call for them?

Hotspur, 3.1.54

The reply does not deny magic; it wants evidence that the spirits turn up, which is a joke about rebellion as much as about sorcery. The two men are at this moment allies who have between them neither Northumberland’s army nor Glendower’s, so the question lands on the alliance itself.

The land is burning; Percy stands on high; And either we or they must lower lie.

Prince Hal, 3.3.175

Hal drops into rhyme to exit, and Falstaff’s first response is to call it rare words and order breakfast. The couplet marks the point where the prince starts speaking in the register of the campaign and the knight declines to follow him into it.
