Henry IV, Part 1 · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Prose against verse

Using the shift between metrical speech and prose to mark who is speaking and where.

Hal moves between the two at will, which is the clearest evidence in the play that his tavern life is a costume: nobody else in Eastcheap can speak the court’s language, and nobody at court can speak Falstaff’s.

A plague of all cowards, I say, and a vengeance too! marry, and amen! Give me a cup of sack, boy.

Falstaff, 2.4.103

The play extempore

Characters improvising a scene inside the scene they are already in.

In 2.4 Falstaff and Hal take turns as king and prince with a cushion for a crown, and the rehearsal predicts both the interview in 3.2 and the rejection at the end of Part 2.

banish plump Jack, and banish all the world.

Falstaff, 2.4.413

Foil structure

Building characters as deliberate answers to one another.

Hal stands between Hotspur, who has too much honour, and Falstaff, who has none, and the cutting between scenes keeps putting the three of them in the same frame.

So honour cross it from the north to south, And let them grapple: O, the blood more stirs To rouse a lion than to start a hare!

Hotspur, 1.3.196

Comic inflation

A lie or a boast that grows by measurable stages while an audience keeps count.

Two men in buckram become four, then seven, then nine, then eleven, and the joke works because Shakespeare gives the audience the true figure before Falstaff starts.

O monstrous! eleven buckram men grown out of two!

Prince Hal, 2.4.193

Soliloquy

A character alone on stage, speaking directly to the audience.

Hal’s in 1.2 is unusual for arriving too early: it hands over the whole plan before the audience has decided how to feel about him, and Falstaff’s in 5.1 answers it from the other end of the moral scale.

I know you all, and will awhile uphold The unyoked humour of your idleness: Yet herein will I imitate the sun, Who doth permit the base contagious clouds To smother up his beauty from the world,

Prince Hal, 1.2.159

Sun and eclipse imagery

A ruling image used to describe political visibility.

Hal imitates the sun behind clouds in 1.2, and his father in 3.2 describes his own rise in the same terms of rationed appearance: father and son share a metaphor without ever comparing notes.

I know you all, and will awhile uphold The unyoked humour of your idleness: Yet herein will I imitate the sun, Who doth permit the base contagious clouds To smother up his beauty from the world,

Prince Hal, 1.2.159
