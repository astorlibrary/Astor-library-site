Henry IV, Part 1 · William Shakespeare

Characters

Prince Hal

Henry, Prince of Wales, the king’s eldest son; first appears 1.2

He tells the audience his whole strategy in his first scene alone, which leaves the play free to spend four acts on the question of whether a man can perform disgrace for years without being changed by it. He is funnier than Falstaff intends him to be and colder than the tavern realises.

Sir John Falstaff

A knight, and the prince’s tavern companion; first appears 1.2

He lies on a scale that is meant to be caught, steals from the poor and from the king’s press with equal cheerfulness, and produces the only argument in the play against dying for a word. He is not a fool and he is not harmless: the hundred and fifty men he recruits for bribes are dead by Act V, and he says so himself.

Hotspur

Henry Percy, son of the Earl of Northumberland; first appears 1.3

He cannot finish a sentence about politics without making it a sentence about himself, and the speed is his charm and his defect. Shakespeare makes him Hal’s age, which the chronicles do not, so that the play can set two versions of one generation against each other.

King Henry IV

King of England, formerly Henry Bolingbroke; first appears 1.1

He opens the play trying to leave the country on crusade and never manages it in this play or the next. His advice to his son is about being seen more than about virtue (a crown is kept by rationing it), which tells an audience exactly how he got it.

Earl of Worcester

Thomas Percy, Hotspur’s uncle; first appears 1.3

He is the cold intelligence behind a hot nephew, and the one man who understands that a pardon offered to the Percys would be honoured for Hotspur and not for him. Suppressing the king’s offer is the single most consequential decision in the play, and he takes it in a short, entirely rational speech.

Owen Glendower

A Welsh leader, Mortimer’s father-in-law; first appears 3.1

He claims the earth shook at his birth and that he can call spirits, and the play neither proves nor disproves him; it simply has Hotspur laugh. He is also the one character with a household, a language and a daughter who sings, which makes the Welsh scene the play’s only picture of home.

Lady Percy

Hotspur’s wife, Mortimer’s sister; first appears 2.3

She describes her husband’s sleep (the muttered orders, the sweat, the starting awake) in the most precise account of a soldier’s mind the play contains, and he answers by refusing to tell her anything. Their scene together is affectionate, funny and entirely one-sided about information.

Ned Poins

A gentleman of Hal’s company; first appears 1.2

He invents the Gadshill counter-robbery, which is the mechanism that lets Hal watch Falstaff lie and then correct him with evidence. He is the prince’s accomplice more than his friend, and the play drops him without comment once the joke has served its purpose.

Earl of Douglas

A Scottish lord, Hotspur’s prisoner and then his ally; first appears 4.1

He spends the battle killing men dressed as the king and getting angrier each time, which makes the play’s theme of counterfeiting into a running joke with corpses in it. He is released without ransom at the end, in the only unambiguously generous act anyone performs.
