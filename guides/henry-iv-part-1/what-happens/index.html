Henry IV, Part 1 · William Shakespeare

What happens

Act I The king, the tavern and the rebels

The king postpones his crusade because Wales and Scotland are both in flames, and envies Northumberland the son he has. Hal agrees to Poins’s trick on Falstaff at Gadshill and then tells the audience, alone, that the whole tavern life is a calculated eclipse. Hotspur refuses to hand over his prisoners and the Percys begin to plan.

1.1
News from Wales and Northumberland cancels the king’s journey to Jerusalem before it starts.
1.2
Falstaff and Hal trade insults, Poins proposes the Gadshill trick, and Hal explains his plan to nobody but us.
1.3
Hotspur’s account of the popinjay on the battlefield does not save him; Worcester makes a quarrel into a conspiracy.

Act II Gadshill, and the play extempore

Carriers, chamberlains and thieves fill the road to Gadshill, and the practical joke goes exactly as Poins designed it. Back at the tavern Falstaff’s lie grows from two men to eleven while the audience counts, and then the two of them take turns playing the king so that Hal can hear his own reckoning read out in advance.

2.1
Carriers complain about fleas and the price of oats; Gadshill arranges his intelligence.
2.2
Falstaff robs the travellers and is robbed in turn by Hal and Poins in disguise.
2.3
Hotspur reads a letter of refusal aloud and will not tell his wife where he is going.
2.4
Francis is baited, the buckram men multiply, the sheriff knocks, and Falstaff is banished in play.

Act III The rebels at Bangor, and the king and Hal

At Bangor the rebels divide England on a map and quarrel about the course of a river, and Glendower and Hotspur cannot get through ten lines without a quarrel. In London the king tells his son that a crown is kept by being rarely seen, and compares him, to his face, with Richard II. Hal promises to redeem everything on Percy’s head.

3.1
Mortimer, Glendower, Worcester and Hotspur carve up the kingdom and argue over a bend in the Trent.
3.2
Henry accuses Hal of being the new Richard; Hal answers with a promise and gets a command.
3.3
Falstaff quarrels with the Hostess over a picked pocket and is given a charge of foot.

Act IV The rebellion thins out

Northumberland is sick, Glendower cannot raise his men in time, and Hotspur decides that fighting without them will look better anyway. Falstaff marches a company of scarecrows he has recruited for the bribes, and admits it. Blunt brings the king’s offer of terms, and Hotspur answers it with the Percys’ grievances.

4.1
Two allies fail to arrive, and Hotspur converts each absence into an argument for attacking.
4.2
Falstaff explains, to the audience, how he sold the men who could afford to buy their way out.
4.3
Blunt brings the king’s offer of pardon; Hotspur recites the Percys’ grievances back to the beginning.
4.4
The Archbishop sends letters, expecting the rebellion to fail and the reprisals to reach him.

Act V Shrewsbury

Worcester suppresses the king’s pardon, so the battle happens on a lie. Decoy kings are killed across the field while the real one is saved by his son. Hal kills Hotspur, speaks over him with unexpected tenderness, and then watches Falstaff claim the body and the credit.

5.1
Hal offers single combat, the king offers pardon, and Falstaff, left alone, takes honour apart word by word.
5.2
Worcester keeps the pardon from Hotspur, who hears instead how handsomely the prince praised him.
5.3
Blunt dies dressed as the king; Falstaff offers Hal a bottle of sack instead of a pistol.
5.4
Hal saves his father, kills Hotspur, and finds Falstaff playing dead and then claiming the kill.
5.5
Worcester is sent to execution, Douglas is freed, and the king divides his armies for the campaigns still to come.
