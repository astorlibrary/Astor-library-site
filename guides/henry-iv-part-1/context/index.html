Henry IV, Part 1 · William Shakespeare

Context

Dates

1364
Henry Percy, called Hotspur, is born He was in fact older than Henry IV, and old enough to be Hal’s father; Shakespeare makes the two young men coevals so that they can be compared.
1399
Henry Bolingbroke takes the crown The deposition staged in Richard II, and the debt the Percys come to collect in this play.
1400
Richard II dies in captivity The death that the crusade to Jerusalem is meant to expiate, and that the new king never gets round to atoning for.
1402
Hotspur defeats the Scots at Homildon Hill The victory whose prisoners he refuses to surrender in 1.3, which begins the quarrel.
1403
The battle of Shrewsbury Fought on 21 July, and won by the king; the single combat between Hal and Hotspur is Shakespeare’s invention.
1413
Henry IV dies and Hal becomes Henry V Two plays later, and with Falstaff still expecting to be made an earl or a duke.
1587
Holinshed’s Chronicles reissued The second edition is the play’s principal quarry for the Percy rebellion and Shrewsbury.
1597
The play is written and staged Usually dated to about this year, and probably in performance before it was entered for printing.
1598
Entered in the Stationers’ Register and printed Andrew Wise entered it on 25 February; Peter Short printed the quarto, whose title page advertises Shrewsbury, Hotspur and the humorous conceits of Sir John Falstaff.
1599
A second quarto, newly corrected The first of a string of reprints that make this the most frequently printed of Shakespeare’s plays before the Folio.
1623
The First Folio Printed as The First Part of Henry the Fourth, fixing the play inside the run from Richard II to Henry V.

Places

Eastcheap, London
The street of the Boar’s-Head, and the play’s second court.
Gad's Hill, near Rochester
The stretch of the Dover road where the robbery and the counter-robbery take place.
Warkworth Castle, Northumberland
The Percy stronghold where Hotspur reads a letter of refusal aloud and will not confide in his wife.
Bangor, Gwynedd
Where the rebels divide England on a map and argue over the course of the Trent.
Shrewsbury
The field where the play’s three worlds finally meet, on 21 July 1403.

Critics

Samuel Johnson

The Plays of William Shakespeare (1765)

Johnson’s note begins “Falstaff, unimitated, unimitable Falstaff, how shall I describe thee?” He found him a compound of sense and vice, despised and yet loved for his perpetual gaiety, and thought the moral was that no man is more dangerous than one with wit enough to please.

Maurice Morgann

An Essay on the Dramatic Character of Sir John Falstaff (1777)

Morgann argued, against the usual view, that Falstaff is not a coward. His flight at Gad’s Hill and his sham death at Shrewsbury are the sense of a man who sees no reason to die for honour, and the play makes the audience feel his courage even when the facts seem to deny it.

Stephen Greenblatt

“Invisible Bullets: Renaissance Authority and Its Subversion”, in Glyph (1981)

Greenblatt argued that Hal’s education in the taverns is a training in political manipulation, and that the play raises doubts about royal authority only to contain them: the subversion it voices ends up serving the power it questions.
