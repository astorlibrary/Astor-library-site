Carmilla · Sheridan Le Fanu

What happens

Prologue and Chapters I–III A guest at the schloss

An editor explains that the narrative comes from Doctor Hesselius’s papers and that the lady who wrote it has since died. Laura remembers waking at six to find a beautiful young woman in her bed and feeling two needles in her breast. At nineteen, on the evening her father tells her that General Spielsdorf’s ward has died, a carriage overturns at the gate, a stately lady leaves her stunned daughter in their care, and Laura recognises the girl’s face from her childhood night. Carmilla says she dreamed of Laura’s face at the same age.

Prologue
Doctor Hesselius’s note, and an editor who publishes the case for the laity.
Chapter I
The schloss, the ruined village of Karnstein, and the face by the bed.
Chapter II
The General’s distracted letter, the moon, the runaway carriage, and a mother in black velvet.
Chapter III
A woman in a turban in the carriage, a dream the two girls share, and a key turned in the lock.

Chapters IV–VI Carmilla

Carmilla will give only three facts about herself. She is languid, rarely comes down before one o’clock, and alternates girlish chatter with passionate embraces that Laura finds thrilling and repellent. She is angry when Laura joins in a funeral hymn and when a hunchbacked pedlar offers to file down her sharp tooth. A cleaned portrait of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein, dated 1698, turns out to be her exact likeness. That night Laura dreams of a monstrous black cat that springs on the bed, and wakes to see a woman’s figure at its foot.

Chapter IV
Three facts, rhapsodies of love and death, a peasant girl’s funeral, and the mountebank’s charms.
Chapter V
Pictures from Gratz, the portrait with the mole on the throat, and a moonlit declaration.
Chapter VI
Love that is always selfish, a ball long ago, and the sooty-black beast in the dark.

Chapters VII–IX Laura’s illness

Laura pins the pedlar’s charm to her pillow and begins to fail: deep sleep, an almost luxurious lassitude, dreams of a caress that fixes at the throat, then strangulation and unconsciousness. One night a voice says that her mother warns her to beware of the assassin, and she sees Carmilla bathed in blood. Carmilla vanishes from her locked room and is found at one o’clock the next day; Laura’s father explains it as sleepwalking. The doctor finds a small blue spot below Laura’s throat and orders that she never be left alone. On the road to Karnstein they meet the General.

Chapter VII
The figure in the lime-tree walk, the charm, the descent of Avernus, and the stain of blood.
Chapter VIII
An empty locked room, a morning of alarm, and a theory of sleepwalking.
Chapter IX
Two needles near the throat, a small blue spot, a delayed letter, and a drive towards Karnstein.

Chapters X–XIV The General’s story

The General, aged by grief and anger, tells how at a masked ball a countess persuaded him to take charge of her daughter Millarca, and how his ward Bertha then sickened with Laura’s symptoms. In the ruined chapel a woodman tells how a Moravian nobleman once freed the village from a vampire and removed the tomb of Mircalla. The General finishes his story: a doctor from Gratz diagnosed a vampire, and watching by Bertha’s bed he saw a black mass swell at her throat, then saw Millarca standing by the bed. Then Carmilla walks in. The General swings a woodman’s hatchet at her; she seizes his wrist and vanishes.

Chapter X
The General’s fury, a pious sacrilege, and the extinct house of Karnstein.
Chapters XI–XII
Count Carlsfeld’s masquerade, a pale man in black, a mission of life and death, and Millarca left behind.
Chapters XIII–XIV
Bertha’s decline, the Moravian nobleman, the black mass at the throat, and the hatchet in the chapel.

Chapters XV–XVI The tomb, and afterwards

Baron Vordenburg, a shabby scholar of vampirism, finds Mircalla’s lost tomb from his ancestor’s notes: that ancestor was the Moravian nobleman, who had loved her and hid her grave. Laura’s sufferings have stopped since Carmilla vanished. An Imperial Commission opens the coffin, finds the body warm and floating in blood, drives a stake through the heart, cuts off the head and burns the remains. Laura sets out what the Baron taught about the laws of vampires, and ends by admitting that Carmilla still returns to her memory in two forms, girl and fiend.

Chapter XV
The Baron’s plan of the chapel, the marble tablet, and the official report.
Chapter XVI
The Baron’s library, the anagram, a lover’s deception confessed, more than a year in Italy, and a light step at the door.
