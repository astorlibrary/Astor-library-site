Carmilla · Sheridan Le Fanu

Form and language

The Hesselius frame

A story placed inside another story or presented by an outside voice.

An unnamed editor introduces the narrative as a paper from the collection of Doctor Hesselius and declines to summarise the Doctor’s reasoning about it. The frame promises a learned explanation and withholds it, and it tells us before the story starts that the narrator has died.

involving, not improbably, some of the profoundest arcana of our dual existence, and its intermediates.

Prologue

Laura’s retrospect

An account given after the event by a narrator who already knows how it ends.

Laura writes knowing the outcome and lets it colour the telling: Heavens! If I had but known all! Her sense of time wavers, eight years since the events in chapter I and more than ten in chapter IV, and she admits that the moments of strongest feeling are the ones she remembers least clearly.

I did feel, as she said, “drawn towards her,” but there was also something of repulsion.

Chapter III

Address to a town lady

A speaker turning to address the reader, or someone within the story, directly.

Laura writes to a correspondent who lives in a town and cannot imagine her solitude: you, who live in towns; a town lady like you. Her last chapter says she wrote only because this reader asked her so often. The address makes the story a confession to a particular listener, and explains why she dwells on how lonely the schloss was.

Embedded histories

A second, older story told by a character to explain the present one.

The General’s story, begun on the drive to Karnstein and finished in the chapel, repeats Laura’s own in every detail: a stranger’s daughter left in care, languor, locked doors, dreams, needles, strangulation. Laura hears her own symptoms described as those of a girl who died, and by the time he has finished she knows who her guest is.

I have been made the dupe of a preternatural conspiracy.

General Spielsdorf, Chapter X

Doubles and anagrams

Characters or situations that mirror one another.

Carmilla, Millarca and Mircalla are one person under three arrangements of the same letters. Laura and Bertha are doubled victims, and the portrait of 1698 doubles the living guest. Carmilla’s dream of Laura mirrors Laura’s memory of her.

Twelve years ago, I saw your face in a dream, and it has haunted me ever since.

Carmilla, Chapter III

Gothic conventions

The furniture of Gothic fiction: ruins, tombs, castles, moonlight and secrets.

Le Fanu uses the full furniture of the Gothic: a schloss with a moat and a Gothic chapel, moonlight on the mist, a ruined village and a roofless church full of tombs, a portrait restored from the dark. He uses it with restraint, and sets it against a cosy household of tea, cards and governesses.

I saw a large black object, very ill-defined, crawl, as it seemed to me, over the foot of the bed, and swiftly spread itself up to the poor girl’s throat, where it swelled, in a moment, into a great, palpitating mass.

General Spielsdorf, Chapter XIV

Objects and omens

Objects that carry meaning beyond their use in the plot.

The portrait that lives, the pedlar’s charm pinned to the pillow, the key always turned in Carmilla’s lock, the hatchet that falls from the General’s hand and the Commission’s signed report each stand for a way of knowing, or of warding off, what cannot be known.

Your noble friend, the young lady at your right, has the sharpest tooth,—long, thin, pointed, like an awl, like a needle; ha, ha!

The hunchback, Chapter IV

Foreshadowing

Hints early in a story of what is to come.

Needles in the nursery, the father’s feeling that some great misfortune is hanging over them, the General’s letter about a fiend, the dying peasants, the hunchback’s remark about the sharp tooth: the reader knows long before Laura does, and the suspense lies in her not knowing.

I was wakened by a sensation as if two needles ran into my breast very deep at the same moment, and I cried loudly.

Chapter I

Shifts of register

A move from one kind of language to another, such as from the intimate to the official.

Laura’s narrative moves from the language of romance and dream to the language of the law. The last two chapters summarise an official report, cite commissions and judicial cases, and set out the vampire’s condition as a system of principles. The dry, measured prose makes the horrors seem certified.

the leaden coffin floated with blood, in which to a depth of seven inches, the body lay immersed.

Chapter XV
