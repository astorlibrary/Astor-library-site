Desire between women

Carmilla’s love for Laura is written in the language of romance: embraces, kisses, murmured declarations, breathing so fast that her dress rises and falls. Laura feels a pleasurable, tumultuous excitement mingled with fear and disgust, and describes a love growing into adoration and also into abhorrence. She even wonders whether a boyish lover is visiting in disguise. The story links the desire to predation and death, and it never pretends that the attraction is anything but real.

Through the book The first night’s embrace; You are mine, you shall be mine; I live in you; love is always selfish; the vampire’s passion resembling the passion of love; the light step at the door.

In the text

I did feel, as she said, “drawn towards her,” but there was also something of repulsion.

Chapter III

Here is the story’s emotional formula, stated at the start: attraction and repulsion together, with attraction immensely prevailing. Laura is scrupulous about naming each feeling, and her honesty makes her a convincing witness. Everything that follows keeps the two feelings alive side by side.

In the rapture of my enormous humiliation I live in your warm life, and you shall die—die, sweetly die—into mine.

Carmilla, Chapter IV

In the language of romance, Carmilla tells the literal truth. She does live on Laura’s life, and Laura is dying into hers. The humiliation she speaks of is her subjection to what she calls the irresistible law of her strength and weakness. Laura hears only a strange intensity; the reader hears a confession.

The vampire

Le Fanu’s vampire is beautiful, aristocratic and languid, with the look of healthy life: Laura, summing up the Baron’s researches, calls deathly pallor a melodramatic fiction. She must return to her grave for some hours every day, appears as a monstrous cat, passes through locked doors, is bound to anagrams of her own name, and courts a chosen victim slowly. The peasants call it the oupire. The lore comes from eighteenth-century reports and treatises, several of which Laura names from the Baron’s library.

Through the book Two needles in the breast; the sharp tooth; a portrait of 1698; the cat; the stain of blood; the coffin floating in blood; the laws of vampires in chapter XVI.

In the text

I was wakened by a sensation as if two needles ran into my breast very deep at the same moment, and I cried loudly.

Chapter I

Laura’s first wound comes years before her first meeting with Carmilla. A child is comforted, embraced and then pierced, and the servants find no mark and fetch a priest to pray. Two needles return in the dream of the cat, in the doctor’s questions and in Bertha’s illness, so this memory, which she says was never effaced, holds the key to everything that follows.

Why you must die—everyone must die; and all are happier when they do. Come home.

Carmilla, Chapter IV

Death, for Carmilla, is simply what everyone does, and all are happier after it: the view of someone who has been dead a long time. The hymn hurts her ears, and her fit on the bench afterwards is the first sign Laura sees of the delicate health her mother spoke of, and of what she really is.

Isolation

From the castle gate the forest runs fifteen miles one way and twelve the other, the nearest inhabited village is seven miles off, and Laura’s friends of her own age are only occasional visitors. Her loneliness is why she begs her father to keep the stranger, why the General’s cancelled visit hurts, and why Carmilla’s fondness flatters her. Solitude is the vampire’s opportunity, and years afterwards Laura still finds solitude insupportably terrific.

Through the book A small income goes a great way; you, who live in towns; a guest; a father quite out of hearing at the far side of the house; more than a year in Italy.

In the text

The fiend who betrayed our infatuated hospitality has done it all.

General Spielsdorf, Chapter II

Laura and her father take the letter for the ravings of grief, and on that same evening they open their own door to a stranger. Infatuated hospitality is exactly what they are about to practise. The General’s self-reproach for his conceited incredulity warns the reader to distrust the father’s calm good sense.

Testimony and proof

The story comes to us as a document: a paper from a doctor’s collection, written with conscientious particularity, containing a general’s eyewitness account, a woodman’s tradition, an ancestor’s written confession and the report of an Imperial Commission signed by all present. Laura argues that human testimony gathered judicially makes the vampire difficult to doubt. The piling up of evidence is how Le Fanu makes the supernatural credible to a sceptical reader.

Through the book Hesselius’s note; the General’s letter; the Gratz doctor’s letter, to be opened before a priest; the Moravian’s journals; the report of the Imperial Commission; the Baron’s digest of judicial cases.

In the text

involving, not improbably, some of the profoundest arcana of our dual existence, and its intermediates.

Prologue

Hesselius’s phrase promises a learned explanation, and the editor then declines to give it. The words are grand and vague, suggesting that vampirism belongs to a borderland between body and spirit. Le Fanu hints at a scientific account of the supernatural and leaves the reader alone with the narrative.

I have been made the dupe of a preternatural conspiracy.

General Spielsdorf, Chapter X

A soldier who requires proof uses the language of a court martial for the supernatural. Laura’s father glances at him with suspicion of his sanity, the same suspicion the General once felt about the doctor from Gratz. The sceptics stay in play until the evidence overwhelms them.

Superstition

Laura’s father explains everything by natural causes: the peasants infect one another with their superstitions, Carmilla walked in her sleep, and a doctor who hints at something else is teased about hippogriffs and dragons. Carmilla herself talks like a materialist. All things proceed from Nature, she says, and the charm works because it is simply natural. The story then proves the superstition true, and the father’s rationalism, like the General’s former incredulity, is what gives the vampire her chance.

Through the book The charm against the oupire; strictly referable to natural causes; hippogriffs and dragons; my conceited incredulity; the ancient and well-attested belief of the country.

In the text

And this disease that invades the country is natural. Nature. All things proceed from Nature—don’t they?

Carmilla, Chapter IV

Le Fanu puts the age’s rationalism into the mouth of its oldest superstition. Carmilla scorns the Creator, which fits what she is, and agrees with the father that the epidemic is natural, which is the belief that protects her.

If human testimony, taken with every care and solemnity, judicially, before commissions innumerable … is worth anything, it is difficult to deny, or even to doubt the existence of such a phenomenon as the Vampire.

Chapter XV

Like a lawyer, Laura appeals to the number and solemnity of the witnesses. The vampire panics of the eighteenth-century Habsburg lands really did produce official reports, and the argument makes the tale feel documented. It also shows how testimony can prove almost anything if enough solemn men agree.

Memory

Laura writes years after the events, and she warns that the scenes in which our passions were most wildly roused are the ones most dimly remembered. The story begins with a memory from the age of six that was never effaced, and ends with her admitting that Carmilla still returns to her memory in two forms, the playful girl and the writhing fiend. What the vampire leaves behind is a memory that will not settle.

Through the book The face at six; Eight years have passed; after an interval of more than ten years; the portrait; ambiguous alternations; a light step at the drawing-room door.

In the text

Twelve years ago, I saw your face in a dream, and it has haunted me ever since.

Carmilla, Chapter III

Carmilla answers Laura’s horror with a romance of destiny. She claims to have dreamed the same night from the other side, and her account matches Laura’s in every detail, down to the room and the scream. Her strange fixed smile of recognition suggests she remembers more than a dream, and she goes on to claim a right to Laura’s intimacy.

to this hour the image of Carmilla returns to memory with ambiguous alternations—sometimes the playful, languid, beautiful girl; sometimes the writhing fiend I saw in the ruined church

Chapter XVI

Nothing here ends in relief. Laura cannot settle which Carmilla was real, the girl she loved or the fiend in the chapel, and she still starts from reveries fancying she hears Carmilla’s light step at the door. The last image is of desire and fear alive in memory long after the stake.

Ancestry

The Karnsteins were a bad family whose blood-stained annals were written in their castle, and Laura’s mother was descended from them. The portrait of Mircalla comes to the schloss among the pictures inherited through her mother, Carmilla claims the same long descent, and the Baron who ends the story bears the title of the man who loved Mircalla and hid her. The past of great families does not stay buried here.

Through the book The ruined village; Marcia Karnstein on the picture list; I am descended from the Karnsteins; the Moravian nobleman’s confession; the heir of Vordenburg.

In the text

First.—Her name was Carmilla. Second.—Her family was very ancient and noble. Third.—Her home lay in the direction of the west.

Chapter IV

Each fact is true, and each is a joke at Laura’s expense. Her name is an anagram of her real one, her family is so ancient that it is extinct, and west is the direction of the Karnstein ruins, three miles away. Laura sets the facts out like numbered evidence, and it is evidence she cannot yet read.

I mean to unearth some of those fine people. I hope, by God’s blessing, to accomplish a pious sacrilege here

General Spielsdorf, Chapter X

Pious sacrilege is the story’s oxymoron for the vampire hunt: opening graves in God’s name. The General’s fierce joke about fine people aims his anger at the aristocracy of the dead as well as at one vampire. The Commission in chapter XV will carry out his sacrilege with official signatures.
