Carmilla · Sheridan Le Fanu

Themes

Desire between women

Carmilla’s love for Laura is written in the language of romance: embraces, kisses, murmured declarations, breathing so fast that her dress rises and falls. Laura feels a pleasurable, tumultuous excitement mingled with fear and disgust, and describes a love growing into adoration and also into abhorrence. She even wonders whether a boyish lover is visiting in disguise. The story links the desire to predation and death, and it never pretends that the attraction is anything but real.

The first night’s embrace; You are mine, you shall be mine; I live in you; love is always selfish; the vampire’s passion resembling the passion of love; the light step at the door.

Twelve years ago, I saw your face in a dream, and it has haunted me ever since.

Carmilla, Chapter III

I did feel, as she said, “drawn towards her,” but there was also something of repulsion.

Chapter III

The vampire

Le Fanu’s vampire is beautiful, aristocratic and languid, with the look of healthy life: Laura, summing up the Baron’s researches, calls deathly pallor a melodramatic fiction. She must return to her grave for some hours every day, appears as a monstrous cat, passes through locked doors, is bound to anagrams of her own name, and courts a chosen victim slowly. The peasants call it the oupire. The lore comes from eighteenth-century reports and treatises, several of which Laura names from the Baron’s library.

Two needles in the breast; the sharp tooth; a portrait of 1698; the cat; the stain of blood; the coffin floating in blood; the laws of vampires in chapter XVI.

I was wakened by a sensation as if two needles ran into my breast very deep at the same moment, and I cried loudly.

Chapter I

In the rapture of my enormous humiliation I live in your warm life, and you shall die—die, sweetly die—into mine.

Carmilla, Chapter IV

Isolation

From the castle gate the forest runs fifteen miles one way and twelve the other, the nearest inhabited village is seven miles off, and Laura’s friends of her own age are only occasional visitors. Her loneliness is why she begs her father to keep the stranger, why the General’s cancelled visit hurts, and why Carmilla’s fondness flatters her. Solitude is the vampire’s opportunity, and years afterwards Laura still finds solitude insupportably terrific.

A small income goes a great way; you, who live in towns; a guest; a father quite out of hearing at the far side of the house; more than a year in Italy.

The fiend who betrayed our infatuated hospitality has done it all.

General Spielsdorf, Chapter II

Testimony and proof

The story comes to us as a document: a paper from a doctor’s collection, written with conscientious particularity, containing a general’s eyewitness account, a woodman’s tradition, an ancestor’s written confession and the report of an Imperial Commission signed by all present. Laura argues that human testimony gathered judicially makes the vampire difficult to doubt. The piling up of evidence is how Le Fanu makes the supernatural credible to a sceptical reader.

Hesselius’s note; the General’s letter; the Gratz doctor’s letter, to be opened before a priest; the Moravian’s journals; the report of the Imperial Commission; the Baron’s digest of judicial cases.

involving, not improbably, some of the profoundest arcana of our dual existence, and its intermediates.

Prologue

First.—Her name was Carmilla. Second.—Her family was very ancient and noble. Third.—Her home lay in the direction of the west.

Chapter IV

Superstition

Laura’s father explains everything by natural causes: the peasants infect one another with their superstitions, Carmilla walked in her sleep, and a doctor who hints at something else is teased about hippogriffs and dragons. Carmilla herself talks like a materialist. All things proceed from Nature, she says, and the charm works because it is simply natural. The story then proves the superstition true, and the father’s rationalism, like the General’s former incredulity, is what gives the vampire her chance.

The charm against the oupire; strictly referable to natural causes; hippogriffs and dragons; my conceited incredulity; the ancient and well-attested belief of the country.

The fiend who betrayed our infatuated hospitality has done it all.

General Spielsdorf, Chapter II

Your noble friend, the young lady at your right, has the sharpest tooth,—long, thin, pointed, like an awl, like a needle; ha, ha!

The hunchback, Chapter IV

Memory

Laura writes years after the events, and she warns that the scenes in which our passions were most wildly roused are the ones most dimly remembered. The story begins with a memory from the age of six that was never effaced, and ends with her admitting that Carmilla still returns to her memory in two forms, the playful girl and the writhing fiend. What the vampire leaves behind is a memory that will not settle.

The face at six; Eight years have passed; after an interval of more than ten years; the portrait; ambiguous alternations; a light step at the drawing-room door.

I was wakened by a sensation as if two needles ran into my breast very deep at the same moment, and I cried loudly.

Chapter I

Twelve years ago, I saw your face in a dream, and it has haunted me ever since.

Carmilla, Chapter III

Ancestry

The Karnsteins were a bad family whose blood-stained annals were written in their castle, and Laura’s mother was descended from them. The portrait of Mircalla comes to the schloss among the pictures inherited through her mother, Carmilla claims the same long descent, and the Baron who ends the story bears the title of the man who loved Mircalla and hid her. The past of great families does not stay buried here.

The ruined village; Marcia Karnstein on the picture list; I am descended from the Karnsteins; the Moravian nobleman’s confession; the heir of Vordenburg.

First.—Her name was Carmilla. Second.—Her family was very ancient and noble. Third.—Her home lay in the direction of the west.

Chapter IV

“You are mine, you shall be mine, you and I are one for ever.”

Carmilla, Chapter IV
