Jealousy

Shakespeare had already written the version with a tempter; here he removes him. Leontes supplies his own evidence, his own interpretation and his own verdict inside a single speech, and the play shows the process in the grammar: clauses that start over, brackets inside brackets, words that turn obscene as he handles them.

Through the play It arrives fully formed in 1.2, hardens into law in 2.1 and 2.3, and survives even the oracle in 3.2 until a child’s death breaks it in a single line.

In the text

Too hot, too hot! To mingle friendship far is mingling bloods. I have tremor cordis on me: my heart dances; But not for joy; not joy.

Leontes, 1.2.107

This is the whole onset of the jealousy, and it occupies four lines. ‘Tremor cordis’ is a physician’s term, so he reaches for a diagnosis before he reaches for an accusation; the repetitions (too hot, mingle and mingling, not for joy, not joy) are a man arguing with something already decided.

There may be in the cup A spider steep’d, and one may drink, depart, And yet partake no venom, for his knowledge Is not infected: but if one present The abhorr’d ingredient to his eye, make known How he hath drunk, he cracks his gorge, his sides, With violent hefts.

Leontes, 2.1.39

It is a brilliant image and it proves nothing whatever: the argument is that knowing makes the poison work, which conveniently means his own conviction is itself the evidence. He ends the sentence with ‘I have drunk, and seen the spider’, having drunk nothing and seen only two men leaving in a hurry.

Time

Halfway through, Time comes on as a chorus, turns an hourglass and moves the action on sixteen years, and everything after that is about what can and cannot be recovered. The daughter comes back and the wife comes back; the son does not, and neither does Antigonus.

Through the play A boy’s unfinished tale in 2.1, the leap of Act IV, and in 5.3 a statue deliberately carved with sixteen years of age on it so that the time cannot be pretended away.

In the text

A sad tale’s best for winter: I have one Of sprites and goblins.

Mamillius, 2.1.25

The play takes its title from a child’s story and then interrupts it for good: he gets two lines further before his father walks in. The phrase comes here, early in Act II, so the title belongs to the first three acts, before the play reaches the summer of the sheep-shearing.

So much the more our carver’s excellence; Which lets go by some sixteen years and makes her As she lived now.

Paulina, 5.3.30

The wrinkles are the play’s proof that the reconciliation is not a reversal: the sixteen years are carved into the object everyone is looking at. It is also a very good lie, and a sculptor who aged his subject would be doing something no sculptor does.

Art and nature

The argument is conducted explicitly over grafted flowers in 4.4 and then acted out in the last scene, where a woman is presented as a painted stone. Perdita will not have improved carnations; Paulina asks a court to accept a work of art that breathes.

Through the play Polixenes argues that the art itself is nature and immediately contradicts himself in practice; the statue scene never says which it is.

In the text

You see, sweet maid, we marry A gentler scion to the wildest stock, And make conceive a bark of baser kind By bud of nobler race: this is an art Which does mend nature, change it rather, but The art itself is nature.

Polixenes, 4.4.93

The argument is that human art is one of nature’s own methods, and it is unanswerable: Perdita simply says ‘So it is’ and then will not do it anyway. The joke is savage: within forty lines the same speaker will forbid his noble son to marry a baser stock and threaten the girl with death.

Chide me, dear stone, that I may say indeed Thou art Hermione; or rather, thou art she In thy not chiding, for she was as tender As infancy and grace.

Leontes, 5.3.24

He asks the statue to scold him and then decides its silence is more like her, which lets him have the rebuke and the forgiveness together. Everything he says in this scene is addressed to an object, and the audience has been given no more information than he has.

Faith, oracles and wonder

An oracle is read out, believed by everyone except the one man who sent for it, and vindicated by a death within the minute. In the last scene nothing supernatural is claimed and everything depends on whether the onlookers will do what Paulina asks and awake their faith.

Through the play Apollo in Act III speaks and is denied; in Act V there is no god at all, only music, a curtain and an instruction.

In the text

Hermione is chaste; Polixenes blameless; Camillo a true subject; Leontes a jealous tyrant; his innocent babe truly begotten; and the king shall live without an heir, if that which is lost be not found.

An Officer, 3.2.130

Five flat verdicts and then one condition, and the condition is the plot of the last two acts. Everything before the final clause is in the present tense and settled; the play’s whole second half hangs on the word ‘if’, which is the only uncertainty Apollo allows.

It is required You do awake your faith. Then all stand still; On: those that think it is unlawful business I am about, let them depart.

Paulina, 5.3.94

The instruction is given to the people on stage and, unavoidably, to the people watching them: a theatre audience is always being asked to do this, and here the request is made out loud. ‘Required’ is the hardest word in the sentence, and anyone unwilling is offered the door.

Women

Hermione argues for her honour in a court that has already convicted her; Paulina tells a king he is a tyrant and then runs his conscience for sixteen years; Perdita rejects a king’s argument about breeding to his face. The men in this play give orders and the women decide what will actually happen.

Through the play Hermione’s defence in 3.2, Paulina’s denunciation later in the same scene, and in 5.3 a woman directing every other character on stage, including two kings.

In the text

if powers divine Behold our human actions, as they do, I doubt not then but innocence shall make False accusation blush and tyranny Tremble at patience.

Hermione, 3.2.26

She makes blushing and trembling the business of the accusers, which reverses the whole physical vocabulary of a trial. The conditional at the start (‘if powers divine behold’) is the risk she is taking, and within a hundred lines Apollo answers it, and Leontes rejects the answer.

You gods, look down And from your sacred vials pour your graces Upon my daughter’s head!

Hermione, 5.3.121

She speaks to the gods, then to her daughter, and never once to her husband: the reunion the play has been building towards turns out to be with the child. Her only explanation is that she preserved herself because the oracle gave hope the baby was alive, which makes the marriage a piece of unfinished business the play leaves alone.

Court and country, tragedy and comedy

The play is built in two unequal halves that do not blend: a claustrophobic court where a man watches his wife’s hands, and an open field with a pedlar, a dance and a cheat. The famous impossible bear stands exactly between them.

Through the play Three acts of tragedy, an exit pursued by a bear, and a shepherd’s remark about things dying and things newborn, which is the play describing its own form.

In the text

The day frowns more and more: thou’rt like to have A lullaby too rough: I never saw The heavens so dim by day. A savage clamour! Well may I get aboard! This is the chase: I am gone for ever.

Antigonus, 3.3.53

‘A lullaby too rough’ is addressed to an infant he is abandoning in a storm, and it is the gentlest thing said in three acts. The last four words are a cry of terror and also a piece of plain information, and the stage direction that follows them is the most famous in the language.

My father named me Autolycus; who being, as I am, littered under Mercury, was likewise a snapper-up of unconsidered trifles.

Autolycus, 4.3.23

Mercury was the god of thieves and of eloquence, and Autolycus in Homer was his son; the name is a joke for anyone who has it and a good name for a pedlar for anyone who has not. ‘Littered’ is a word for animals, and he applies it to himself without complaint.
