The Winter's Tale · William Shakespeare

Themes

Jealousy

Shakespeare had already written the version with a tempter; here he removes him. Leontes supplies his own evidence, his own interpretation and his own verdict inside a single speech, and the play shows the process in the grammar: clauses that start over, brackets inside brackets, words that turn obscene as he handles them.

It arrives fully formed in 1.2, hardens into law in 2.1 and 2.3, and survives even the oracle in 3.2 until a child’s death breaks it in a single line.

Too hot, too hot! To mingle friendship far is mingling bloods. I have tremor cordis on me: my heart dances; But not for joy; not joy.

Leontes, 1.2.107

But to be paddling palms and pinching fingers, As now they are, and making practised smiles, As in a looking-glass, and then to sigh, as 'twere The mort o' the deer

Leontes, 1.2.114

Time

The play’s hinge is a chorus who turns an hourglass and moves the action on sixteen years, and everything after that is about what can and cannot be recovered. The daughter comes back and the wife comes back; the son does not, and neither does Antigonus.

A boy’s unfinished tale in 2.1, the leap of Act IV, and in 5.3 a statue deliberately carved with sixteen years of age on it so that the time cannot be pretended away.

A sad tale's best for winter: I have one Of sprites and goblins.

Mamillius, 2.1.25

Hermione is chaste; Polixenes blameless; Camillo a true subject; Leontes a jealous tyrant; his innocent babe truly begotten; and the king shall live without an heir, if that which is lost be not found.

An Officer, 3.2.130

Art and nature

The argument is conducted explicitly over grafted flowers in 4.4 and then acted out in the last scene, where a woman is presented as a painted stone. Perdita will not have improved carnations; Paulina asks a court to accept a work of art that breathes.

Polixenes argues that the art itself is nature and immediately contradicts himself in practice; the statue scene never says which it is.

You see, sweet maid, we marry A gentler scion to the wildest stock, And make conceive a bark of baser kind By bud of nobler race: this is an art Which does mend nature, change it rather, but The art itself is nature.

Polixenes, 4.4.93

O Proserpina, For the flowers now, that frighted thou let'st fall From Dis's waggon! daffodils, That come before the swallow dares, and take The winds of March with beauty

Perdita, 4.4.117

Faith, oracles and wonder

An oracle is read out, believed by everyone except the one man who sent for it, and vindicated by a death within the minute. In the last scene nothing supernatural is claimed and everything depends on whether the onlookers will do what Paulina asks and awake their faith.

Apollo in Act III speaks and is denied; in Act V there is no god at all, only music, a curtain and an instruction.

if powers divine Behold our human actions, as they do, I doubt not then but innocence shall make False accusation blush and tyranny Tremble at patience.

Hermione, 3.2.26

Women

Hermione argues for her honour in a court that has already convicted her; Paulina tells a king he is a tyrant and then runs his conscience for sixteen years; Perdita rejects a king’s argument about breeding to his face. The men in this play give orders and the women decide what will actually happen.

Hermione’s defence in 3.2, Paulina’s denunciation later in the same scene, and in 5.3 a woman directing every other character on stage, including two kings.

You speak a language that I understand not: My life stands in the level of your dreams, Which I'll lay down.

Hermione, 3.2.78

if powers divine Behold our human actions, as they do, I doubt not then but innocence shall make False accusation blush and tyranny Tremble at patience.

Hermione, 3.2.26

Court and country, tragedy and comedy

The play is built in two unequal halves that do not blend: a claustrophobic court where a man watches his wife’s hands, and an open field with a pedlar, a dance and a cheat. The famous impossible bear stands exactly between them.

Three acts of tragedy, an exit pursued by a bear, and a shepherd’s remark about things dying and things newborn, which is the play describing its own form.

A sad tale's best for winter: I have one Of sprites and goblins.

Mamillius, 2.1.25

The day frowns more and more: thou'rt like to have A lullaby too rough: I never saw The heavens so dim by day. A savage clamour! Well may I get aboard! This is the chase: I am gone for ever.

Antigonus, 3.3.53
