The Winter's Tale · William Shakespeare

Context

Dates

1588
Robert Greene’s Pandosto published The prose romance Shakespeare follows closely, and whose jealous king kills himself at the end.
1592
Greene dies, having attacked Shakespeare in print The pamphlet that calls him an upstart crow appeared in the same year; twenty years later Shakespeare took Greene’s plot.
1610
The Winter’s Tale most likely written Usually dated 1609–11, among the late plays and close to Cymbeline and The Tempest.
1611
Simon Forman sees the play at the Globe His notebook entry of 15 May records the plot and, at length, Autolycus, but not the bear or the statue; the moral he draws is to beware of trusting feigned beggars.
1613
Played at court for Princess Elizabeth’s wedding festivities The King’s Men gave a long series of plays for the marriage of James’s daughter, and this was among them.
1614
Jonson mocks plays of this kind in Bartholomew Fair The induction to Bartholomew Fair jeers at plays full of servant-monsters and drolleries: a contemporary judgement on exactly this kind of writing.
1623
First Folio The only early text, printed last among the comedies and generally thought to have been set from a scribe’s transcript.
1756
Garrick’s Florizel and Perdita An adaptation that kept the pastoral and cut most of the first three acts, and held the stage for decades.
1802
The full play returns to the London stage The nineteenth century restored the tragic first half, and the statue scene became one of the great set pieces of the English theatre.

Places

Sicily
Leontes’s court, the trial, and the chapel in Paulina’s house.
Bohemia
Polixenes’s kingdom and the sheep-shearing, given a sea-coast that Ben Jonson mocked, since Bohemia is a hundred miles from any sea.
Delphi
The play calls it Delphos and makes it an island; Cleomenes and Dion sail there for the oracle.
Delos
The Aegean island sacred to Apollo, routinely confused with Delphi in Shakespeare’s day and the reason the ambassadors go by sea.
The Globe, Bankside
Where Simon Forman saw the play in May 1611 and wrote down what he made of it.

Critics

Simon Forman

The Booke of Plaies (1611)

Forman saw the play at the Globe on 15 May 1611 and wrote a summary in his notebook, the earliest account of it. He did not mention the statue scene, and drew a moral from Autolycus: beware of trusting feigned beggars or fawning fellows.

Ben Jonson

Conversations with William Drummond (1619)

Jonson told Drummond that Shakespeare had brought shipwrecked men ashore in Bohemia, which has no sea within about a hundred miles, the first recorded complaint about the play’s geography.
