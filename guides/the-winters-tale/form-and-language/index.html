The Winter's Tale · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Broken syntax

Sentences that fracture, restart or bury themselves in parenthesis to show a mind under strain.

Leontes’s speeches in 1.2 and 2.1 are among the hardest passages Shakespeare wrote, and the difficulty is deliberate: the audience has to work out the thought at the same speed he is failing to.

Too hot, too hot! To mingle friendship far is mingling bloods. I have tremor cordis on me: my heart dances; But not for joy; not joy.

Leontes, 1.2.107

The Chorus

A figure outside the action who addresses the audience and moves it through time or space.

Time appears at the start of Act IV to turn his glass and skip sixteen years, apologising for the liberty: a device Sidney had mocked in print, used here without embarrassment.

The reported scene

A major event described by witnesses who saw it offstage.

The reunion of Leontes and Perdita, the scene any other play would build to, is handed to three gentlemen in 5.2, so that the statue can be the only wonder the audience actually sees.

That she is living, Were it but told you, should be hooted at Like an old tale: but it appears she lives, Though yet she speak not.

Paulina, 5.3.115

Festival and song

A set-piece of country celebration, with dance, ballads and the giving of flowers.

The sheep-shearing in 4.4 is one of the longest scenes in Shakespeare, and it is built of ballads, a pedlar’s pack and a flower speech that turns into a lament halfway through.

My father named me Autolycus; who being, as I am, littered under Mercury, was likewise a snapper-up of unconsidered trifles.

Autolycus, 4.3.23

The statue scene

A piece of staging held long enough to become the play’s argument.

A woman standing motionless, once the curtain is drawn, for some eighty lines while a court talks about her: the last scene works because nobody is allowed to touch her until she moves.

when you do dance, I wish you A wave o' the sea, that you might ever do Nothing but that; move still, still so, And own no other function

Florizel, 4.4.141

Tragicomic structure

A play that goes as far as tragedy will take it and then turns.

Two deaths and an abandonment in Act III are followed by a stage direction about a bear and a scene of comic misunderstanding; the join is left visible on purpose.

The day frowns more and more: thou'rt like to have A lullaby too rough: I never saw The heavens so dim by day. A savage clamour! Well may I get aboard! This is the chase: I am gone for ever.

Antigonus, 3.3.53
