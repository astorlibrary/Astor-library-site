The Winter's Tale · William Shakespeare

Characters

Leontes

King of Sicilia; first appears 1.2

His jealousy has no source outside himself, and the play makes that a formal problem: the verse breaks apart into half-lines, parentheses and obscene punning within seconds of the first suspicion. He is given the longest penance in Shakespeare and the play never says outright that it has been enough.

Hermione

Queen of Sicilia; first appears 1.2

She is witty and entirely at ease in the first scene, which is what condemns her, and at the trial she argues from honour, because innocence has already been ruled inadmissible. After the trial she speaks fewer than ten lines, and none of them to her husband.

Paulina

A lady of the Sicilian court, Antigonus’s wife; first appears 2.2

She says the unsayable to a king in Act III and then organises the next sixteen years of his life. Whether she hid a living woman for that long or performed a miracle is left deliberately unresolved, and she loses a husband to a bear and is handed a new one in the last thirty lines.

Perdita

Leontes and Hermione’s lost daughter, raised as a shepherd’s child; first appears 4.4

She presides over the sheep-shearing in borrowed finery and is uncomfortable in it, and she holds her own in an argument about grafting against a king. The play is careful that her authority looks natural without ever settling whether that is breeding, upbringing or the pastoral convention doing its work.

Polixenes

King of Bohemia; first appears 1.2

He begins as the innocent party and ends the fourth act threatening to have a shepherd’s daughter’s face scratched with briars. The play gives him the elegant argument that art is itself a part of nature, and then has him forbid exactly the kind of grafting he has just defended.

Camillo

A Sicilian lord, later Polixenes’s counsellor; first appears 1.1

Ordered to commit a murder, he warns the victim and leaves with him, which is the one action in the first half that prevents a death. He then spends sixteen years homesick and engineers the escape to Sicilia largely because he wants to see the place again.

Florizel

Prince of Bohemia, courting as Doricles; first appears 4.4

He is in the same disguise Rosalind and Viola wear, and he uses it only to be poorer than he is. His praise of Perdita dancing is the most beautiful speech in the play and also, read carefully, a wish that she would never do anything else.

Autolycus

A pedlar, ballad-seller and pickpocket; first appears 4.3

He arrives after the interval of sixteen years and changes the play’s weather entirely, singing, cheating and selling printed ballads about impossible events to people who buy them because they are printed. He profits from the happy ending without ever meaning to help it.

Mamillius

The young prince of Sicilia; first appears 1.2

He is beginning a ghost story for his mother (“A sad tale’s best for winter”) when the guards come in, and the play takes its title from the tale he never finishes. He dies of what a servant calls mere conceit and fear, and nothing in the last act replaces him.

Antigonus

A Sicilian lord, Paulina’s husband; first appears 2.1

He argues for the queen, is bullied into swearing an oath, and then keeps it, which is the play’s clearest illustration of how obedience does the damage that tyranny orders. His death is the most famous stage direction in the language, and his last line is one of the quietest.
