Paradise Lost · John Milton

Characters

Satan

Leader of the rebel angels, formerly Lucifer; first appears Book I

Perhaps the most persuasive speaker in English poetry and the one the poem most wants you to distrust. He is magnificent in Books I and II, cannot stop talking to himself in Book IV, and by Book IX has agreed to be squeezed into a snake: a career the poem records as a steady shrinking.

Eve

The first woman; first appears Book IV

She is given the poem’s best argument (that an untested virtue is no virtue at all), and the poem never answers it; it only shows what happens next. She also speaks its tenderest lines and makes the first move towards reconciliation after the Fall, while Adam is still composing his complaint.

Adam

The first man; first appears Book IV

An archangel tells him the whole truth in advance and he falls anyway, because he will not live without her; ignorance has nothing to do with it. Milton is careful about this: Adam is not deceived, which makes his eating the more deliberate act and the harder one to condemn cleanly.

God the Father

Creator, and the poem’s defendant; first appears Book III

Milton puts him on the stand in Book III and has him argue his own case: the angels and man were made free, freedom that cannot fail is not freedom, and therefore the blame is not his. Alexander Pope complained that in the poem ‘God the Father turns a School-Divine’, and the voice is cold: the price of making an argument instead of an atmosphere.

The Son

The Father’s agent in war, creation, judgement and mercy; first appears Book III

He does everything in the poem that actually takes effect: he ends the war alone, speaks the world into being, passes sentence in Eden and then kneels down to clothe the people he has sentenced. Where Satan announces, the Son acts and says very little about it.

Raphael

The affable archangel, sent to warn Adam; first appears Book V

He comes to lunch and stays for four books, explaining the revolt, the war and the creation in terms a person without experience of either could hold. His honesty has limits he admits to, and his parting advice, “be lowly wise”, is the one instruction Adam and Eve both fail to keep.

Michael

The archangel who carries out the eviction; first appears Book VI

He leads the loyal host in the war and returns in the last two books as the poem’s historian, showing Adam the future in pictures until the pictures become unbearable and he switches to plain narration. He is the one who names the paradise within.

Abdiel

A seraph among the rebel legions; first appears Book V

Milton invents him. He hears out Satan’s case for independence, answers it in public, and then walks the whole night back through an army that despises him. For a poet who had defended a beheaded king’s executioners and then watched the monarchy return, the figure of the lone dissenter was not an abstraction.

Beelzebub

Satan’s second, and the voice of the winning motion; first appears Book I

He speaks last in the debate in Hell and proposes the plan Satan wants, which the assembly then adopts as its own idea. The manoeuvre is the most recognisable piece of politics in the poem: the leader does not have to argue for his policy if the right subordinate does.

Sin and Death

The keepers of Hell’s gate; first appears Book II

Half allegory and half family, they are Satan’s daughter and his son by her, and they guard the door he needs opened. Once the Fall has happened they build a bridge from Hell to earth, and a moral idea becomes a piece of civil engineering.
