Paradise Lost · John Milton

What happens

Book I The fallen angels, and Pandemonium

The fallen angels come to on a burning lake, and Satan makes the worst news imaginable into a programme. The roll-call of the devils doubles as a catalogue of the gods later worshipped on earth, and Pandemonium rises out of the ground in the time it takes to describe it.

Book II The debate, the gates, and the flight through Chaos

Hell holds a policy meeting with four speakers and a fixed outcome, since Beelzebub is arguing Satan’s case for him. Satan then leaves alone, meets Sin, his daughter, and Death, their son, keeping the gate, and beats his way up through Chaos towards the new world.

Book III God foresees the Fall, and the Son offers himself

God sees Satan coming and states, in advance, that man will fall and that the fall will be man’s own doing. The Son volunteers to pay for it. The book opens with the poem’s most personal passage, an address to light by a man who cannot see it.

Book IV Satan in Eden

Satan reaches the garden and finds that he has brought Hell with him. What follows is mostly domestic: two people gardening, talking, going to bed, while the intruder squats beside them taking notes and the angelic guard finally moves him on.

Book V Raphael’s visit, and Abdiel

Eve wakes from a dream she did not choose, and Raphael arrives to eat fruit and explain the terms. His history of the revolt gives the poem Abdiel, the one angel who hears Satan’s argument through and walks out of the meeting.

Book VI The war in Heaven

Angels fight, invent artillery, throw hills at each other and cannot be killed, so the war cannot be won until the Son ends it alone on the third day. Force turns out to be the least interesting power in the poem.

Book VII The Creation

Raphael answers Adam’s question about how the world began. Milton’s six days are noisy, crowded and physical, full of animals shouldering their way out of the ground, and the whole account is framed as speech accommodated to a listener who could not otherwise follow it.

Book VIII Adam’s story, and Raphael’s warning

Adam asks about astronomy and is told to be lowly wise. He then describes his first minutes of consciousness, his argument with God about loneliness, and his response to Eve, at which Raphael frowns and delivers the poem’s least comfortable piece of marital advice.

Book IX The separation, and the Fall

The crisis is a disagreement about how to spend a working day. Eve wants a virtue that has been tested; Adam gives way sooner than command her; the serpent finds her alone and argues her into eating, and Adam eats knowing exactly what he is doing. The book ends in mutual accusation.

Book X Judgement, and the road built from Hell

The Son comes down to sentence them and stays to clothe them. Sin and Death pave a highway from Hell to earth, Satan returns to an ovation that turns into hissing, and Eve, who is blamed for everything, is the first to propose that they stop blaming each other.

Book XI Michael’s visions of the future

Michael takes Adam up a hill and shows him what his choice has produced: the first murder, a lazar-house of diseases, war, and a flood. Adam learns history the way a man learns of a bereavement, one instance at a time.

Book XII Michael’s narration, and leaving Eden

Michael switches from vision to narration and carries the story to the Incarnation and beyond. Adam is offered a paradise that cannot be lost because it is internal, and the poem closes on two figures walking away with the whole world to choose from.
