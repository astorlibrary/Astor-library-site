Freedom, foreknowledge and fault

The poem’s stated purpose is to justify the ways of God to men, and its argument is all about liberty: obedience that could not have been withheld would prove nothing, so the possibility of the Fall is the price of everything good in Eden. God knows what will happen and insists this is not the same as causing it.

Through the poem Set out as doctrine in Book III, dramatised as a marital argument in Book IX, and left with Adam in Book XII as a choice about how to live in a world where the worst has already happened.

In the text

I made him just and right, Sufficient to have stood, though free to fall.

God the Father, Book III

The whole defence is balanced on one line, and on the difference between ‘sufficient’ and ‘certain’. Had man been made unable to fall, the standing would have been mechanical; the poem’s God would rather be accused of cruelty than build a creature whose obedience proves nothing.

And what is faith, love, virtue, unassayed Alone, without exterior help sustained?

Eve, Book IX

This is Milton’s own position, put in the mouth of the character about to be ruined by acting on it: he had argued in print that a virtue never exposed to its opposite is worth nothing. The poem does not refute her. It simply lets the argument be correct and the morning be a disaster.

Persuasion

Almost every turn of the plot is a speech that works. Satan raises an army with one, carries a parliament with another through a proxy, and takes Eden with a third; the loyal side answers with Abdiel’s rebuttal and Raphael’s patient exposition. Milton, who wrote pamphlets for a revolution, knew how much damage a good argument can do.

Through the poem From public oratory in Books I and II, to private flattery in Book IX, to the quieter persuasion of Michael’s history, which changes Adam without once raising its voice.

In the text

What though the field be lost? All is not lost; the unconquerable will, And study of revenge, immortal hate, And courage never to submit or yield:

Book I

Read the list and ignore the tone: an unconquerable will, a course of study in revenge, and hate described as immortal. Three of the four items are heroic virtues and one is simply a refusal to stop, and the speech works on the reader exactly as it works on the legions: it sounds like resolve before it can be inspected as a programme.

So spake the Seraph Abdiel, faithful found; Among the faithless, faithful only he;

Book V

The repetition works like a count: faithful, faithless, faithful, with the tally narrowing to ‘only he’. Milton, writing after the Restoration had made his own politics unspeakable, gives his invented character the one action available to a defeated dissenter: to say so out loud and then leave.

Rule and service

Satan frames the whole conflict as a choice between reigning and serving, and the poem spends twelve books dismantling the opposition. Service in Heaven turns out to be a form of activity and delight; Satan’s reign is a monarchy of one over a wasteland, maintained by speeches to people who have no alternative.

Through the poem Announced in Book I as a preference, tested in the Hell debate, and answered in Book VI, where the Son’s single-handed victory shows what authority looks like when it does not need to be asserted.

In the text

The mind is its own place, and in itself Can make a Heaven of Hell, a Hell of Heaven.

Book I

The claim is refuted by the speaker himself three books later, when he finds that the mind being its own place is exactly the trouble. The symmetry of the line wins agreement before the sense has been checked: it is the kind of sentence that survives detached from its speaker, which is how it usually travels.

Long is the way And hard, that out of Hell leads up to light;

Satan, Book II

He is describing a route through Chaos and stating, without noticing, the moral condition of everyone listening. The line is also a piece of management: by making the errand sound impossible he ensures nobody else offers to share it, and the sole risk becomes sole credit.

Manhood and gender

The poem states a hierarchy plainly and then writes scenes that strain against it. Eve is described as made for Adam, and it is Eve who produces the argument nobody refutes, who eats first, and who ends the quarrel after the Fall. Milton’s hierarchy is doctrine; his drama keeps handing the initiative to the wrong person.

Through the poem Declared in Book IV, accepted by Eve in her own voice, contested in the separation scene of Book IX, and reversed without comment at the end of Book X.

In the text

For contemplation he and valour formed, For softness she and sweet attractive grace; He for God only, she for God in him.

Book IV

The doctrine is stated in the most memorable line available, with the parallel structure standing in for proof. Notice who is looking: the description is placed inside Satan’s view, and the poem’s own drama spends five more books declining to behave as the line prescribes.

Go; for thy stay, not free, absents thee more; Go in thy native innocence, rely On what thou hast of virtue, summon all; For God towards thee hath done his part; do thine.

Adam, Book IX

He gives way on the same principle he is worried about: keeping her by command would make her presence worthless. The last four words are the whole theology of the poem compressed into a domestic exchange, and they are said by a man who has just lost an argument he was right to be having.

Knowledge and its limits

The forbidden tree is a tree of knowledge, and the poem is full of people asking questions they are told not to press: Adam on astronomy, Eve on what the fruit might do, Satan on whether he was created at all. Raphael’s answer is that some knowledge is no use to the person who wants it; he does not call curiosity wicked.

Through the poem Raphael’s warning in Book VIII comes back in the serpent’s temptation as its exact opposite, knowledge offered as promotion, and is settled in Book XII, where what Adam needs turns out to be practice more than information.

In the text

Heaven is for thee too high To know what passes there; be lowly wise; Think only what concerns thee and thy being;

Raphael, Book VIII

‘Lowly wise’ means wise about the right things, and the proof that it does not mean ignorant is that the angel has just spent three books answering questions. The serpent will offer Adam’s wife the opposite bargain, knowledge as promotion, and this warning makes the offer recognisable when it comes.

then wilt thou not be loth To leave this Paradise, but shalt possess A Paradise within thee, happier far.

Michael, Book XII

The offer is comparative (‘happier far’), which is a startling thing to say to a man being evicted from the best place there has ever been. The list that leads up to it is all practice: deeds, faith, virtue, patience, temperance, love. The paradise on offer is a way of living, and no longer a place.

Loss and exile

Everyone in the poem has been thrown out of somewhere. The angels lose Heaven, Adam and Eve lose the garden, and Milton (blind, out of favour, on the wrong side of a restored monarchy) writes the loss from the inside. The ending gives them a version of paradise they can carry with them: “A paradise within thee, happier far”.

Through the poem Hell in Book I is exile as rage; Book IV is exile as homesickness watching other people’s happiness; Book XII makes it a journey with a direction, undertaken hand in hand.

In the text

yet from those flames No light, but rather darkness visible Served only to discover sights of woe

Book I

The phrase is a contradiction that works as a description: flames that illuminate nothing except how much there is to dread. Milton needs light in Hell so that the fallen can see each other and their situation, and needs it to be useless, so he invents a category of darkness that performs the office of light.

Which way I fly is Hell; myself am Hell; And, in the lowest deep, a lower deep Still threatening to devour me opens wide,

Satan, Book IV

This answers his boast in Book I that the mind is its own place: here the mind is the trap. The geometry is the frightening part: a lowest deep that opens on a lower one means there is no floor.
