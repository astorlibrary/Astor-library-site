Paradise Lost · John Milton

Themes

Freedom, foreknowledge and fault

The poem’s stated purpose is to justify the ways of God to men, and its argument is all about liberty: obedience that could not have been withheld would prove nothing, so the possibility of the Fall is the price of everything good in Eden. God knows what will happen and insists this is not the same as causing it.

Set out as doctrine in Book III, dramatised as a marital argument in Book IX, and left with Adam in Book XII as a choice about how to live in a world where the worst has already happened.

Persuasion

Almost every turn of the plot is a speech that works. Satan raises an army with one, carries a parliament with another through a proxy, and takes Eden with a third; the loyal side answers with Abdiel’s rebuttal and Raphael’s patient exposition. Milton, who wrote pamphlets for a revolution, knew how much damage a good argument can do.

From public oratory in Books I and II, to private flattery in Book IX, to the quieter persuasion of Michael’s history, which changes Adam without once raising its voice.

What though the field be lost? All is not lost; the unconquerable will, And study of revenge, immortal hate, And courage never to submit or yield:

Book I

Rule and service

Satan frames the whole conflict as a choice between reigning and serving, and the poem spends twelve books dismantling the opposition. Service in Heaven turns out to be a form of activity and delight; Satan’s reign is a monarchy of one over a wasteland, maintained by speeches to people who have no alternative.

Announced in Book I as a preference, tested in the Hell debate, and answered in Book VI, where the Son’s single-handed victory shows what authority looks like when it does not need to be asserted.

Manhood and gender

The poem states a hierarchy plainly and then writes scenes that strain against it. Eve is described as made for Adam, and it is Eve who produces the argument nobody refutes, who eats first, and who ends the quarrel after the Fall. Milton’s hierarchy is doctrine; his drama keeps handing the initiative to the wrong person.

Declared in Book IV, accepted by Eve in her own voice, contested in the separation scene of Book IX, and reversed without comment at the end of Book X.

For contemplation he and valour formed, For softness she and sweet attractive grace; He for God only, she for God in him.

Book IV

My author and disposer, what thou bidd’st Unargued I obey; so God ordains: God is thy law, thou mine: to know no more Is woman’s happiest knowledge, and her praise.

Eve, Book IV

Knowledge and its limits

The forbidden tree is a tree of knowledge, and the poem is full of people asking questions they are told not to press: Adam on astronomy, Eve on what the fruit might do, Satan on whether he was created at all. Raphael’s answer is that some knowledge is no use to the person who wants it; he does not call curiosity wicked.

Raphael’s warning in Book VIII comes back in the serpent’s temptation as its exact opposite, knowledge offered as promotion, and is settled in Book XII, where what Adam needs turns out to be practice more than information.

Loss and exile

Everyone in the poem has been thrown out of somewhere. The angels lose Heaven, Adam and Eve lose the garden, and Milton (blind, out of favour, on the wrong side of a restored monarchy) writes the loss from the inside. The ending gives them a version of paradise they can carry with them: “A paradise within thee, happier far”.

Hell in Book I is exile as rage; Book IV is exile as homesickness watching other people’s happiness; Book XII makes it a journey with a direction, undertaken hand in hand.
