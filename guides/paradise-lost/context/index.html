Paradise Lost · John Milton

Context

Dates

1608
John Milton born in Bread Street, London The son of a scrivener and composer, in a household that could afford tutors and music.
1638
Milton travels in Italy He later wrote of having visited the aged Galileo, then a prisoner of the Inquisition; Galileo’s telescope appears in the poem.
1644
Areopagitica published His argument against pre-publication licensing turns on the claim that a virtue never tested is no virtue, which is the position Eve takes in Book IX.
1649
Charles I executed; Milton defends the regicide in print The Tenure of Kings and Magistrates appears within weeks, and Milton is appointed Secretary for Foreign Tongues to the Council of State.
1652
Milton is completely blind From this point everything he writes is dictated, including the whole of the epic.
1660
The Restoration Milton goes into hiding, is arrested and released; his defences of the regicide are burnt by the hangman.
1665
Plague drives Milton out of London He took a cottage at Chalfont St Giles in Buckinghamshire, where work on the poem continued.
1667
Paradise Lost published in ten books Printed in London the year after the Great Fire, with a contract paying Milton five pounds down.
1671
Paradise Regained and Samson Agonistes published together The brief epic of the temptation in the wilderness, and a tragedy about a blind man among his enemies.
1674
The twelve-book second edition; Milton dies Books VII and X were each split in two and a few lines added. Milton died in November and was buried at St Giles-without-Cripplegate.
1688
Tonson’s illustrated folio The first grand edition, published by subscription, which made the poem a national monument.
1790
Blake begins The Marriage of Heaven and Hell The first of the Romantic readings that take Milton’s Satan as the poem’s real energy, a reading the poem’s structure was designed to resist.

Places

Mount Sinai
Oreb, or of Sinai: the mountain named in the opening invocation as where the shepherd Moses was first inspired.
Jerusalem
Zion hill and Siloa’s brook, offered in the invocation as the alternative source of the poet’s inspiration.
Mount Helicon
The Aonian mount of the Greek muses, which Milton announces at the outset that he intends to fly above.
Vallombrosa
The Tuscan valley whose autumn leaves supply the simile for the fallen angels lying on the lake in Book I.
Chalfont St Giles
The Buckinghamshire village where Milton took a cottage during the plague of 1665, with the poem near completion.
St Giles-without-Cripplegate, London
The church where Milton was buried in November 1674, months after the twelve-book edition appeared.

Critics

Samuel Johnson

“Milton”, in Lives of the Poets (1779)

Johnson ranked the poem with the greatest epics and praised its sublimity, but admitted that it is admired more than it is enjoyed. Readers lay it down and forget to take it up again, and none, he said, ever wished it longer.

William Blake

The Marriage of Heaven and Hell (1790–93)

Blake wrote that Milton wrote in fetters when he wrote of angels and God, and at liberty when he wrote of devils and Hell, because he was a true poet “and of the Devil’s party without knowing it”. The remark began the long argument over whether Satan is the poem’s real hero.

C. S. Lewis

A Preface to Paradise Lost (1942)

Lewis answered the Romantic admiration for Satan. He traced Satan’s decline through the poem, from hero and general to politician, spy, toad and finally snake, and argued that Milton meant him to be seen as absurd as well as terrible.

Stanley Fish

Surprised by Sin: The Reader in Paradise Lost (1967)

Fish argued that the poem draws the reader into sympathy with Satan and then corrects that sympathy, so that the reader repeats the Fall in the act of reading and learns to recognise it.
