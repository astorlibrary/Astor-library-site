Paradise Lost · John Milton

Form and language

Blank verse

Unrhymed iambic pentameter, in which the sentence and the line are free to disagree.

Milton’s sentences habitually run past the line-ending, so the verse arrives at its main verb late and the reader holds several clauses in suspense: the opening sentence runs sixteen lines and reaches its imperative, “Sing”, only in the sixth.

Of Man’s first disobedience, and the fruit Of that forbidden Tree, whose mortal taste Brought death into the world, and all our woe, With loss of Eden, till one greater Man Restore us, and regain the blissful seat

Book I

Beginning in the middle

Opening an epic after the decisive events, and supplying the earlier history later through a character’s narration.

The war in Heaven is already lost when the poem starts, and the rebels have lain nine days on the burning lake. Books V to VIII are Raphael’s visit, in which the past is filled in, so the exposition is a warning delivered to the two people it concerns.

Epic simile

An extended comparison that leaves the narrative for several lines and acquires a life of its own.

The fallen angels lie thick as autumn leaves in Vallombrosa; Satan’s shield is the moon seen through Galileo’s glass. The comparisons keep reaching for the ordinary world, which is the only one the reader has.

Invocation

A direct address to a muse or a source of inspiration, placed at the head of a book.

Four of them punctuate the poem, and they are where Milton speaks in his own person, most openly at the start of Book III, where the address to light becomes an account of blindness.

Of Man’s first disobedience, and the fruit Of that forbidden Tree, whose mortal taste Brought death into the world, and all our woe, With loss of Eden, till one greater Man Restore us, and regain the blissful seat

Book I

Catalogue

A long list of objects or names used in place of narrative.

The roll-call of devils in Book I names them by the gods they will later become in Canaan, Egypt and Greece, so the list of the defeated is also a history of idolatry to come.

Allegory

Figures who are named for what they mean, moving through a narrative alongside literal characters.

Sin and Death are abstractions with a family tree, and Milton lets them do physical work (keeping a gate, building a road), so that the machinery of the idea is visible in a poem otherwise committed to persons.

The one seemed woman to the waist, and fair, But ended foul in many a scaly fold Voluminous and vast, a serpent armed With mortal sting.
