Who speaks for the poor

Every powerful man in the book has a theory of the poor, and none of them asks a poor person anything. Filer has tables, Cute has a phrase, Sir Joseph has a paternal system and the red-faced gentleman has a lost golden age. Will Fern is the one labourer given a platform, and he uses it first to ask the gentry to leave him alone and then to ask for better homes, food and laws.

Through the book The theories are set out as talk in the first two Quarters. The vision in the third and fourth shows them applied to Meg, Lilian, Richard and Fern over years.

In the text

They hung there, in all weathers, with the wind and rain driving in upon them; facing only the outsides of all those houses; never getting any nearer to the blazing fires that gleamed and shone upon the windows

First Quarter

Every clause describes Trotty’s working day as well as the bells’: out in the weather, looking at other people’s fires through their windows. Dickens builds the likeness before any goblin appears, so that when the bells start to speak the reader already suspects they are speaking with Trotty’s voice.

You needn’t trouble yourself to think about anything. I will think for you; I know what is good for you; I am your perpetual parent.

Sir Joseph Bowley, Second Quarter

Paternalism is stated here with no disguise at all. The clauses move from thinking to knowing to being a parent, and ‘perpetual’ means that Trotty is never to grow up. A few sentences later Sir Joseph names the one lesson the poor require as ‘entire Dependence on myself’, and Trotty, listening, begins to feel more comfortable.

Born bad

Trotty’s question at noon is whether the poor are born bad or have been made to feel that they are. The gentlemen answer it for him, a newspaper story seems to confirm the answer, and the vision exists to reverse it: Meg, the best person he knows, is driven towards the act he called unnatural that evening.

Through the book A doubt in the First Quarter, a conviction by the end of the Second, and a retraction at the river, where Trotty pleads that the crime has sprung from ‘Love perverted’.

In the text

I get so puzzled sometimes that I am not even able to make up my mind whether there is any good at all in us, or whether we are born bad. We seem to be dreadful things; we seem to give a deal of trouble; we are always being complained of and guarded against. One way or other, we fill the papers.

Trotty, First Quarter

He says ‘we’ throughout, so the doubt is about a class and not about himself. His phrases, ‘complained of and guarded against’ and ‘we fill the papers’, are the language in which the poor are written about, and the book’s argument starts here: Trotty has learnt his opinion of himself from newspapers.

‘Unnatural and cruel!’ Toby cried. ‘Unnatural and cruel! None but people who were bad at heart, born bad, who had no business on the earth, could do such deeds. It’s too true, all I’ve heard to-day; too just, too full of proof. We’re Bad!’

Trotty, Second Quarter

Almost every phrase is borrowed. ‘Born bad’ is his own doubt from the morning, ‘no business on the earth’ is what the gentlemen have said all day, and ‘too full of proof’ accepts Filer’s method. An hour after sheltering Fern and Lilian, Trotty still judges the woman in the paper by other men’s rules, and the bells answer him immediately.

Putting down

Alderman Cute’s favourite phrase treats punishment as social policy: whatever is inconvenient about poverty can be removed by a magistrate. Dickens tests it on Will Fern, jailed for sleeping in a shed and then for almost every attempt to live, and on the banker Deedles, who shoots himself and is mourned by Cute as the most respectable of men.

Through the book Threatened in the First Quarter, rung by the bells, described from the receiving end in Fern’s speech at Bowley Hall, and exposed by the Deedles episode, which it never reaches.

In the text

Famous man for the common people, Alderman Cute! Never out of temper with them! Easy, affable, joking, knowing gentleman!

First Quarter

These lines have no main verb and no named speaker, and they sound like Cute’s reputation talking. Dickens repeats the trick each time the Alderman is at his worst, with ‘Always affable with the working classes’ and ‘O, he knew how to banter the common people’, until the praise has become an accusation the narrator never has to make outright.

Give us, in mercy, better homes when we’re a-lying in our cradles; give us better food when we’re a-working for our lives; give us kinder laws to bring us back when we’re a-going wrong; and don’t set jail, jail, jail, afore us, everywhere we turn.

Will Fern, Third Quarter

After a list of arrests, each ending ‘To jail with him’, Fern turns to what could be done instead, and his order follows a life: the cradle, the working years, the moment of going wrong. ‘Jail, jail, jail’ is his reply to Cute’s ‘Put Down’. Among his hearers are ‘you gentlemen that sits at Sessions’, and Sir Joseph’s response is to call him a living example.

The right to marry

Richard and Meg want to marry because waiting will only make them old. Filer calls that ignorance of political economy and Cute calls it unbecoming, and in the vision their advice works: the engagement lingers and breaks, and the marriage that finally happens is a rescue attempt. The book ends with the wedding restored to New Year’s Day and a band in the room.

Through the book Proposed at noon, talked out of existence by the gentlemen, shown failing across the vision, and reinstated in the last pages.

In the text

He says that if we wait: people in our condition: until we see our way quite clearly, the way will be a narrow one indeed—the common way—the Grave, father.

Meg, reporting Richard, First Quarter

Richard’s reasoning is economic, and it answers Filer before Filer has spoken: if the poor wait for security before they marry, the only certain thing ahead of them is the grave. The word ‘way’ shifts three times in the sentence, from a clear view of the future to a narrow path to the road everybody takes. Meg delivers it ‘in a tremble, but quite plainly’.

and may heap up facts on figures, facts on figures, facts on figures, mountains high and dry; and he can no more hope to persuade ’em that they have no right or business to be married, than he can hope to persuade ’em that they have no earthly right or business to be born.

Mr Filer, First Quarter

The sentence moves from marrying to being born as if the second followed obviously from the first, and Filer adds that the point was ‘reduced to a mathematical certainty long ago’. He has just divided the nation’s tripe by its widows and orphans to reach a pennyweight each. Dickens lets the argument run in its own terms until the terms condemn it: a couple are told their existence was an error in the sums.

The Old Year and the New

The story runs from noon to midnight on the last day of a year, and its clock is audible throughout: dinner-time, the quarter chimes, the old year dying like a worn-out labourer, the New Year awaited like an heir. The Goblin speaks for Time and tells Trotty that lamenting the good old times is a wrong done to it.

Through the book The Second Quarter makes the dying year an allegory of an old working man. The Third makes Time a force that cries Advance. The Fourth ends in the New Year peal and Trotty’s prophecy of a sea of Time that will sweep oppressors away.

In the text

Put ’em down, Put ’em down! Good old Times, Good old Times! Facts and Figures, Facts and Figures!

First Quarter

Each gentleman supplies one phrase, and the bells double each one the way a peal repeats its changes. ‘If they said anything they said this’, the narrator adds, which places the words in Trotty’s ear and leaves the bells’ own meaning open. By the Second Quarter his trot has fallen into the same measure and ‘would fit itself to nothing else’.

‘The voice of Time,’ said the Phantom, ‘cries to man, Advance! Time is for his advancement and improvement; for his greater worth, his greater happiness, his better life; his progress onward to that goal within its knowledge and its view, and set there, in the period when Time and He began.

The Goblin of the Great Bell, Third Quarter

Here Time is a machine that only goes forward: whoever tries to hold it back, the Goblin continues, ‘arrests a mighty engine which will strike the meddler dead’. Its next charge makes a lament for ‘days which have had their trial and their failure’ a wrong done to the bells, which turns the red-faced gentleman’s good old times into an offence, and Trotty, who half believed him, into an accessory.

The Dignity of Labour

Trotty loves to earn his money, and Fern says he never held his hand back from work however hard or poorly paid. Sir Joseph toasts the Dignity of Labour at a banquet his tenants are let into after eating their puddings in another hall. Meg and Lilian’s needlework shows what the phrase means in practice: long nights of work that earn only enough to go on working.

Through the book Praised as a principle in Sir Joseph’s library, toasted at Bowley Hall, and measured in the vision by what Meg can earn with a baby in her lap.

In the text

I only want to live like one of the Almighty’s creeturs. I can’t—I don’t—and so there’s a pit dug between me, and them that can and do.

Will Fern, Second Quarter

Fern asks for nothing beyond what any creature has, and ‘I can’t—I don’t’ shows him correcting a complaint into a plain statement of fact. The pit between him and ‘them that can and do’ is the gap the gentlemen have been talking across all day without seeing it. He adds that men like him could be counted ‘by hundreds and by thousands’.

Strike me old, Meg! Wither me, and shrivel me, and free me from the dreadful thoughts that tempt me in my youth!

Lilian, Third Quarter

Lilian asks for age as protection, because her youth and looks are the one thing she has that someone would pay for. Dickens never names the temptation, and the next time she is heard of she is sending Meg money through Richard. ‘Wither’ and ‘shrivel’ treat her beauty as a danger, which is how Cute spoke of Meg’s when he warned Trotty she was ‘much too handsome’.

Respectable men

The book saves the word for the rich. Deedles, who puts a pistol to his mouth in his own counting house, is ‘one of the most respectable men’ Cute ever knew, while the poor mother in Trotty’s newspaper is unnatural and cruel. At that point the narrator breaks in and asks Cute to weigh the two cases on his own scales.

Through the book Sir Joseph’s settled accounts set the standard in the Second Quarter; Deedles breaks it in the Third; Tugby, a ‘retail Friend and Father’, applies it to Meg in the Fourth.

In the text

‘Oh the brain, the brain!’ exclaimed the pious Alderman, lifting up his hands. ‘Oh the nerves, the nerves; the mysteries of this machine called Man! Oh the little that unhinges it: poor creatures that we are!

Alderman Cute, Third Quarter

For a rich man’s suicide Cute finds nerves, the brain and ‘this machine called Man’, and says he will wear the deepest mourning. The narrator’s adjective ‘pious’ is sarcastic. A paragraph later the narrator asks where the Putting Down has gone, and tells him to weigh this death against a starving mother’s on the same scales.

‘This is the last night of an Old Year, and I won’t carry ill-blood and quarrellings and disturbances into a New One, to please you nor anybody else,’ said Tugby, who was quite a retail Friend and Father.

Tugby, Fourth Quarter

Sir Joseph’s title travels down the social scale to a small shopkeeper who was once his porter. ‘Retail’ is a shop word, and the joke has a serious point: the habit of mind needs no fortune, only a doorway to stand in. Tugby even echoes Sir Joseph’s rule about carrying nothing into the New Year, and Meg goes from his door towards the river.
