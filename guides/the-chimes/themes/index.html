The Chimes · Charles Dickens

Themes

Who speaks for the poor

Every powerful man in the book has a theory of the poor, and none of them asks a poor person anything. Filer has tables, Cute has a phrase, Sir Joseph has a paternal system and the red-faced gentleman has a lost golden age. Will Fern is the one labourer given a platform, and he uses it first to ask the gentry to leave him alone and then to ask for better homes, food and laws.

The theories are set out as talk in the first two Quarters. The vision in the third and fourth shows them applied to Meg, Lilian, Richard and Fern over years.

They hung there, in all weathers, with the wind and rain driving in upon them; facing only the outsides of all those houses; never getting any nearer to the blazing fires that gleamed and shone upon the windows

First Quarter

I get so puzzled sometimes that I am not even able to make up my mind whether there is any good at all in us, or whether we are born bad. We seem to be dreadful things; we seem to give a deal of trouble; we are always being complained of and guarded against. One way or other, we fill the papers.

Trotty, First Quarter

Born bad

Trotty’s question at noon is whether the poor are born bad or have been made to feel that they are. The gentlemen answer it for him, a newspaper story seems to confirm the answer, and the vision exists to reverse it: Meg, the best person he knows, is driven towards the act he called unnatural that evening.

A doubt in the First Quarter, a conviction by the end of the Second, and a retraction at the river, where Trotty pleads that the crime has sprung from ‘Love perverted’.

I get so puzzled sometimes that I am not even able to make up my mind whether there is any good at all in us, or whether we are born bad. We seem to be dreadful things; we seem to give a deal of trouble; we are always being complained of and guarded against. One way or other, we fill the papers.

Trotty, First Quarter

and may heap up facts on figures, facts on figures, facts on figures, mountains high and dry; and he can no more hope to persuade ’em that they have no right or business to be married, than he can hope to persuade ’em that they have no earthly right or business to be born.

Mr Filer, First Quarter

Putting down

Alderman Cute’s favourite phrase treats punishment as social policy: whatever is inconvenient about poverty can be removed by a magistrate. Dickens tests it on Will Fern, jailed for sleeping in a shed and then for almost every attempt to live, and on the banker Deedles, who shoots himself and is mourned by Cute as the most respectable of men.

Threatened in the First Quarter, rung by the bells, described from the receiving end in Fern’s speech at Bowley Hall, and exposed by the Deedles episode, which it never reaches.

Famous man for the common people, Alderman Cute! Never out of temper with them! Easy, affable, joking, knowing gentleman!

First Quarter

And if you attempt, desperately, and ungratefully, and impiously, and fraudulently attempt, to drown yourself, or hang yourself, I’ll have no pity for you, for I have made up my mind to Put all suicide Down!

Alderman Cute, First Quarter

The right to marry

Richard and Meg want to marry because waiting will only make them old. Filer calls that ignorance of political economy and Cute calls it unbecoming, and in the vision their advice works: the engagement lingers and breaks, and the marriage that finally happens is a rescue attempt. The book ends with the wedding restored to New Year’s Day and a band in the room.

Proposed at noon, talked out of existence by the gentlemen, shown failing across the vision, and reinstated in the last pages.

He says that if we wait: people in our condition: until we see our way quite clearly, the way will be a narrow one indeed—the common way—the Grave, father.

Meg, reporting Richard, First Quarter

and may heap up facts on figures, facts on figures, facts on figures, mountains high and dry; and he can no more hope to persuade ’em that they have no right or business to be married, than he can hope to persuade ’em that they have no earthly right or business to be born.

Mr Filer, First Quarter

The Old Year and the New

The story runs from noon to midnight on the last day of a year, and its clock is audible throughout: dinner-time, the quarter chimes, the old year dying like a worn-out labourer, the New Year awaited like an heir. The Goblin speaks for Time and tells Trotty that lamenting the good old times is a wrong done to it.

The Second Quarter makes the dying year an allegory of an old working man. The Third makes Time a force that cries Advance. The Fourth ends in the New Year peal and Trotty’s prophecy of a sea of Time that will sweep oppressors away.

Put ’em down, Put ’em down! Good old Times, Good old Times! Facts and Figures, Facts and Figures!

First Quarter

‘The voice of Time,’ said the Phantom, ‘cries to man, Advance! Time is for his advancement and improvement; for his greater worth, his greater happiness, his better life; his progress onward to that goal within its knowledge and its view, and set there, in the period when Time and He began.

The Goblin of the Great Bell, Third Quarter

The Dignity of Labour

Trotty loves to earn his money, and Fern says he never held his hand back from work however hard or poorly paid. Sir Joseph toasts the Dignity of Labour at a banquet his tenants are let into after eating their puddings in another hall. Meg and Lilian’s needlework shows what the phrase means in practice: long nights of work that earn only enough to go on working.

Praised as a principle in Sir Joseph’s library, toasted at Bowley Hall, and measured in the vision by what Meg can earn with a baby in her lap.

You needn’t trouble yourself to think about anything. I will think for you; I know what is good for you; I am your perpetual parent.

Sir Joseph Bowley, Second Quarter

I only want to live like one of the Almighty’s creeturs. I can’t—I don’t—and so there’s a pit dug between me, and them that can and do.

Will Fern, Second Quarter

Respectable men

The book saves the word for the rich. Deedles, who puts a pistol to his mouth in his own counting house, is ‘one of the most respectable men’ Cute ever knew, while the poor mother in Trotty’s newspaper is unnatural and cruel. At that point the narrator breaks in and asks Cute to weigh the two cases on his own scales.

Sir Joseph’s settled accounts set the standard in the Second Quarter; Deedles breaks it in the Third; Tugby, a ‘retail Friend and Father’, applies it to Meg in the Fourth.

‘Oh the brain, the brain!’ exclaimed the pious Alderman, lifting up his hands. ‘Oh the nerves, the nerves; the mysteries of this machine called Man! Oh the little that unhinges it: poor creatures that we are!

Alderman Cute, Third Quarter

‘This is the last night of an Old Year, and I won’t carry ill-blood and quarrellings and disturbances into a New One, to please you nor anybody else,’ said Tugby, who was quite a retail Friend and Father.

Tugby, Fourth Quarter
