The Chimes · Charles Dickens

Characters

Toby Veck (Trotty)

Ticket-porter at the church door; first appears First Quarter

An old porter, over sixty, who stands outside a London church waiting for errands and is called Trotty from his pace. He loves earning his money and wants to believe he is worth his salt, which is why the gentlemen’s talk hurts him: he takes it seriously. The book follows how he comes to think that people like him are born bad, and how hard it is for him to unlearn it.

Meg Veck

Trotty’s daughter, a needlewoman; first appears First Quarter

Trotty’s only child, whose mother died when she was small. She brings her father his dinner, plans a New Year’s Day wedding with Richard, and is frightened out of it by one speech from Alderman Cute. In the vision she becomes the test case for the gentlemen’s theories, worked and starved over years until she carries her baby to the river.

Richard

A young smith, engaged to Meg; first appears First Quarter

A strong, handsome young smith who argues that the poor cannot afford to wait for better times, because waiting leads only to the grave. In the vision he takes the gentlemen’s hint that he could do better than Meg, loses his work, looks and friends to drink, and marries her too late to be saved. Awake, he wants to answer the Alderman back, and Meg will not let him.

Will Fern

A labourer from Sir Joseph Bowley’s village; first appears Second Quarter

A sun-browned countryman with grizzled hair who has walked to London to look for work and for a friend of Lilian’s dead mother, carrying the child in his arms. He talks about a working life that never kept him without heat or self-pity. In the vision he comes out of jail again and again, until he visits Meg to say goodbye and speaks of fires in the dark.

Lilian Fern

Will Fern’s orphaned niece; first appears Second Quarter

A child of nine when Trotty carries her home, and in the vision a young woman sewing beside Meg for bare bread. She asks Meg to make her old so that she will not be tempted, and later sends money through Richard from a life the book describes only by what it has cost her. The Spirit of the Chimes that guides Trotty wears her face as a child.

Alderman Cute

Alderman and Justice of the Peace; first appears First Quarter

A merry, knowing magistrate who is sure he understands the common people and means to Put Down whatever troubles him about them, from want and starvation to young mothers and suicide. When a rich banker shoots himself, he talks instead about nerves and the mysteries of the machine called Man.

Mr Filer

A political economist; first appears First Quarter

A low-spirited gentleman who reduces everything to tables. He is so short-sighted that he has to put his face almost into Trotty’s tripe to see what it is, which is Dickens’s joke about how closely he looks at the poor. He calculates the waste on tripe until Trotty feels he has starved a garrison, and declares that the poor have no more right to marry than to be born.

Sir Joseph Bowley

Baronet and Member of Parliament; first appears Second Quarter

The self-appointed Poor Man’s Friend and Father, who settles every account before the year turns and tells Trotty that his only business in life is with Sir Joseph. He says he agrees with no party, then signs a letter asking Cute to commit Will Fern as a vagabond. In the vision he gives a banquet for his tenants on his wife’s birthday and toasts the Dignity of Labour.

Mrs Chickenstalker

A shopkeeper in the general line; first appears Second Quarter

A stout widow with a crowded little shop, to whom Trotty owes ten or twelve shillings. In the vision she has married Tugby, Sir Joseph’s former porter, and she stands up for Meg against a husband who wants the dying Richard sent to the workhouse. In the waking ending she arrives with a steaming pitcher of flip and turns out to have been the friend of Lilian’s mother.

The Goblin of the Great Bell

The spirit of the largest bell in the tower; first appears Third Quarter

A bearded, hooded figure the size of its bell, which speaks for all the Chimes. It tries Trotty for the wrongs he has done them in words and sends him to learn from his daughter’s life how bad the bad are born, a sentence that turns out to mean the reverse of what Trotty first hears in it.
