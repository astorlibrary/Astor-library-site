The Chimes · Charles Dickens

What happens

First Quarter Tripe on the church steps

Trotty stands at his post in the cold, wondering aloud whether the poor are born bad. Meg brings him hot tripe and the news that Richard wants to marry her on New Year’s Day, since waiting will only make them old. Three gentlemen come out of the house whose steps they are eating on. Filer proves by arithmetic that tripe is wasteful, a red-faced gentleman mourns the good old times, and Alderman Cute tells Meg he will Put Down her troubles before she has had them. The couple leave apart, and the bells seem to ring the gentlemen’s phrases.

The church door
The narrator’s opening about sleeping alone in a church, Trotty’s niche in the church wall, and his belief that the bells talk to him.
Meg’s basket
A guessing game over a basin of tripe, and Meg’s report of Richard’s argument that the poor who wait for better times wait for the grave.
The three gentlemen
Filer’s figures, the good old times, and Cute’s promise to Put Down distressed wives, shoeless boys and suicide.
The changed tune
Cute pays Trotty sixpence to carry a letter, and the bells, which used to tell him to keep a good heart, now repeat the gentlemen.

Second Quarter The Poor Man's Friend

Trotty carries Cute’s letter to Sir Joseph Bowley, a Member of Parliament who settles every account before the year turns and calls himself the Poor Man’s Friend and Father. The letter asks whether Will Fern, a labourer from Sir Joseph’s village found asleep in a shed, should be put down, and Sir Joseph dictates a reply recommending prison. On the way home Trotty runs into Fern himself, carrying his orphaned niece Lilian, warns him off, and takes them both home to Meg. Alone later with his newspaper, he reads of a mother who killed herself and her child, decides the poor really are bad, and hears the bells calling him up the tower.

Sir Joseph’s library
A charity that gives two votes for five pounds, a full-length portrait over the fireplace, and a letter asking for Fern’s committal as a vagabond.
Behind-hand with the world
Trotty admits to owing Mrs Chickenstalker ten or twelve shillings and a little rent, and is sent away as an example of his class.
Will Fern
A countryman explains, without raising his voice, why he wants the gentry to leave him alone, and Trotty carries his niece home.
The tower door
The newspaper, the bells ringing ‘Haunt and hunt him’, and a climb in the dark until Trotty faints among the bells.

Third Quarter The Goblin Sight

Trotty comes round in a belfry swarming with spirits of the bells. The Goblin of the Great Bell charges him with wronging the Chimes, above all by hearing in them the creed of the men who would put the poor down, and a spirit with the face of the child Lilian becomes his guide. He sees his own body at the foot of the tower, nine years dead. He then watches Meg and the grown Lilian sewing for bare bread, a New Year’s Day banquet at Bowley Hall interrupted by Will Fern fresh from prison, Richard come to Meg ruined by drink, and Lilian’s return to die at Meg’s knees.

The belfry
Spirits pour out of the bells and die away when the ringing stops, and the Great Bell lists the wrongs Trotty has done in words.
Meg and Lilian
Two women at their needlework, and Lilian asking to be made old so that her youth cannot tempt her.
Bowley Hall
Skittles with the tenants, news that the banker Deedles has shot himself, and Will Fern’s speech to the gentry.
The garret
Richard brings a purse from Lilian that Meg will not touch. Later Lilian comes back herself and dies asking to be forgiven.

Fourth Quarter To desperation

More years pass in the vision. Mrs Chickenstalker has married Tugby, Sir Joseph’s former porter, and Meg lodges above their shop, married at last to Richard in the hope of saving him. Richard dies and leaves her with a baby girl. Will Fern visits her for the last time and speaks of fires to come, and on the last night of the year Tugby turns her out. She carries the baby to the river, and Trotty, crying that he has learnt his lesson, holds her back as the New Year bells break the vision. He wakes to find Meg trimming her gown for the wedding and Richard at the door, and the neighbours arrive with a band.

Tugby’s shop
Mrs Tugby tells a doctor who attends the poor how Meg came to marry Richard, and Richard dies upstairs.
Will Fern’s last visit
Fern holds the baby, learns her name, and leaves Meg repeating ‘Like Lilian, when her mother died and left her’.
The river
Turned away by Tugby, Meg runs to the water with the child, and Trotty pleads for her to the figures in the bells.
New Year’s Day
Richard claims the first kiss of the year, the Drum brings a band, and Mrs Chickenstalker turns out to be the friend Will Fern came to London to find.
