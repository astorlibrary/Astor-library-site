The Chimes · Charles Dickens

Form and language

Mock praise

Praising a character in terms that condemn him.

Whenever Alderman Cute is at his worst, the narrator adds a line of applause in the voice of his admirers: famous man for the common people, always affable with the working classes, not a bit of pride. The praise comes straight after his cruellest remarks, so the reader does the judging.

and may heap up facts on figures, facts on figures, facts on figures, mountains high and dry; and he can no more hope to persuade ’em that they have no right or business to be married, than he can hope to persuade ’em that they have no earthly right or business to be born.

Mr Filer, First Quarter

Refrain

A phrase repeated at intervals so that its meaning shifts with what has happened since.

Trotty hears the bells say ‘keep a good heart’, then ‘Put ’em down’ and ‘Facts and Figures’, then ‘Haunt and hunt him’, then ‘Follow her’ and ‘To desperation’. The bells keep one rhythm and Trotty fits whatever he fears into it. His own refrain, ‘Here we are and here we go’, belongs to his moments of generosity.

And if you attempt, desperately, and ungratefully, and impiously, and fraudulently attempt, to drown yourself, or hang yourself, I’ll have no pity for you, for I have made up my mind to Put all suicide Down!

Alderman Cute, First Quarter

Personification

Objects and abstractions given bodies, voices and feelings.

The bells were baptised by bishops and had godfathers; the old year lies down to die like a labourer who has done his work; the New Year is an infant heir. Dickens gives the calendar a class structure, and the Goblin of the Great Bell, speaking for Time, grows out of the same habit.

They hung there, in all weathers, with the wind and rain driving in upon them; facing only the outsides of all those houses; never getting any nearer to the blazing fires that gleamed and shone upon the windows

First Quarter

Dialect

Spelling and grammar used to mark where a speaker comes from.

Trotty says ‘obserwation’ and ‘creetur’, and Will Fern speaks a country English (‘I has a nat’ral angry word with that man’) that the gentlemen would hear as ignorance. Dickens gives Fern the most coherent argument in the book in that dialect, while Sir Joseph flounders for words in his own library.

I get so puzzled sometimes that I am not even able to make up my mind whether there is any good at all in us, or whether we are born bad. We seem to be dreadful things; we seem to give a deal of trouble; we are always being complained of and guarded against. One way or other, we fill the papers.

Trotty, First Quarter

Direct address

A narrator turning to speak to the reader directly.

The first sentence asks for a ‘mutual understanding’ between story-teller and story-reader, and the last paragraph turns to the ‘listener’ and asks for something to be done in the reader’s own sphere. In between, the narrator interrupts to rebuke Cute and to call on ‘Youth and Beauty’ to look at Lilian.

If it be so, O listener, dear to him in all his visions, try to bear in mind the stern realities from which these shadows come; and in your sphere—none is too wide, and none too limited for such an end—endeavour to correct, improve, and soften them.

Fourth Quarter

Dream frame

A story told as a vision or dream, from which the dreamer wakes.

Trotty climbs the tower and faints, yet at the end he catches his feet in the newspaper that has fallen on his hearth, as if he had never left his chair. The last paragraph asks whether he dreamed and leaves the question open, while insisting that the ‘stern realities’ behind the shadows are real.

‘Have mercy on her!’ he exclaimed, ‘as one in whom this dreadful crime has sprung from Love perverted; from the strongest, deepest Love we fallen creatures know! Think what her misery must have been, when such seed bears such fruit! Heaven meant her to be good. There is no loving mother on the earth who might not come to this, if such a life had gone before.

Trotty, Fourth Quarter
