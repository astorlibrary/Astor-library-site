Empire and loot

Every fortune in the book was made in India and none of it honestly. A rajah hedging his bets in 1857, four guards at a gate, two officers with gambling debts: the chest passes by murder and fraud into a villa in Norwood, and the consequences follow it up the Thames. Small’s word for it throughout is loot. The novel lets that word stand, and an Indian partner gets the sharpest line on the subject, yet it never imagines the jewels going back; the only possible ends are an English heiress or the riverbed.

Through the book Pearls in a cardboard box, then an Eastern room in a suburban terrace, then a garden dug up like a goldfield, and finally the story of Agra, told by a man who went out at eighteen with the Queen’s shilling.

In the text

There was something strangely incongruous in this Oriental figure framed in the commonplace doorway of a third-rate suburban dwelling-house.

Chapter III

Watson finds the man incongruous; the plot says he is nothing of the kind. A suburb like this is where Indian money was spent, and the servant is in the doorway because a major came home rich. The sentence frames him like a picture, which is how the book treats almost every Indian it shows in London: as décor that came back with the furniture.

It was soothing to catch even that passing glimpse of a tranquil English home in the midst of the wild, dark business which had absorbed us.

Chapter VII

English home on one side, wild and dark on the other: Watson sorts the night into domestic and foreign. The case will not let the division stand. The wild business is an English major’s fraud carried out from an English villa, and the income that built such halls came, often enough, from where the treasure did. The glimpse soothes him because it lets him forget that.

Race and the colonial gazetteer

Holmes identifies the second man by reading aloud from a reference book, and what it says about the Andaman Islanders (stunted, hideous, cannibal, untameable) is false in nearly every particular. The novel presents it as the latest authority and then confirms it with Watson’s own eyes on the river. Small’s language about India is of the same kind. This is more than incidental colour: the plot needs a killer nobody will mourn, and it builds one out of the prejudices of its sources.

Through the book A Hindu servant who looks incongruous in a doorway; a footprint read by racial type; the gazetteer; a face on a launch; and a last chapter that talks of black devils while showing the Indian partners keeping faith and an English officer breaking it.

In the text

They are naturally hideous, having large, misshapen heads, small, fierce eyes, and distorted features.

Chapter VIII

Holmes, who never guesses, takes this on trust because it is in print. The entry goes on to put the people’s height below four feet and to end every shipwreck with a cannibal feast; none of it is true of the Andamanese, who were not cannibals and did not use blowpipes either. Naturally is the word to watch. It makes a judgement about looks into a fact of nature, and the chapter treats a book of colonial hearsay as the same kind of evidence as a footprint.

He was stanch and true, was little Tonga. No man ever had a more faithful mate.

Jonathan Small, Chapter XII

Set this beside Watson’s bestiality and cruelty: the only man who knew Tonga calls him faithful, and means it. But the praise is a master’s. Little, mate and, elsewhere, hell-hound, imp and poor Tonga are the words for a dog or a child, and the same speaker beat him with a rope’s end. There are two accounts of the Islander in the book, a monster and a pet, and no third in which he might have had reasons of his own.

Observation, inference and proof

Holmes’s method is set out as doctrine in the first chapter and tested in a garret in the sixth. It is impressive and it has limits the book is frank about. The watch trick wounds a friend. The trail ends at a barrel of creosote. Days pass with nothing. The launch is found by legwork, and the motive is told by Small, never deduced. Detection here gets the men; it does not get the treasure or the truth about Agra.

Through the book Monographs on tobacco ash and footprints, a demonstration on a watch, a reconstruction from a rope and a thorn, a false scent, a disguise, and then a long silence while the prisoner talks.

In the text

Detection is, or ought to be, an exact science, and should be treated in the same cold and unemotional manner.

Sherlock Holmes, Chapter I

Is, or ought to be: the correction concedes that it is not yet. Holmes goes on to compare Watson’s book to an elopement worked into a proposition of Euclid, and the novel that follows is exactly that: a love story run through a problem. Doyle lets his detective condemn the form of the book he is in, and then writes it anyway.

How often have I said to you that when you have eliminated the impossible whatever remains, however improbable, must be the truth?

Sherlock Holmes, Chapter VI

The most quoted rule in detective fiction, and here it is doing modest work: the door, the window and the chimney have been ruled out, and Watson supplies the answer himself (the hole in the roof). As logic it is sound only if the list of possibilities is complete, and Holmes never shows that it is. What the sentence really supplies is tone: ‘how often’ makes Watson, and the reader, the slow pupil.

Justice, oaths and rightful owners

The four call their mark on Major Sholto’s body an act of justice, and the book does not quite laugh at them. Small murdered for the jewels, but he was then cheated by an officer who had sworn an oath to him, and his argument (who has earned this, if not the men who paid twenty years for it?) goes unanswered by Holmes, Watson or Jones. English law can hang him. It has no view on whose the treasure was.

Through the book An anonymous letter promises a wronged woman justice in chapter II; a card on a corpse claims it in chapter V; Small shouts the word back at a policeman in chapter XII.

In the text

The cursed greed which has been my besetting sin through life has withheld from her the treasure, half at least of which should have been hers.

Major John Sholto, Chapter IV

He allows that half at least should have been hers, and says nothing of the rest. The dying man reckons only with the English claim on the jewels, his dead friend’s daughter. The four convicts whose secret he bought with an oath, and the rajah before them, do not enter the account. He adds that he made no use of the treasure; possession was the whole pleasure. It is a miser’s confession and it is also, on a small scale, a colonial one.

Whose loot is this, if it is not ours? Where is the justice that I should give it up to those who have never earned it?

Jonathan Small, Chapter XII

Loot came into English from Hindi, and Small uses it without embarrassment: he does not claim the treasure was clean, only that nobody in the room has a better title. Earned is a grotesque word for murder followed by twenty years in a swamp, yet the challenge stands. The Sholtos’ claim rests on fraud and the Crown’s on conquest. Doyle gives the argument to a convict in handcuffs and lets no one reply.

Marriage and money

Watson’s romance is governed by arithmetic. A half-pay surgeon may court a governess; he may not court the richest heiress in England without looking like what he fears he is. So the love story can only end well if the detective story ends badly, and it does. The neatness is a little chilling: Mary’s legal inheritance is destroyed by a criminal, and the man who loves her thanks God for it to her face.

Through the book Attraction at first sight, selfishness at the mention of the sum, hands held in the dark garden at Norwood, and a proposal over an empty iron box.

In the text

“You really are an automaton,—a calculating-machine!” I cried. “There is something positively inhuman in you at times.”

Dr John Watson, Chapter II

Watson is stung on Mary’s behalf, within minutes of meeting her. Holmes answers that the most winning woman he ever knew was hanged for poisoning three children, so the exchange sets feeling against statistics, and the book will need both: Watson’s attention keeps Mary a person, and Holmes’s keeps her a case. The word inhuman will come back, far less playfully, when Watson looks at Tonga.

Surely it was the place of a loyal friend to rejoice at such news; yet I am ashamed to say that selfishness took me by the soul, and that my heart turned as heavy as lead within me.

Chapter IV

Watson is honest about a feeling that does him no credit, and that honesty is what keeps him likeable through the book. It also gives the narrator a private stake in the outcome. From here the man telling the story of the hunt for the treasure wants it lost, and the reader should hold that in mind when he reports his relief in chapter XI as if it were simple.

Appetite and habit

The book opens on an addiction and keeps finding others. Holmes needs cocaine when he has no problem to work on and says so without shame. Major Sholto could not bear to part with a single pearl he never used. Small’s craving passes from the jewels to revenge. The case cures nobody: it is only, for Holmes, a better stimulant than the one in the morocco case, and when it is over he returns to the first.

Through the book Three injections a day on the first page, a miser’s deathbed in chapter IV, a lifetime spent on one hatred in chapter XII, and the long white hand going up to the mantelpiece on the last.

In the text

“It is cocaine,” he said,—“a seven-per-cent. solution. Would you care to try it?”

Sherlock Holmes, Chapter I

The offer is the shock. Holmes does not hide the habit or defend it; he states the concentration like a chemist and passes the case across as he might a cigar. Doyle, a practising doctor writing in 1889, gives Watson the medical objection and gives Holmes the better lines, and the drug was legal. The scene sets the terms of the book: a mind that treats its own body as apparatus.

“For me,” said Sherlock Holmes, “there still remains the cocaine-bottle.” And he stretched his long white hand up for it.

Sherlock Holmes, Chapter XII

The novel ends on the gesture it began with, the long white hand and the bottle on the mantelpiece, so the whole case is enclosed between two doses. It is an oddly bleak finish for an adventure story. Everyone else is paid in the ordinary coin of plots, marriage, promotion, the gallows; the hero’s reward is the return of his boredom. Doyle had not yet decided that Holmes would be a series, and he closes him here as a character study of a man who cannot bear to be unoccupied.

London

Doyle maps the case across a city that is growing faster than it can be known. Holmes recites the streets south of the river from inside a cab; the brick terraces are tentacles; Norwood hides an Indian fortune behind a wall topped with glass. The Thames is the way the empire’s goods come in and the way the fugitives mean to get out, and the chase ends on marshland that is barely London at all.

Through the book Fog in the Strand, a new terrace near the Wandsworth Road, a suburban villa, the wharves opposite Millbank, the Pool, and the mud flats by Plumstead.

In the text

Was ever such a dreary, dismal, unprofitable world? See how the yellow fog swirls down the street and drifts across the dun-coloured houses.

Sherlock Holmes, Chapter I

Unprofitable is Hamlet’s word for the uses of this world, and Holmes borrows the pose with it. The fog is doing two jobs. It is the London the case will be conducted in, and it is the inside of his head without a problem: yellow, dun, nothing with an edge. A reader is being shown that the great reasoner’s complaint is closer to melancholy than to logic.

An oasis of art in the howling desert of South London.

Thaddeus Sholto, Chapter IV

Doyle had dined with Oscar Wilde the evening this novel was commissioned, and Thaddeus is an aesthete drawn for laughs. The joke has a point he cannot see. His oasis is furnished from a murdered man’s jewel-box, and the desert he despises is the ordinary England his father’s money let him withdraw from. Taste, in this room, is what plunder looks like a generation on.
