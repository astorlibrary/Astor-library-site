The Sign of Four · Arthur Conan Doyle

Themes

Empire and loot

Every fortune in the book was made in India and none of it honestly. A rajah hedging his bets in 1857, four guards at a gate, two officers with gambling debts: the chest passes by murder and fraud into a villa in Norwood, and the consequences follow it up the Thames. Small’s word for it throughout is loot. The novel lets that word stand, and an Indian partner gets the sharpest line on the subject, yet it never imagines the jewels going back; the only possible ends are an English heiress or the riverbed.

Pearls in a cardboard box, then an Eastern room in a suburban terrace, then a garden dug up like a goldfield, and finally the story of Agra, told by a man who went out at eighteen with the Queen’s shilling.

There was something strangely incongruous in this Oriental figure framed in the commonplace doorway of a third-rate suburban dwelling-house.

An oasis of art in the howling desert of South London.

Thaddeus Sholto, Chapter IV

Race and the colonial gazetteer

Holmes identifies the second man by reading aloud from a reference book, and what it says about the Andaman Islanders (stunted, hideous, cannibal, untameable) is false in nearly every particular. The novel presents it as the latest authority and then confirms it with Watson’s own eyes on the river. Small’s language about India is of the same kind. This is more than incidental colour: the plot needs a killer nobody will mourn, and it builds one out of the prejudices of its sources.

A Hindu servant who looks incongruous in a doorway; a footprint read by racial type; the gazetteer; a face on a launch; and a last chapter that talks of black devils while showing the Indian partners keeping faith and an English officer breaking it.

In an experience of women which extends over many nations and three separate continents, I have never looked upon a face which gave a clearer promise of a refined and sensitive nature.

Observation, inference and proof

Holmes’s method is set out as doctrine in the first chapter and tested in a garret in the sixth. It is impressive and it has limits the book is frank about. The watch trick wounds a friend. The trail ends at a barrel of creosote. Days pass with nothing. The launch is found by legwork, and the motive is told by Small, never deduced. Detection here gets the men; it does not get the treasure or the truth about Agra.

Monographs on tobacco ash and footprints, a demonstration on a watch, a reconstruction from a rope and a thorn, a false scent, a disguise, and then a long silence while the prisoner talks.

“My mind,” he said, “rebels at stagnation. Give me problems, give me work, give me the most abstruse cryptogram or the most intricate analysis, and I am in my own proper atmosphere.

Sherlock Holmes, Chapter I

Detection is, or ought to be, an exact science, and should be treated in the same cold and unemotional manner.

Sherlock Holmes, Chapter I

Justice, oaths and rightful owners

The four call their mark on Major Sholto’s body an act of justice, and the book does not quite laugh at them. Small murdered for the jewels, but he was then cheated by an officer who had sworn an oath to him, and his argument (who has earned this, if not the men who paid twenty years for it?) goes unanswered by Holmes, Watson or Jones. English law can hang him. It has no view on whose the treasure was.

An anonymous letter promises a wronged woman justice in chapter II; a card on a corpse claims it in chapter V; Small shouts the word back at a policeman in chapter XII.

The cursed greed which has been my besetting sin through life has withheld from her the treasure, half at least of which should have been hers.

Major John Sholto, Chapter IV

I could not but think what a terrible criminal he would have made had he turned his energy and sagacity against the law, instead of exerting them in its defence.

Chapter VI

Marriage and money

Watson’s romance is governed by arithmetic. A half-pay surgeon may court a governess; he may not court the richest heiress in England without looking like what he fears he is. So the love story can only end well if the detective story ends badly, and it does. The neatness is a little chilling: Mary’s legal inheritance is destroyed by a criminal, and the man who loves her thanks God for it to her face.

Attraction at first sight, selfishness at the mention of the sum, hands held in the dark garden at Norwood, and a proposal over an empty iron box.

“You really are an automaton,—a calculating-machine!” I cried. “There is something positively inhuman in you at times.”

Dr John Watson, Chapter II

Chapter II

Appetite and habit

The book opens on an addiction and keeps finding others. Holmes needs cocaine when he has no problem to work on and says so without shame. Major Sholto could not bear to part with a single pearl he never used. Small’s craving passes from the jewels to revenge. The case cures nobody: it is only, for Holmes, a better stimulant than the one in the morocco case, and when it is over he returns to the first.

Three injections a day on the first page, a miser’s deathbed in chapter IV, a lifetime spent on one hatred in chapter XII, and the long white hand going up to the mantelpiece on the last.

“It is cocaine,” he said,—“a seven-per-cent. solution. Would you care to try it?”

London

Doyle maps the case across a city that is growing faster than it can be known. Holmes recites the streets south of the river from inside a cab; the brick terraces are tentacles; Norwood hides an Indian fortune behind a wall topped with glass. The Thames is the way the empire’s goods come in and the way the fugitives mean to get out, and the chase ends on marshland that is barely London at all.

Fog in the Strand, a new terrace near the Wandsworth Road, a suburban villa, the wharves opposite Millbank, the Pool, and the mud flats by Plumstead.

Was ever such a dreary, dismal, unprofitable world? See how the yellow fog swirls down the street and drifts across the dun-coloured houses.
