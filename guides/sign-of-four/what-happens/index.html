The Sign of Four · Arthur Conan Doyle

What happens

Chapters I–II A needle, a watch and a client with six pearls

Watson finally protests about the cocaine, and Holmes answers with a lecture on method and a reading of Watson’s dead brother from the scratches on his watch. Mary Morstan arrives with a father missing since 1878, a pearl for every year since 1882, and a letter asking her to be outside the Lyceum at seven. Watson notices the client more than the case.

Chapter I
The seven-per-cent solution, Holmes’s complaint that Watson has made a romance of his first case, and the watch.
Chapter II
Mary Morstan’s account: Captain Morstan’s disappearance, the advertisement, the pearls and the anonymous letter.

Chapters III–IV Thaddeus Sholto’s story

A cab takes the three of them through fog to a half-built suburb, where a Hindu servant opens the door on Thaddeus Sholto, his hookah and his tiger-skins. He explains that his father, Major Sholto, brought the Agra treasure home, saw Captain Morstan die in an argument over it, hid the body and lived in terror of a man with a wooden leg. His brother Bartholomew has just found the chest.

Chapter III
A paper with four names and a plan of a fort, the rendezvous at the Lyceum, and the drive south through streets Holmes names one by one.
Chapter IV
Thaddeus Sholto’s sanctum, the Major’s deathbed, the face at the window, and the chaplet from which the pearls were taken.

Chapters V–VII A locked room, a thorn and a dog

At Pondicherry Lodge the garden has been dug to pieces by six years of treasure-hunting, and Bartholomew Sholto sits dead at his table with a fixed grin and a poisoned thorn above his ear. Holmes works out that a one-legged man was hauled up by a small barefooted accomplice who came in through the roof. Athelney Jones arrests the household; Holmes borrows a mongrel called Toby and follows creosote across south London.

Chapter V
The prize-fighter at the gate, the housekeeper’s fright, the face seen through the keyhole, and the card that reads The sign of the four.
Chapter VI
The rope, the wooden stump’s marks, the footprint in the garret, and Jones’s confident theory that Thaddeus did it.
Chapter VII
Watson takes Mary home and fetches Toby; the trail runs to a timber-yard barrel, then back, and ends at a wharf.

Chapters VIII–X The search for the Aurora, and the river chase

The fugitives have hired the steam launch Aurora and vanished. Holmes reads out a gazetteer’s account of the Andaman Islanders to name the accomplice, sends his street boys along both banks, and at last finds the launch himself, disguised as an old sailor, in a repair yard. A police boat runs it down at dusk. The Islander raises a blowpipe and is shot; Small’s wooden leg sinks in the mud of the south bank.

Chapter VIII
Mordecai Smith’s wharf, the newspaper report, the dozen street boys, and the gazetteer entry.
Chapter IX
Days of nothing, a chastened Athelney Jones, and an asthmatic old seaman who turns out to be Holmes.
Chapter X
Dinner, Jacobson’s Yard, the pursuit downriver, the two pistol shots and the dart in the woodwork.

Chapters XI–XII The empty chest, and Jonathan Small’s story

Watson carries the iron chest to Mary Morstan and forces it open: Small has scattered the jewels along five miles of river. With the fortune gone, Watson can propose. Small then tells his story: the crocodile, the indigo plantation, Agra under siege, the murder of the merchant Achmet, the two officers who cheated the four, and the Islander who got him off the Andamans. Watson gets a wife, Jones the credit, Holmes the bottle.

Chapter XI
Small’s first remarks in the cabin, the empty chest in Mrs Forrester’s drawing-room, and Thank God.
Chapter XII
The confession entire, from Pershore to Plumstead Marshes, and the last exchange at Baker Street.
