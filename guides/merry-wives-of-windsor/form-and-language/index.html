The Merry Wives of Windsor · William Shakespeare

Form and language

Prose against verse

The switch between prose and verse as a signal of status, mood or place.

Almost all of Windsor speaks prose. Verse is reserved for Fenton and Anne, for the planning of the masque and for the fairies at Herne’s oak, so that the play’s romance and its magic sound different from its business.

O, what a world of vile ill-favoured faults Looks handsome in three hundred pounds a year!

Anne Page, 3.4.31

Dramatic irony

The audience knowing more than a character does.

Falstaff tells Brook about his appointments with Ford’s wife, and describes Ford as a cuckoldly knave, while talking to Ford.

they shall be my East and West Indies, and I will trade to them both.

Falstaff, 1.3.57

Idiolect

A way of speaking that belongs to one character.

Evans’s Welsh, Caius’s French, Quickly’s malapropisms and Nym’s humours make every character recognisable by ear and supply much of the comedy.

Why then, the world’s mine oyster, Which I with sword will open.

Pistol, 2.2.2

Foils

Characters placed side by side so that each shows up the other.

Page and Ford receive the same warning and react in opposite ways; Slender and Caius are two bad choices set against Fenton.

As jealous as Ford, that searched a hollow walnut for his wife’s leman.

Ford, 4.2.133

Wordplay

Puns and double meanings.

Waist and waste, Ford and ford, deer and dear, and a Latin lesson heard as a list of rude words.

Indeed, I am in the waist two yards about, but I am now about no waste; I am about thrift.

Falstaff, 1.3.31

The buck-basket

A physical object on stage that drives the action.

The basket of dirty linen carries Falstaff out and becomes Ford’s obsession; the antlers at Herne’s oak finish the joke by putting the cuckold’s horns on the would-be seducer.

Buck? I would I could wash myself of the buck! Buck, buck, buck! Ay, buck!

Ford, 3.3.120
