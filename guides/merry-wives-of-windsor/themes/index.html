Marriage

Two established marriages are tested by Falstaff and by jealousy, and a new one is fought over. The play compares a husband who trusts too easily, a husband who cannot trust at all, and parents who treat a daughter’s marriage as a deal.

Through the play The Fords are reconciled in 4.4, and the play ends with a marriage made by the couple themselves against both parents’ plans.

In the text

Letter for letter, but that the name of Page and Ford differs!

Mistress Page, 2.1.54

The discovery that the letters are identical is the moment the wives stop being flattered and start plotting. Mistress Page goes on to imagine a thousand copies with a blank for the name.

Wives may be merry and yet honest too.

Mistress Page, 4.2.86

The line that gives the play its title is a defence of the wives’ conduct: they can joke and plot and remain faithful. Honest here means chaste.

Jealousy

Ford’s jealousy is the most dangerous thing in Windsor: he pays a stranger to seduce his wife, searches his house with half the town behind him, and beats an old woman he thinks is a witch.

Through the play It is cured by being made ridiculous. By the end Ford can joke that he will not mistrust his wife until the parson can woo her in good English.

In the text

Better three hours too soon than a minute too late.

Ford, 2.2.247

Ford’s soliloquy is a run of rage, and this proverb is its practical conclusion. His jealousy makes him organised, and so dangerous.

Buck? I would I could wash myself of the buck! Buck, buck, buck! Ay, buck!

Ford, 3.3.120

Buck-washing was laundering, and a buck was a horned animal. Ford hears the cuckold’s horns in a word about laundry while the man he is looking for is carried past him.

Money and love

Falstaff courts for money, Page chooses Slender for his land, Mistress Page chooses Caius for his friends at court, and even Fenton admits that Anne’s fortune was his first motive.

Through the play Anne’s line about three hundred pounds a year and Ford’s closing couplet, money buys lands, and wives are sold by fate, frame the question the whole plot raises.

In the text

Indeed, I am in the waist two yards about, but I am now about no waste; I am about thrift.

Falstaff, 1.3.31

A pun on waist and waste makes his size into a business plan. The joke announces the play’s subject: Falstaff’s courtship is about money from the start.

Why then, the world’s mine oyster, Which I with sword will open.

Pistol, 2.2.2

The line became a proverb for opportunity. Pistol means he will rob his way through the world, which is why Falstaff answers with a list of the scrapes he has got him out of.

Disguise and exposure

Ford becomes Brook, Falstaff becomes a bundle of washing, an old woman and a stag, children become fairies, and two boys are married as Anne.

Through the play Each disguise exposes the person wearing it: Ford’s jealousy, Falstaff’s appetite, the parents’ schemes.

In the text

you may know by my size that I have a kind of alacrity in sinking

Falstaff, 3.5.10

He makes his own humiliation funny before anyone else can. Alacrity usually belongs to quick movement; he attaches it to going under.

There is an old tale goes that Herne the hunter, Sometime a keeper here in Windsor Forest,

Mistress Page, 4.4.24

The play turns to local legend and to verse together. The story of a horned ghost gives the wives a way to put the cuckold’s horns on Falstaff himself.

A knight among citizens

Falstaff assumes that citizens’ wives will be flattered by a knight, and the citizens of Windsor, who have money and sense, prove him wrong.

Through the play The last scene turns the court’s own ceremony, the Order of the Garter, into a blessing spoken by Mistress Quickly over Windsor Castle.

In the text

they shall be my East and West Indies, and I will trade to them both.

Falstaff, 1.3.57

He speaks of two women as colonies to be traded with. The comedy is in the confidence; the audience has seen neither wife yet and already suspects he is wrong.

Now, good Sir John, how like you Windsor wives?

Mistress Page, 5.5.102

The question names the play’s heroines and settles the contest: the town has beaten the knight.

English mangled

Evans speaks Welsh English, Caius French English, Mistress Quickly malapropisms, Pistol stage bombast and Nym the word humour. Half the jokes are about how people speak.

Through the play Falstaff’s lowest moment is being mocked by one that makes fritters of English.

In the text

Have I lived to stand at the taunt of one that makes fritters of English?

Falstaff, 5.5.133

Falstaff can bear being tricked by the wives; being corrected by the Welsh parson is the last straw. His pride is in his language.
