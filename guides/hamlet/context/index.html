Hamlet · William Shakespeare

Context

Dates

1200
Saxo Grammaticus writes the Amleth story into the Gesta Danorum Composed around the turn of the thirteenth century: feigned madness, a murdered father and a dangerous uncle are all already there.
1570
Belleforest retells Amleth in the Histoires tragiques The French version that carried the Danish story into sixteenth-century print and, in some form, to Shakespeare.
1599
The Globe opens on Bankside The playhouse the Lord Chamberlain’s Men built for themselves, and the theatre Hamlet was written for.
1601
Hamlet most likely completed Usually dated to about 1599–1601, close to Julius Caesar and Twelfth Night.
1602
Entered in the Stationers’ Register by James Roberts The entry precedes the first printing by a year.
1603
The First Quarto printed Published by Nicholas Ling and John Trundle. Much shorter than the later texts, and differently ordered in places.
1604
The Second Quarto printed Printed by James Roberts for Nicholas Ling, and advertised as enlarged; the longest of the early versions.
1623
The First Folio text It prints material the Second Quarto lacks and leaves out material the Quarto has, so the two are independent witnesses, and neither is a draft of the other.
1661
Thomas Betterton plays Hamlet after the theatres reopen The beginning of the long tradition in which the part is treated as the measure of an actor.
1899
Sarah Bernhardt plays Hamlet She played it in Paris in a French prose translation by Eugène Morand and Marcel Schwob, and filmed the duel scene in 1900.
1948
Laurence Olivier’s film Heavily cut, and the first Shakespeare film to win the Academy Award for Best Picture.
1996
Kenneth Branagh films a full-text Hamlet A four-hour film that includes the material most productions have to cut.

Places

Kronborg Castle, Helsingør
The fortress on the sound that gave Shakespeare his Elsinore, guarding the narrowest crossing into the Baltic.
The Øresund
The strait the battlements overlook, and the water Hamlet sails out on towards England.
Wittenberg
The university Hamlet and Horatio come from, and which Claudius will not let him return to.
Paris
Where Laertes goes, and where Reynaldo is sent to spread useful slander about him.
London
England, where the sealed commission sends Hamlet to die, and where the play was first performed.

Critics

Samuel Taylor Coleridge

Lectures on Shakespeare (1811–12)

Coleridge saw in Hamlet a mind in which thinking outweighs the power to act: each occasion for action becomes an occasion for reflection, and the reflection replaces the deed. The lectures survive in notes and reports, published after his death.

A. C. Bradley

Shakespearean Tragedy (1904)

Bradley rejected the explanations that the task is impossible or that Hamlet is too much given to thought. He argued that the delay comes from melancholy, brought on by the shock of his father’s death and still more by his mother’s quick remarriage, which leaves him disgusted with life and unable to act.

T. S. Eliot

“Hamlet and His Problems”, in The Sacred Wood (1920)

Eliot called the play “most certainly an artistic failure”. Hamlet’s disgust, he argued, is “in excess of the facts as they appear”: nothing in the play, Gertrude included, is equal to it. The essay introduced the term “objective correlative”, for a set of objects or events that gives an emotion its exact expression. The essay first appeared in a magazine in 1919.

Ernest Jones

Hamlet and Oedipus (1949)

Developing an essay of 1910 and a note by Freud in The Interpretation of Dreams (1900), Jones argued that Hamlet cannot kill Claudius because Claudius has done what Hamlet unconsciously wished to do: kill his father and take his mother. The reading influenced Laurence Olivier’s performances of the part.

G. Wilson Knight

“The Embassy of Death: An Essay on Hamlet”, in The Wheel of Fire (1930)

Wilson Knight turned the usual sympathies round. He saw Claudius as a capable king and the court as ordinary and healthy, and Hamlet, who has been with the Ghost and thinks of nothing but death, as the sickness at its centre.

Elaine Showalter

“Representing Ophelia: Women, Madness, and the Responsibilities of Feminist Criticism”, in Shakespeare and the Question of Theory (1985)

Showalter traces how Ophelia has been shown in painting, on the stage and in nineteenth-century medical photographs of women in asylums. She argues that each period has made Ophelia’s madness a picture of its own ideas about women.

Stephen Greenblatt

Hamlet in Purgatory (2001)

Greenblatt reads the Ghost, who is confined to fire until his sins are “burnt and purg’d away”, against the abolition of Purgatory in the English Reformation. The Protestant Church had done away with the prayers and masses by which the living helped the dead, and he argues that the play turns that loss into a crisis of remembering.
