Wuthering Heights · Emily Brontë

Characters

Heathcliff

Foundling, then servant, then master of both houses; first appears Chapter 1

He has one name, no surname and no history before Liverpool, and the novel never supplies the three years in which he acquired his money. What it does supply is the method: he takes the two estates by mortgage, marriage and guardianship, with one act of force (Cathy is locked in until she marries Linton), and finds at the end that he has lost the appetite for the result.

Catherine Earnshaw

Of Wuthering Heights, later Mrs Linton; first appears Chapter 3

She scratches three surnames on a window ledge before she has worn any of them, and her great speech is an argument that identity can be shared. She marries for money and standing and says so plainly, then treats her own decision as a wrong done to her.

Edgar Linton

Master of Thrushcross Grange; first appears Chapter 6

Kind, cultivated, physically no match for anybody, and the only adult in the first half of the novel who behaves well to a child. The book is not on his side and does not pretend he deserves what happens to him; his worst act is to raise a daughter inside a park wall and tell her nothing.

Nelly Dean

Servant at both houses; the novel’s chief narrator; first appears Chapter 4

She raises three generations, and almost every significant concealment in the plot is hers: she does not tell Catherine that Heathcliff is listening, does not tell Edgar that his wife is ill, and does not tell anyone where Cathy has gone. She presents herself throughout as the sensible one.

Mr Lockwood

Tenant of Thrushcross Grange; the outer narrator; first appears Chapter 1

A man who congratulates himself on a taste for solitude he does not have, and who is wrong about every person he meets in the first three chapters. He is the reader’s proxy, and Brontë makes him unreliable on purpose: everything in the book has passed through the judgement of a man who thinks a violent household is picturesque.

Hindley Earnshaw

Catherine’s brother, master of the Heights after his father; first appears Chapter 4

He begins the cruelty and is destroyed by it more thoroughly than his victim. The novel is careful to date his collapse to his wife’s death, which makes him the first of the book’s men to be undone by a bereavement, and the template for what happens to Heathcliff twenty years later.

Isabella Linton

Edgar’s sister; Heathcliff’s wife; first appears Chapter 6

She has read enough novels to want a dangerous man and is warned in plain terms by the one person who knows. Her long letter from the Heights is the only sustained account in the book from inside the house that is not Nelly’s, and she is one of the few characters who gets out.

Catherine Linton

Daughter of Catherine and Edgar; first appears Chapter 18

Her mother’s temper with her father’s kindness attached, raised inside a park and let out too late. What distinguishes her is that she can be taught and can teach: the second half of the book turns on her deciding to make a friend of the man she has been mocking.

Hareton Earnshaw

Hindley’s son, raised by Heathcliff; first appears Chapter 2

Deliberately kept illiterate by the man who was kept illiterate before him, and the only person in the book who grieves for Heathcliff. His late attempt at reading, undertaken to impress a girl who has been laughing at him, is the novel’s one unambiguous piece of progress.

Joseph

Servant at Wuthering Heights; first appears Chapter 1

A Calvinist who has outlived four masters and disapproves of all of them, speaking a broad West Riding that Charlotte Brontë later thinned out for southern readers. He supplies the novel’s religion, and the fact that it is entirely joyless is part of the argument.
