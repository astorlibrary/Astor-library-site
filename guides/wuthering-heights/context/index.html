Wuthering Heights · Emily Brontë

Context

Dates

1818
Emily Brontë born at Thornton The fifth of six children; the family moved to Haworth parsonage two years later.
1824
Emily sent to school at Cowan Bridge Emily went with her sisters to the Clergy Daughters’ School, and was withdrawn the following year after Maria and Elizabeth fell fatally ill.
1838
Emily teaches at Law Hill, near Halifax Emily taught at a school here for a few months; the district’s houses and local histories have often been connected with the novel.
1842
Emily and Charlotte study in Brussels Emily and Charlotte studied at the Pensionnat Heger; the French essays Emily wrote there are among the few pieces of her prose outside the novel.
1845
Charlotte finds Emily’s poems The discovery, against Emily’s wishes, led to the sisters’ joint volume and to all three attempting novels.
1846
Poems by Currer, Ellis and Acton Bell published Published at the sisters’ own expense; it sold two copies.
1847
Wuthering Heights published Thomas Cautley Newby issued it in December in three volumes, with Anne Brontë’s Agnes Grey as the third.
1848
Emily Brontë dies at Haworth In December, of consumption, a few months after her brother Branwell and about a year after the novel appeared.
1850
Charlotte Brontë’s revised edition She corrected the text, thinned Joseph’s dialect for southern readers, and added a biographical notice and a preface answering the charge of brutality.
1939
William Wyler’s film With Laurence Olivier and Merle Oberon; it ends at Catherine’s death and omits the second generation entirely, which shaped the popular idea of the book for decades.
2011
Andrea Arnold’s film Shot on the moors with a Black actor as Heathcliff and almost no score, it returns the novel’s questions about race and origin to the foreground.

Places

Haworth
The parsonage where the novel was written, at the top of a steep village street above the moor.
Top Withens
The ruined moorland farmhouse traditionally associated with the situation of Wuthering Heights, though not with the building itself.
Ponden Hall
A seventeenth-century house near Stanbury with a small box bed and window, long linked by readers with Thrushcross Grange and with Chapter 3.
Liverpool
Where Mr Earnshaw finds the child in the street in about 1771, and the only place outside Yorkshire the novel names as an origin.
Law Hill, Halifax
Where Emily Brontë taught briefly in 1838, in a district of old halls and long-running family quarrels.
Gimmerton (fictional)
The novel’s market village and kirk; no real place of the name exists, and the coordinates mark only the moorland district it belongs to.

Critics

Charlotte Brontë

Editor’s Preface to the New Edition of Wuthering Heights (1850)

After Emily’s death Charlotte defended the novel to readers who found it coarse, explaining it by her sister’s isolated life. Of Heathcliff she wrote: “Whether it is right or advisable to create beings like Heathcliff, I do not know: I scarcely think it is.”

Q. D. Leavis

“A Fresh Approach to Wuthering Heights”, in Lectures in America (1969)

Leavis argued that the novel is best read as a realistic account of social and economic change in the north of England, and that the Gothic and romantic elements are not its centre.

Terry Eagleton

Myths of Power: A Marxist Study of the Brontës (1975)

Eagleton reads Heathcliff’s rise in terms of property and class: an outsider who gains the land and houses of the families who rejected him, by using their own laws of marriage and inheritance against them.
