Astor Teaching Room 01 · William Shakespeare

Teach Macbeth.

Begin with a voice trying to make murder possible. Follow the words as they move from prophecy to persuasion, vision, public collapse and a final sense that time itself has emptied out. This room can supply fifteen minutes, one complete lesson or a short sequence across the play.

Choose the scale

One question, not a pile of activities.

Each route produces a piece of thinking that can be kept. The short task isolates a change inside one speech. The full lesson makes students test competing interpretations. The sequence carries the same habits across the whole tragedy.

15-minute close reading

The dagger and the decision.

Use Act 2, Scene 1 after students know that Macbeth and Lady Macbeth have agreed to kill Duncan. The task works on screen, aloud or from a printed copy.

Teacher setup · 2 minutes

Open the speech.

Use the annotated “Is this a dagger” passage. Ask one student to read while the class follows without writing.

Keep in viewMacbeth has already accepted the murder plan. The bell will tell him that Lady Macbeth’s preparations are complete.

Student instructions

  1. Mark the grammar. Underline questions once, statements twice and commands with a box.
  2. Find the turn. Choose the line where Macbeth moves from testing what he sees to treating it as a guide.
  3. Make a claim. Finish this sentence: The dagger does not make Macbeth decide; it...
  4. Complicate it. Add a second sentence beginning Yet the vision also...

At 12 minutes: take two claims. For each, ask the class which single word carries most of the argument. End by rereading “Thou marshall’st me the way that I was going”. The past progressive “was going” matters: the destination existed before the vision appeared.

Put this on screen

Who is in control of the movement?

Read from “Is this a dagger” to “There’s no such thing”. Mark questions, statements and commands. Then decide whether the dagger leads Macbeth, obeys Macbeth or gives a visible shape to a decision already made. Your answer must use one verb and one change in sentence form.

One 50-minute lesson

Does the dagger lead Macbeth, or reveal the way he already chose?

Learning aim: to explain how grammar, imagery and performance create a struggle between supernatural influence and personal responsibility. Students finish with a paragraph that answers the question and tests its own answer.

Before the class

What you need.

Open the annotated passage. Students need the speech from “Is this a dagger” to “There’s no such thing”, with enough space to mark it.

Prior knowledgeMacbeth has heard the prophecy, considered murder, resisted Lady Macbeth and then declared himself “settled”. Banquo has just refused to compromise his allegiance.

Do not settle in advanceThe class does not need to agree that the dagger is supernatural or psychological. The lesson is about what each reading can explain.

  1. 0–5Hear the movementRead once without pens. Students write only the first word that describes the speaker’s state at the beginning and another for the end of the extract.
  2. 5–12Mark the sentence formsUnderline questions, box the command “Come, let me clutch thee” and circle each statement beginning “I”. Ask what changes when a questioner gives an order.
  3. 12–20Read the five phrase notesPairs use the margin notes on the Astor passage. Each chooses one note that strengthens Macbeth’s agency and one that seems to weaken it.
  4. 20–32Stage three daggersRead the speech as fear, invitation and self-deception. No props are needed. The reader changes pace, focus and gesture; observers identify the exact words that made the reading possible.
  5. 32–42Write the first answerStudents state a judgement, quote no more than seven words, analyse one grammatical or figurative choice and link it to Macbeth’s action at the end of the scene.
  6. 42–48Test the answerSwap paragraphs. The reader writes one sentence beginning “This is convincing because...” and one beginning “The phrase that still complicates it is...”
  7. 48–50Exit lineComplete: Shakespeare makes Macbeth responsible not by removing uncertainty, but by...
Listen for

Movement that was already under way.

Students often say the dagger “forces” Macbeth towards Duncan. Return to “the way that I was going” and “such an instrument I was to use”. The vision supplies a form and direction for intentions already present.

Make room for

A vision that still exerts pressure.

Agency does not make the dagger unimportant. Its handle faces Macbeth’s hand, it appears palpable, and blood gathers on it. A strong answer can argue that the mind externalises desire while the staged image feels like an invitation.

Writing prompt · final 18 minutes

How does Shakespeare present Macbeth’s responsibility in the dagger speech?

  1. Give a direct answer that can be disagreed with.
  2. Choose one short phrase and name what its grammar or image does.
  3. Explain how that choice affects the audience’s judgement of Macbeth.
  4. Add the detail that makes your answer less simple.

Three-lesson sequence

From “king hereafter” to “Signifying nothing”.

Each lesson can stand alone, but together they track a precise change: possibility becomes a plan; the body becomes something to command; equivocal promises collapse into a speech that finds no meaning in time or action.

Lesson 1 · Prophecy and choice

Who begins the murder?

Core scenes: 1.3 and 1.7. Begin with the witches’ greeting, then place it beside Macbeth’s private reasons not to kill Duncan and his later agreement.

  • Sort what the witches predict, what they do not instruct and what Macbeth supplies in his own thought.
  • Read the movement from “We will proceed no further in this business” to “I am settled”.
  • Stage Lady Macbeth’s interruptions in 1.7. Decide when attack becomes persuasion and when Macbeth begins to persuade himself.
  • Write a two-part answer: the prophecy makes murder imaginable; responsibility lies in the choices that turn imagination into a plan.

KeepA timeline with three columns: what is spoken to Macbeth, what he imagines, what he chooses.

Lesson 2 · Body and persuasion

What must be changed before Duncan can be killed?

Core scenes: 1.5, 1.7 and 2.2. Use the “unsex me here” close reading as the centre.

  • Group Lady Macbeth’s commands under body, nurture, conscience and darkness.
  • Compare what she asks the spirits to remove with what she later attacks in Macbeth: fear, pity and a gendered idea of courage.
  • After Duncan’s murder, follow hands, blood, sight and sound. Ask which body is now least obedient to its owner.
  • Write one paragraph that treats gender as language used to police action, not as a simple statement that cruelty belongs to men.

KeepAn annotated chain from “milk” to “gall”, then from bloody hands to the imagined stain of Act 5.

Lesson 3 · Equivocation and ending

What happens when words that sounded certain prove double?

Core scenes: 4.1, 5.1 and 5.5. Put the apparitions’ promises beside the moving wood and “Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow”.

  • Rewrite each promise as Macbeth first understands it, then as events fulfil it.
  • Distinguish a lie from a statement designed to produce a false conclusion.
  • Read the final speech aloud at three speeds. Track time, theatre and story as its governing images.
  • Decide whether “Signifying nothing” is tragic knowledge, exhausted self-description or one final attempt to control the meaning of events.

KeepAn independent answer to: How does Shakespeare present the danger of trusting words?

The whole play in six turns

Where the direction changes.

A turning point should alter what becomes possible, not merely mark a famous scene. Ask students to name the decision, discovery or public consequence that makes the next stage of the tragedy different from the last.

1.3 · A future is named

The witches greet Macbeth as king.

They give him a title he has not yet heard he has won and a crown he does not possess. The speed with which the first prediction comes true gives the last one force, but murder is Macbeth’s thought, not their instruction.

1.7 · Refusal becomes agreement

Macbeth declares himself settled.

His objections are moral, political and personal. Lady Macbeth attacks the meaning of his love and courage, then supplies a practical plan. The turn matters because he moves from knowing the reasons against murder to choosing it anyway.

2.1–2.2 · Thought becomes deed

The dagger reaches Duncan’s chamber.

Macbeth recognises the vision as the shape of his “bloody business” and follows the bell. After the murder, the imagined act has sounds, objects and bodily consequences that cannot be dismissed as thought.

3.4 · Private fear becomes public

Banquo’s ghost takes Macbeth’s place.

A king who arranged murder in secret loses command of a royal feast. Whether the ghost is visible beyond Macbeth or not, his response is public. Lady Macbeth can no longer keep the appearance of secure rule intact.

4.1 · Warning is heard as immunity

The apparitions answer without making Macbeth safe.

Macbeth treats “none of woman born” and the condition about Birnam Wood as guarantees. He ignores the armed head’s warning and orders the murder of Macduff’s family. Confidence produces the action that strengthens his enemies.

5.5 · The promises change meaning

Death and the moving wood arrive together.

Lady Macbeth’s death is followed by a speech in which days only creep towards death. The messenger then reports Birnam Wood moving. Macbeth finally names the fiend’s “equivocation”, but recognition comes after the words have governed his choices.

Discussion and writing

Tasks that leave evidence behind.

Each task asks for a decision and the language that supports it. They can be used as paired talk, a short written response or the beginning of a larger essay.

Voice

Who owns the thought?

Compare the witches’ words in 1.3 with Macbeth’s asides. Make two lists: what comes from outside Macbeth and what he contributes. Turn the difference into a sentence about responsibility.

Marriage

Where does partnership end?

Place “my dearest partner of greatness” beside the murder of Banquo, which Macbeth withholds from Lady Macbeth. Choose the scene where their political partnership begins to become separate isolation.

Body

Which body refuses control?

Follow Lady Macbeth’s blood, milk and hand imagery from 1.5 to 5.1. Explain why a character who begins by commanding the body ends by repeating an action she cannot complete.

Performance

Who can see the supernatural?

Direct the dagger or Banquo’s ghost as visible, invisible or uncertain. Explain what the audience is allowed to see and how that changes its judgement of Macbeth’s fear and guilt.

Rule

When does king become tyrant?

Choose one of three moments: Duncan’s murder, Banquo’s murder or the attack on Macduff’s household. Defend the point at which private crime becomes a system of government.

Ending

Does Macbeth understand himself?

Set “Signifying nothing” against one earlier image of greatness or futurity. Decide whether the last speech exposes the emptiness of his ambition or tries to make all life share his defeat.

Essay-evidence ladder

From topic to an argument that can stand up.

Use the ladder for a whole essay or stop after step four for a paragraph. Students should be able to point to the exact place where their answer becomes interpretation rather than plot summary.

  1. Answer the question.Write one sentence that takes a position. Replace broad words such as “powerful” or “evil” with the precise relation you mean.
  2. Locate the moment.Name the speaker, scene and pressure acting on the words. A quotation does not arrive in the play without a situation.
  3. Choose less.Select a phrase short enough to examine. Keep the verb, image, pronoun or sentence shape that actually carries the claim.
  4. Show the work.Explain what the language makes possible on stage or in the listener’s mind. Do not stop at naming a technique.
  5. Set it beside another moment.Use repetition or change. Lady Macbeth’s command over blood in 1.5, for example, becomes a failure to cleanse the hand in 5.1.
  6. Use context precisely.Bring in Folio, chronicle, kingship, equivocation or gender only when it changes the reading of the words you have chosen.
  7. Test the claim.Find the phrase, scene or performance choice that makes the answer less comfortable. Explain why the argument still holds, or revise it.
  8. Return with a difference.Conclude by showing what the full pattern reveals that the opening claim could not yet contain.
Independent essay

How does Shakespeare present Macbeth’s responsibility for his own downfall?

Build your answer through three decisions rather than three themes. You might use his response to prophecy, his agreement in 1.7, the dagger, the choice to kill Banquo, his reading of the apparitions or his response when Birnam Wood appears to move. At least once, place Macbeth’s words beside a different character’s.

Teacher guidance

Keep the difficult questions difficult.

The play invites sharp disagreement, but not every shortcut is equally useful. These notes identify four places where a familiar label can close the reading too early.

Fate and agency

The witches do not give a murder instruction.

  • Separate prediction from command.
  • Notice that Banquo hears prophecy without copying Macbeth’s choices.
  • Let supernatural influence remain possible while asking who turns it into action.
Gender

“Unsex” is not simply “make me a man”.

  • Lady Macbeth asks for culturally gendered associations with pity, nurture and reproductive body to be altered or blocked.
  • She later uses manhood as a weapon in persuasion.
  • Keep those uses of gender distinct from a claim that the play naturally assigns courage or cruelty to one sex.
Equivocation

A true sentence can be designed to deceive.

  • Use the Porter’s equivocator and the apparitions as a language pattern.
  • Avoid the empty statement that Jacobean audiences were simply more superstitious.
  • The useful historical link is to contemporary arguments about equivocation, intensified in the period of the Gunpowder Plot.
Text and stage

There is no early quarto to settle against the Folio.

  • Macbeth first appeared in print in the 1623 First Folio.
  • Simon Forman recorded a performance he saw in 1611.
  • The short Folio text and debated Hecate material make textual and performance choices worth naming rather than hiding.

Content and care. The play includes murder, the killing of children, suicide reported offstage, coercive language and imagery of pregnancy and infant death. Before reading 1.7 or 4.2 aloud, give students the scene’s content and allow an observer or analytical role that does not require performance.
